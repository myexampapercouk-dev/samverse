WordPress migration, in detail

Most migration disasters come from small things: email records forgotten during a DNS change, a redirect sending every page to the homepage, or old hosting cancelled before anyone checked the new site. This is how I move websites between hosts, between domains and from other platforms onto WordPress, and what I check afterwards.

A migration, step by step

  1. Free review and a fixed quote. Send the website address, where it is hosted now, where you want it to go, and who manages your domain and email.
  2. Inventory and backup. A full backup of files and database, a list of every URL, a record of every DNS record, and your current Search Console figures as a baseline.
  3. Prepare the new host. The right PHP version, SSL and a staging area, with the DNS TTL lowered a day ahead so the switch spreads faster.
  4. Copy and test. The site is moved, or rebuilt for a platform migration, then tested on a temporary address while the old site keeps running.
  5. Switch over. A final sync of any new content or orders, then DNS is pointed to the new host, SSL is activated and redirects go live.
  6. Post-move checks. The full post-move checklist, then Search Console monitoring over the following weeks.

Moving from Wix, Blogger, Shopify and others

A platform migration is a rebuild with your content carried across, not a copy. Each platform has its own catch:

  • Wix has no export of its design, so pages are rebuilt in WordPress, images re-uploaded and blog posts often imported from the RSS feed; see moving from Wix to WordPress.
  • Blogger posts and comments import well, but images hosted on Blogger must be pulled into your media library and old post URLs redirected; see moving from Blogger to WordPress.
  • Shopify products, customers and orders can move to WooCommerce, but customer passwords usually cannot, and /products/ and /collections/ URLs need mapping; see moving from Shopify to WooCommerce.
  • Squarespace, Joomla and static HTML follow the same pattern: list every page, move the content, map the URLs.

Because the design is rebuilt anyway, many clients refresh it at the same time; that becomes a website redesign with the migration included.

Zero-downtime steps

Visitors see no downtime because the old site keeps serving until the new one is proven. Three details make that work:

  • Staging and a temporary address. The copied site is tested through a preview URL or a hosts-file change on my computer before your domain points to it.
  • DNS TTL lowered in advance, so when the record changes, most visitors reach the new host within minutes to a few hours rather than a day.
  • Email records recreated exactly. If your email runs on Google Workspace, Zoho or your old host, the MX, SPF, DKIM and DMARC records are copied to the new DNS before nameservers change. This is the most common migration mistake; SPF, DKIM and DMARC explained covers what each record does.

For stores, the switch happens in a quiet period, and orders placed on the old site during the change are synced across.

SEO-safe redirects

On a host-to-host move nothing should change for Google, because every URL stays the same. When URLs or the domain change, I build a redirect map from your sitemap, a crawl and Search Console's top pages, then:

  • Redirect each old URL to its closest new equivalent, never everything to the homepage.
  • Use permanent 301 redirects, not temporary 302s; see 301 vs 302 redirects.
  • Send http, https, www and non-www versions to the final URL in one hop, with no chains.
  • Carry over titles, descriptions, headings and structured data on pages that rank.

For a new domain, the old domain stays registered and redirecting, and Google's Change of Address tool is used; changing your domain without losing SEO goes through it.

The post-move checklist

  • SSL active on every version of the domain, with no mixed content warnings; see fixing mixed content after moving to HTTPS.
  • Every form sends, and notification emails arrive, using SMTP where the new host needs it.
  • Checkout, payment gateway webhooks and order emails tested with a real order flow.
  • Caching suited to the new host, with old host-specific caching plugins removed.
  • No noindex or password left from staging, a fresh sitemap submitted, and no new 404s in Search Console.
  • Backups and uptime monitoring running on the new host, and a speed check against the old one.
  • Old hosting kept for a week or two as a fallback, then cancelled.

How long it takes

A typical WordPress host-to-host move takes 1 to 3 working days including testing, plus a few hours for DNS to spread. Large media libraries, big stores and slow access to the old host take longer. Platform migrations follow rebuild timelines: usually 1 to 3 weeks for a business site, and longer for stores with many products. A domain change takes a few days of work, then several weeks of watching Search Console as Google processes it.

FAQ

Common questions

Will my website go down during the move?

No. I copy and test the site on the new host first, then switch DNS, so visitors see no downtime.

Will I lose my Google rankings?

Not with proper redirects and settings. I preserve URLs where possible and redirect anything that changes.

Can you recommend good hosting?

Yes. I can suggest reliable hosting based on your traffic and budget, and set it up for you.

Can you move my email too?

I make sure your email DNS records keep working, and can help coordinate moving mailboxes if needed.

Should I cancel my old hosting before the move?

No. Keep it running until the new site has been live and checked for a week or two, so there is a fallback if anything was missed. Do check its renewal date, so you are not charged for another year while you wait.

Can you move a WooCommerce store without losing orders?

Yes. The switch is planned for a quiet period, any orders placed on the old site during the change are synced across, and payments, webhooks and order emails are tested on the new host before it takes real traffic.

Will my Wix or Shopify design come across exactly?

Not automatically. Wix and Shopify designs cannot run on WordPress, so the design is rebuilt. It can be matched closely, but most clients use the rebuild to improve layouts that were not working.

Can I change host and domain at the same time?

It is possible, but changing one thing at a time is safer. If rankings dip after two changes at once, it is much harder to tell which one caused it. Where both are needed, I usually move the host first and change the domain once everything is stable.
