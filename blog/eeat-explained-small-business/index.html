E-E-A-T Explained in Plain English for Small Business Websites

What E-E-A-T (experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust) really means, what it isn't, and practical ways a small business website can show it.

Key takeaways
  • E-E-A-T is Google's shorthand for experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust; it is not a single score or something a plugin can add.
  • Real photos, named authors with genuine qualifications, case studies and honest reviews show E-E-A-T far better than claims or badges.
  • Health, finance and legal businesses are held to a higher standard, so qualified review and accurate, up-to-date content matter most there.

If you've read anything about SEO recently, you've probably seen "E-E-A-T" mentioned as if it were a secret ranking formula. It isn't a formula, and it isn't a secret. It's Google's shorthand for the qualities that make content worth trusting. Here's what it means in plain English, and the practical things a small business website can do to show it.

What E-E-A-T stands for

LetterMeaningSmall business example
ExperienceFirst-hand experience of the topicA pest control company showing photos and notes from jobs it actually did
ExpertiseKnowledge or skill in the subjectA CA explaining a tax change, with their qualification shown
AuthoritativenessBeing a recognised go-to sourceA manufacturer listed by its industry association and mentioned in trade publications
TrustBeing accurate, honest, safe and reliableClear contact details, genuine reviews and honest claims

Google added the first "E", for Experience, in late 2022. It describes trust as the most important of the four; the other three mostly exist to support it.

What E-E-A-T is (and isn't)

E-E-A-T comes from Google's Search Quality Rater Guidelines, a document used by people Google hires to assess the quality of search results. Their ratings help Google judge whether its systems are working well. They don't directly move your pages up or down.

  • It is not a single score Google calculates for your site
  • There is no plugin or setting that "adds" E-E-A-T
  • It is a useful description of what Google's ranking systems try to reward: helpful, reliable content made for people

So treat it as a checklist for being the kind of business, and website, that people and search engines can trust. It lines up closely with what Google rewards after core updates.

Why it matters more for some businesses

Google holds some topics to a higher standard. Its guidelines call them "Your Money or Your Life" (YMYL) topics: subjects that could affect someone's health, finances, safety or legal situation. If your website is for a clinic, hospital, pharmacy, financial advisor, insurance agent, lawyer, CA or immigration consultant, E-E-A-T carries extra weight. A plumber's post about fixing a dripping tap needs less proof than a clinic's page about treatment options.

That doesn't mean other businesses can ignore it. A customer choosing a caterer or a packers and movers company is still asking, "Can I trust these people?"

Show experience: prove you've done the work

Experience is often the easiest quality for a genuine small business to show, because you have it and large content websites often don't.

  • Use real photos of your projects, premises, products and team instead of stock images
  • Write case studies with the actual problem, what you did and what happened; see how to write case studies
  • Add details only a practitioner would know: what usually goes wrong, what customers in your city ask, what you'd do differently
  • In blog posts, say what you've seen first-hand, for example "In our clinic, patients most often ask..."

Show expertise: put names and qualifications on your content

  • Add an author name to blog posts and guides, with a short bio and relevant qualifications
  • Create a simple author or team page for each person who writes or reviews content
  • For medical, legal or financial content, have a qualified person write or review it, and say so on the page
  • Show registrations, certifications and memberships that are current and verifiable, following any advertising rules your professional body sets
  • Go deeper on service pages: explain your process, options, timelines and what affects cost, instead of three lines of generic text

If you use AI tools to draft content, someone with real expertise still needs to check it and add to it; see using AI tools for content responsibly.

Show authority: be known beyond your own website

Authority comes mostly from what others say about you, so it builds slowly. Useful signals for a small business include:

  • A complete Google Business Profile with genuine reviews and thoughtful replies
  • Listings with industry associations, chambers of commerce and trade bodies you belong to
  • Mentions and links from suppliers, partners, local news sites or industry publications
  • Talks, interviews, podcasts or guest articles where you share real knowledge
  • The same business name, address and phone number everywhere you're listed

Stay focused on your field. Being a respected source on a few topics beats writing a little about everything.

Show trust: the part that matters most

Trust ties everything together. On a small business website, it usually comes down to these basics:

  • Who you are: an About page with real names and faces; see how to write an About page that builds trust
  • How to reach you: full address, phone, WhatsApp and a business email on your own domain
  • Honest claims: no "No. 1 in India" or "guaranteed results" unless you can genuinely back it up
  • Accurate, current content: keep services, prices and team details up to date, and show a "last updated" date on guides
  • Clear policies: a privacy policy, plus refund, cancellation and shipping terms if you take payments
  • A secure, working site: HTTPS, no broken forms, no spammy pop-ups and no signs of hacking

Structured data such as Organization or Person schema can help Google connect your website to your business and your people, but it only describes trust you already have. It can't create it.

Mistakes that backfire

  • Fake reviews or testimonials: they break Google's policies and destroy trust when customers spot them
  • Invented authors with stock photos and made-up credentials
  • Stock photos presented as your team or your work
  • Exaggerated numbers like "thousands of happy clients" that you can't support
  • Mass-produced content published without anyone knowledgeable checking it
  • Hiding the business behind the website: no address, no names, only a form

None of this is quick, and that's the point. E-E-A-T rewards real businesses for making their reality visible. Start with your About page, your most important service pages and your Google Business Profile, then improve one page at a time.
