How to Move From WooCommerce to Shopify, Step by Step: Products, Customers, Orders, Redirects and Cutover

Move a WooCommerce store to Shopify step by step: export and import products and customers, bring orders across, map URLs to 301 redirects and plan the cutover.

Key takeaways
  • Products, variants, categories and customers move through Shopify CSV files or a migration app, while past orders usually need an app.
  • Shopify uses fixed paths such as /products/ and /collections/, so every old WooCommerce URL needs a 301 in Shopify URL redirects.
  • Passwords cannot move, so plan how customers sign in, and run a cutover checklist covering payments, GST invoices, redirects and DNS.

Stores move from WooCommerce to Shopify for sensible reasons. Nobody on the team wants to manage hosting, plugin updates and security any more, or the store has outgrown a setup patched together over several years. Shopify takes hosting and upkeep off your hands in return for monthly plan and app costs. If that trade suits you, the move itself is manageable, as long as you protect three things: your data, your URLs and your customers' ability to sign in.

This guide is the reverse of my Shopify to WooCommerce migration guide, and follows the order I work in when moving a store as part of my Shopify development service. Customer passwords need a decision up front. At the time of writing, Shopify's newer customer accounts sign people in with a one-time code sent to their email, with no password at all, which removes the problem for most stores. With classic accounts, customers accept an account invitation or reset their password. Check Shopify's current help pages for which account types your store can use, and plan a short email that explains the change before launch.

Also list every plugin on the WooCommerce site and what it does: GST invoices, COD rules, shipping, reviews, wishlists, subscriptions, wholesale pricing. Each needs a Shopify equivalent, a theme feature or a decision to drop it. This list is where a migration shows its real size, because some WooCommerce customisations have no simple app equivalent.

Step 1: Export and clean your WooCommerce data

  1. Take a full backup of the WordPress files and database, and keep it somewhere safe.
  2. Export products with WooCommerce's built-in product CSV exporter, including variations and custom meta. Export customers and orders through WooCommerce's export tools or an export plugin.
  3. Export a list of every URL: products, categories, tags, pages and posts. The XML sitemap is a quick source and a crawler gives a fuller one. Add the pages with the most clicks and backlinks from Search Console, so you know which redirects matter most.
  4. Clean before importing: drop discontinued and draft products you don't want to carry, fill in missing SKUs and weights, standardise variation names so S, Small and small become one option, and remove test customers.
  5. Keep the old site online during the import. Migration apps usually fetch images from the old site's URLs.

The move is also a natural moment to improve product content. If descriptions are still supplier copy, rewrite your best sellers before or during the import; writing product descriptions that sell has a framework you can follow.

Step 2: Import products, collections and customers

There are two routes, and the right one depends on the size and shape of the catalogue:

  • Shopify's product CSV. You map WooCommerce columns onto Shopify's template. It is free and gives full control, but variable products need care: Shopify expects one row per variant with the product handle repeated, and option names in fixed columns. It suits catalogues of straightforward products.
  • A migration app. Shopify's own store importer or a third-party service connects to WooCommerce and moves products, customers and, depending on the tool, orders, reviews and blog posts. It is quicker for large catalogues and the practical way to bring orders across. Check what each tool supports at the time you use it, and do a trial run first.

Whichever route you take, import a small batch first and check it in the admin and on the storefront: variants, prices, compare-at prices, image order, stock, weights and tax settings. Then import the rest.

Categories become collections. Shopify doesn't nest collections the way WooCommerce nests categories; a hierarchy is built in the navigation menu instead. Manual collections behave like WooCommerce categories, while automated collections fill themselves from rules such as tag, type or vendor, which is often tidier for a large catalogue. Extra product attributes such as fabric or material become metafields so the theme can display them consistently.

For customers, check how email marketing consent maps across. Anyone who didn't opt in on WooCommerce should not arrive in Shopify marked as subscribed.

Step 3: Map URLs and add 301 redirects

This is where rankings are protected or lost. At the time of writing, Shopify uses fixed URL paths that can't be changed: products under /products/, collections under /collections/, pages under /pages/ and blog posts under /blogs/ followed by the blog's name. WooCommerce URLs usually look different:

WooCommerce URLShopify URL
/product/blue-cotton-kurta//products/blue-cotton-kurta
/product-category/kurtas//collections/kurtas
/about-us//pages/about-us
/how-to-choose-a-kurta//blogs/news/how-to-choose-a-kurta
/my-account//account

Keep each slug, which Shopify calls the handle, the same wherever you can, so the mapping is only a change of path. Then add a 301 redirect for every old URL in Shopify's URL redirects, which sit with the navigation settings in the admin at the time of writing. Redirects can be imported in bulk from a CSV with two columns, the old path and the new one. Point each old URL to its closest new page; sending everything to the home page throws away relevance. 301 vs 302 redirects covers why the redirect type matters too.

One quirk worth knowing: at the time of writing, a Shopify URL redirect only works when no live page exists at the old address. That rarely matters in a migration, because WooCommerce paths don't exist on Shopify, but it explains why a redirect can occasionally seem to do nothing.

Step 4: Blog posts, pages and SEO checks

Blog posts move into a Shopify blog, through a migration app or by hand if there are only a few. Check each one: images uploaded to Shopify rather than still pointing at the old WordPress media library, internal links updated to the new URLs, headings intact, and leftover shortcodes from WordPress plugins removed, since they will show up as plain text. Pages built with a WordPress page builder usually need rebuilding with theme sections rather than copying.

Then run the SEO checks:

  • Titles and meta descriptions. Some migration tools carry these across from WordPress SEO plugins and some don't. Check a sample, and fill in the search engine listing by hand on your key pages.
  • Canonical URLs. A product can open at a collection-based address such as /collections/kurtas/products/blue-cotton-kurta and also at /products/blue-cotton-kurta, with a canonical tag naming the shorter one. Make sure menus and internal links use the short address; canonical tags explained covers why.
  • Structured data. Test product pages for price, availability and reviews with Google's Rich Results Test. Schema markup explained shows what to look for.
  • Image alt text. It often doesn't survive the move, so add it back on key products.
  • Sitemap. Shopify generates one at /sitemap.xml; submit it in Search Console once the store is live.

Step 5: The cutover checklist

Plan the switch for a quiet sales period, and never for the week before a sale.

  1. Payments connected and tested. For Indian stores, Shopify Payments isn't available in India at the time of writing, so a provider such as Razorpay, PayU, Cashfree or PhonePe is used, and Shopify may add its own transaction fee depending on your plan. COD is set up as a manual payment method.
  2. A GST invoice app configured and checked by your CA, since Shopify doesn't create GST-compliant invoices by itself at the time of writing.
  3. Shipping rates, the courier app and pin code checks tested.
  4. Test orders placed: prepaid, COD, a failed payment and a refund.
  5. All redirects imported, and a sample of old URLs, including your top pages, opened to confirm they land correctly.
  6. WooCommerce put into maintenance or catalogue mode, so no orders are placed during the switch.
  7. A final import of orders and customers created on WooCommerce since the main import.
  8. DNS changed to point the domain at Shopify, with email records left untouched.
  9. Customers emailed about the new store and how to sign in.
  10. The old WordPress site and its backup kept available, away from the public domain, for a few months.

In the weeks after, watch Search Console for 404 errors and add redirects for anything missed, and compare organic traffic to key pages with the period before. Some movement while Google recrawls is common; a sustained drop on particular pages usually points to a missing redirect or content that didn't come across. FAQs

Will I lose Google rankings when moving to Shopify?

Not if every old URL redirects to its closest new page and the content is at least as good. Ranking losses after a migration usually trace back to missing redirects, thinner product pages or titles and descriptions that didn't come across.

Can I move my orders from WooCommerce to Shopify?

Usually, with a migration app. At the time of writing, Shopify's own CSV import handles products and customers but not orders, so check what your chosen tool supports before you rely on it.

Will customers have to create new accounts?

Their details and order history can move, but passwords can't. With Shopify's newer customer accounts they sign in with a one-time email code; with classic accounts they set a new password.

How long does a WooCommerce to Shopify migration take?

It depends on the size of the catalogue, how clean the data is, how many plugin features need replacing and how much content there is. The import itself is quick; testing, redirects and replacing features take most of the time.

Should I redesign the store at the same time?

It is tempting, but doing both at once makes problems harder to trace. Where possible, move first with a theme close to your current look, then redesign once traffic has settled.
