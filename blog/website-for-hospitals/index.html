Websites for Hospitals and Multi-Speciality Centres: Emergency Contact First, Departments, a Doctor Directory With OPD Schedules, Appointments, Insurance and Cashless Information

Plan a hospital website around urgency: emergency contact first, departments, a doctor directory with OPD timings, booking and cashless insurance lists.

Key takeaways
  • Design for the most urgent visitor first: the emergency number, ambulance contact and directions should be reachable in one tap from every page.
  • A doctor directory with qualifications, departments and OPD timings, kept accurate by a named person, is usually the most used part of a hospital site.
  • Insurance, TPA and cashless lists, accreditation and package prices change often, so show them only as accurately as you can maintain them, with a date.

A hospital website has to serve people in very different states at once: someone in an emergency, a patient choosing a surgeon, a family checking visiting hours, a referring doctor and a nurse looking for work. The way to handle that is to rank the tasks by urgency, make the most urgent ones reachable from every page, and give each of the others a clear route that does not get in the way.

Clinic and hospital sites share most of their building blocks, which I describe on the website design for doctors and clinics page: doctor profiles, treatment pages, appointment enquiries and local SEO. Many hospitals already have a site that has grown untidy over the years, so this is often a website redesign rather than a fresh build, with redirects to protect the pages that already rank.

Everything on the site should be placed by this order. A homepage slider of awards pushing the emergency number below the fold gets it backwards.

Seconds: the emergency route

On mobile, a fixed bar with two tap-to-call buttons, emergency and ambulance, should stay on screen as the visitor scrolls. Beside it, a "Directions to Emergency" link opens maps to the casualty entrance specifically, not the main gate, if the two differ. On the emergency page itself, keep it brief: what the emergency department handles, that it is open round the clock if it is, and what to bring if there is time. Test those numbers regularly; a mistyped digit here is the most serious error a hospital site can have.

If you run your own ambulances, give them a separate page with coverage areas and types of vehicle. Websites for ambulance services covers that side in depth.

Minutes: the doctor directory and booking

The directory is usually the most visited section. Each doctor's profile should give:

  • Name, photo, department and designation
  • Degrees with university, and registration details as current medical council guidance suggests
  • Areas of clinical interest and procedures performed, in plain words
  • Languages spoken
  • OPD days and times at each location, including whether they see patients by appointment only
  • A booking button that pre-selects that doctor

Visitors should be able to filter the directory by department, location, day and language, and search by doctor name. A profile written for patients reads differently from a CV; the Dr. Nitish Gupta site, for a pulmonologist in Agra, shows one way to present qualifications, memberships and specialised procedures clearly.

Keeping schedules accurate

Schedules change when doctors go on leave, join or leave. Assign one person, usually in the front office or marketing team, to update the directory, and give them a simple editing screen rather than access to page layouts. If you run a hospital information system that already holds OPD schedules, ask whether it can feed the website or provide an embeddable booking widget, so the schedule is entered once.

Appointment options

Three common setups: an appointment request form that your call centre confirms, a booking widget from the hospital's existing system, or a separate booking tool with live slots. The request form is simplest and avoids double booking, but tell patients when to expect the confirmation call. Online appointment booking for websites compares the tools. Keep forms to name, phone, preferred doctor and date; avoid collecting symptoms or reports through open forms.

Days: departments and planned treatment

Give each department a page that a patient could read before a consultation: the conditions treated, common procedures explained simply, the facilities and equipment, the doctors in that department linked to their profiles, and a direct booking route. The medical content should come from your clinicians and be reviewed by them; I can structure and edit it, but the facts are theirs.

Avoid outcome claims, success rates and "best hospital" language unless you are sure they are permitted and supportable. Professional and advertising rules in healthcare are strict, and the guidance changes. Check the current position with your medical and legal advisers.

Insurance, TPA and cashless information

Patients planning admission often ask whether their insurer or TPA is accepted for cashless treatment. A page listing the insurers, TPAs and government schemes you work with is useful, but these arrangements change. Date the list, say that patients should confirm with the insurance desk before admission, and give the desk's direct number and hours. Outline the general cashless and reimbursement process and the documents patients usually need.

Package prices

If you publish prices for procedures or health check-ups, state what each includes, such as room category and days of stay, and what can change the final bill. Many hospitals prefer to offer an estimate on request; either way, make it easy to ask.

Any time: patients, families and visitors

A patient and visitor guide answers the questions that otherwise reach the reception desk:

  • Admission and discharge process, and what to bring
  • Visiting hours by ward, and rules for intensive care areas
  • Parking, drop-off points, wheelchairs and accessibility
  • Pharmacy, canteen, ATM and accommodation nearby for families
  • How to get medical records or a copy of a discharge summary
  • Patient rights and how to raise a concern or complaint

Accreditation, stated as it is

NABH accreditation, laboratory accreditation for your lab, and similar marks matter to patients and insurers. Show only what you currently hold, with the scope it covers and the accrediting body's name, and use logos as that body's rules allow. If a certification is in progress, do not imply it is complete. Registration under clinical establishment rules, where they apply in your state, is something to confirm with your adviser. If your hospital lab also serves walk-in patients, websites for diagnostic labs covers the test catalogue side.

More than one location

Hospital groups and networks with satellite clinics need a page per location: address, map, departments available there, emergency availability, OPD schedules and phone numbers, matched to each location's Google Business Profile. Doctors who consult at several locations should show the timings for each. Keep the location name, address and phone identical everywhere they appear, because mismatched details confuse both patients and map listings.

Speed and accessibility are not optional

Many visitors are older, anxious or on a weak mobile connection inside the hospital itself. Keep pages light, avoid heavy sliders and autoplay video, use large readable text and strong contrast, and make sure the site works with a screen reader and keyboard. Websites for older users lists practical checks. Regional language versions of key pages, such as emergency, visiting hours and appointments, help patients who are not comfortable reading English.

Redesigning an existing hospital site

An older hospital site often has hundreds of pages, some of them ranking for doctor names and procedures. Before changing anything:

  1. Export the list of URLs and the pages that get search traffic.
  2. Decide which pages stay, merge or go, and map every changed URL to its new address.
  3. Build the new site on staging and check the doctor directory and forms end to end.
  4. Launch with 301 redirects in place and watch Search Console closely for errors.

FAQs

What should be at the top of a hospital website?

The emergency and ambulance numbers as tap-to-call buttons, directions to the emergency entrance, and a quick route to find a doctor and book.

Can our hospital information system feed the doctor schedule?

Sometimes. Ask your vendor whether it offers an embeddable booking widget or a data feed. If not, assign one person to keep the website schedule current.

Should we list the insurers and TPAs we accept?

Yes, if you can keep the list accurate. Date it, and ask patients to confirm cashless eligibility with your insurance desk before admission.

Can we show NABH accreditation on the website?

If you hold it, state it accurately with the scope and the accrediting body, and follow that body's current rules on using its mark.

Will redesigning our site affect search rankings?

It does not have to. Keeping important URLs, redirecting the ones that change and checking Search Console after launch protects most of what already ranks.
