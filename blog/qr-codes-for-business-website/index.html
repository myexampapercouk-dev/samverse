QR Codes for Your Business: Bringing Offline Customers to Your Website

How to use a QR code for your business website in India: where to place it, which page to link, tracking with UTM tags, design tips and mistakes to avoid.

Key takeaways
  • A QR code should open a specific, mobile-friendly page that matches where it was scanned, not just your homepage.
  • Use a URL you control, add UTM tags so each placement shows up separately in analytics, and test every printed code before a print run.
  • Keep payment QR codes (UPI) and website QR codes separate and clearly labelled, so customers know what will happen when they scan.

A QR code for your business website turns a printed surface, such as a visiting card, shop counter, menu, brochure, product box or hoarding, into a direct path to a specific web page. In India, where almost every customer already scans UPI codes daily, people are comfortable scanning. The value comes from sending them to the right page, making that page work well on a phone, and tracking which placement brings visits.

This guide covers where to use website QR codes, which page each one should open, how to track them, design rules and the mistakes that waste a print run.

How to use a QR code for your business website

  1. Decide the goal for each placement: read reviews, see the menu, book an appointment, register a warranty, chat on WhatsApp, view a product guide.
  2. Choose or create the landing page that achieves that goal on a phone.
  3. Build the URL on your own domain and add UTM tags for the placement.
  4. Generate the code as a high-resolution or vector file.
  5. Add a short call to action next to it: "Scan to see today's menu" or "Scan for installation video".
  6. Test it on several phones, in the actual size and lighting, before printing in bulk.

Send people to a specific page, not your homepage

The most common mistake is pointing every QR code at the homepage. Someone scanning a product box wants the manual for that product, not your company story. A dedicated page:

  • matches what the person expected when they scanned;
  • loads fast on mobile data, with the main action visible on the first screen;
  • has one clear next step: book, call, WhatsApp, buy, download;
  • lets you measure that placement separately.

The page should not need pinch-zooming or a desktop layout. Check it on a mid-range Android phone over a mobile connection, since that is how most scans happen. Website speed on Indian mobile networks covers what slows pages down.

One page per placement type is usually enough. A restaurant might need only a menu page and a review page, while a manufacturer might want one page per product line.

Static vs dynamic QR codes

A static QR code contains the final URL directly. It never expires and needs no service. A dynamic QR code contains a short link from a QR service that redirects to your page, so you can change the destination later and see scan counts.

TypeProsCons
StaticFree, permanent, no third partyDestination fixed once printed
Dynamic (third-party)Editable destination, scan statsMay stop working if the subscription ends or the service closes
Static code to your own redirect URLPermanent and editable, no subscriptionNeeds a redirect set up on your site

The third option is often the best for small businesses. Print a static code pointing to something like yourdomain.in/scan/menu, then set that address to redirect wherever you want. On WordPress, a redirection plugin can manage this. If you ever change the destination, you update the redirect, not the print. You keep full control, and the code keeps working as long as your domain does.

Tracking QR code scans with UTM tags

Without tracking, QR traffic appears in Google Analytics as "direct", mixed up with people typing your address. Add UTM parameters to the destination URL, for example a source of "visiting-card", a medium of "qr" and a campaign name for the print run. Each placement then shows separately in your reports. UTM tags explained covers naming rules, and GA4 events explained shows how to count bookings or enquiries that follow a scan.

If you use your own redirect URL, put the UTM tags on the redirect target rather than the printed address, so the printed code stays short and clean.

Design and printing tips

  • Size: on a visiting card, make it large enough to scan easily from a short distance; on posters and standees, much larger. Test at the printed size.
  • Contrast: dark code on a light background. Inverted or low-contrast colours fail on some phones.
  • Quiet zone: leave a clear margin around the code.
  • Short URLs: fewer characters mean a simpler, more reliable pattern.
  • Logos in the centre: possible with higher error correction, but test thoroughly.
  • Surface: glossy lamination can cause glare under shop lights. Matte usually scans better.
  • Placement: avoid curved surfaces and spots where people cannot hold the phone steady.

Website QR codes vs UPI payment QR codes

Many Indian shops have a UPI payment QR at the counter. If you add a second code for reviews or the menu, label both clearly so customers do not scan the wrong one. Keep them visually distinct, perhaps with different frames and text such as "Pay here" and "Menu and offers". Never place a website QR code where it could be mistaken for the payment code. Also check your printed codes from time to time: tampered or stuck-over codes are a known scam pattern, and customers trust codes that look official.

QR codes on product packaging for online stores

For brands selling on WooCommerce or Shopify, a code on the box can drive repeat sales and reduce support calls. Useful destinations include a setup video, care instructions, a warranty registration form, a reorder page with the same product pre-selected, or WhatsApp support. Keep these pages free of distracting pop-ups. If the store also sells through marketplaces, a QR code to your own site gives customers a direct route back for the next order. Keeping printed QR codes working for years

Printed material outlives websites. A visiting card or product box may still be in circulation years after a redesign. A few habits protect your codes:

  • Keep a simple list of every printed code, its URL, where it was used and when.
  • When you redesign or migrate the site, keep every QR destination URL alive or redirect it to the closest current page.
  • Renew your domain well ahead of expiry; if it lapses, every code you ever printed breaks.
  • Scan a sample from each print run every few months.

Redirect planning is a normal part of any site migration. Keeping reliable backups also means a broken page can be restored quickly.

If a code does break, a friendly custom 404 page with links to your menu, contact page and WhatsApp still rescues the visit.

Security and privacy

Only link to pages on your own domain or trusted services. Avoid URL shorteners you do not control, since they hide the destination and can be blocked or expire. If the page collects personal details, keep the form short, say why you need the information and follow your privacy policy. Do not put personal data in the QR URL itself.

FAQs

How do I make a QR code for my business website?

Decide the page, add UTM tags to its URL, generate a high-resolution code with any reputable generator, add a call to action and test it on several phones before printing.

Do QR codes expire?

Static codes do not. Dynamic codes from third-party services may stop working if the subscription ends. A static code pointing to a redirect on your own domain avoids this.

Can I change where a printed QR code goes?

Only if it points to a redirect you control or a dynamic QR service. A static code to a final URL cannot be changed after printing.

How do I track how many people scan my QR code?

Add UTM parameters to the destination URL and check them in Google Analytics, or use the scan counts in a dynamic QR service.

Can a QR code help my SEO?

Not directly. It brings visits and can help gather reviews, which support local visibility, but the code itself is not a ranking factor.
