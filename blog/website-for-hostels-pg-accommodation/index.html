Websites for Hostels and PG Accommodation: Rooms and Sharing, Rent and Deposit, Food, House Rules, Safety and Visit Booking

Plan a hostel or PG website: room and sharing options, rent and deposit, food plans, house rules, safety, commute times, reviews and booking a visit.

Key takeaways
  • Describe each room type the way residents compare them: sharing, bathroom, cooling, furniture, what the rent includes and which beds are free.
  • Set out rent, deposit, notice period and extra charges, or say clearly what is on request, and write food plans and house rules out in full.
  • List only the safety measures you really have, give honest commute times, and make booking a visit or a video tour the next step on every page.

Before anyone visits a PG, they have usually ruled out several others from their phone, on rent, food, rules, photos and the distance to college or work. A hostel or PG website wins the visit by answering those points room by room, honestly, and then making it easy to book a visit or a video tour. Parents often read the same pages, so the tone has to work for them too.

Long-stay accommodation borrows a lot from hotel websites: room pages, real photos, an enquiry or booking flow and a Google Business Profile linked to the site, which is what my hotel and homestay website design work covers. The differences are monthly rent instead of nightly rates, house rules instead of check-out times, and a visit before the booking. For a single building with simple needs, a compact WordPress website with these pages, WhatsApp and a visit form is enough.

Describe beds, not just the building

Residents compare options one room type at a time. "Double sharing available" tells them little, and every missing detail becomes a WhatsApp message you have to answer. Give every room type the same set of details:

DetailWhat to writeWhy people check it
SharingSingle, twin, triple or four-sharingIt decides both the rent and the privacy
BathroomAttached, or shared and by how manyIt shapes every morning before college or work
CoolingAC, cooler or fan, and whether AC electricity is billed separatelyIt changes the monthly bill in summer
FurnitureBed and mattress, lockable cupboard, study table and chair, shelvesMost residents arrive with a couple of bags and nothing else
Included in rentMeals, Wi-Fi, housekeeping, laundry, electricity up to a limitIt lets people compare the real monthly cost
AvailabilityBeds free now, and from which dateIt saves calls about rooms that are already full

Set this up as fields your manager can edit from a phone, so a bed can be marked as taken the day it fills. A page that still advertises a vacancy filled two months ago does more damage than a page that shows none.

Rent, deposit and the full monthly cost

Many owners worry that publishing rent invites bargaining. But people usually decide on budget first, and a site that gives no figure at all can lose them before they ask. If rent varies by floor or room, show a starting rent for each room type. If you would rather not publish it, write "rent on request", say how to ask, and reply quickly. Whatever you publish, set out the full cost line by line:

  • Monthly rent, and exactly what it includes
  • Security deposit: whether it is fully refundable, when it is returned and what can be deducted
  • One-time charges, such as maintenance or agreement charges, if you have any
  • Electricity: included, shared by meter reading, or charged for AC use
  • Lock-in and notice period: any minimum stay, and the notice a resident gives before leaving
  • Rent due date and how residents pay, such as UPI or a payment link

Rent, deposits and licence or rental agreements fall under state laws and local rules that differ from place to place, so have your agreement checked by a lawyer and make the website match it word for word.

Food plans people can picture

"Homely food" appears on almost every PG listing and means nothing by itself. Food is one of the first things residents and parents ask about, so be specific:

  • Which meals are included each day, and what happens on Sundays and holidays
  • Veg only, or veg and non-veg, and whether they are cooked separately; Jain or other options only if you really provide them
  • Meal timings, and whether breakfast is early enough for morning lectures or office cabs
  • A packed lunch for people who are out all day
  • Whether residents can opt out of meals, and whether the rent changes if they do
  • Drinking water, a fridge, and whether residents may use the kitchen

Publish the week's menu as a simple table on the site, not a photo of the mess board, and update it when it changes. A photo of the kitchen and dining area on an ordinary day says more than any adjective. If you also sell meals to outsiders, that is a separate business with its own needs; see websites for tiffin and meal subscription services.

House rules, written before anyone asks

Rules decide whether a PG suits someone as much as rent does. A professional on night shifts and a first-year student's parents read the same rules in opposite ways, so write them plainly and let people choose. Cover:

  • Gate timings, and what happens when a resident is late
  • Visitors: who may visit, where, until when, and whether overnight guests are allowed
  • Smoking, alcohol, cooking in rooms and electrical appliances
  • Quiet hours, especially around exams
  • Leave and absence, and whether parents are informed for students
  • Cleaning, laundry days and what happens to belongings left behind

Write each rule the way you would explain it at the gate. For example:

The main gate closes at [time]. If you will be later, message the warden on WhatsApp before then. Parents and siblings may visit in the common room during the day; overnight guests are not permitted.

A published rules page also helps later, because both sides can point to what was on the site before the resident moved in.

Safety: list what exists, and nothing more

Safety is often the first thing parents ask about, and a line such as "fully secure premises" does not answer it. List the measures you actually have:

  • CCTV at entrances and in common areas, never in rooms or bathrooms, and who can view the footage
  • A warden or resident manager: whether someone is on site at night, and an emergency contact number
  • Entry control, such as a register, access cards or biometric entry, and how visitors are logged
  • Fire safety: extinguishers, marked exits and what residents are told to do in an emergency
  • Staff checks: whether cooks, cleaners and guards are verified before they start
  • Medical help: a first aid kit, and the nearest clinic and hospital

If the building is women-only, or has floors set aside for women with separate access, say so and explain how access is controlled. If it is not, do not suggest it with a photo or a vague phrase. Some cities ask PG and hostel owners to register their premises or share resident details with the local police; check what applies to you before mentioning it on the site.

Students and working professionals: two different searches

A PG near a college and one near an IT park can be the same kind of building with very different residents. If you take both, give each group its own page; if you take only one, say so on the homepage.

For students and their parents

Cover the route to named colleges and coaching centres, study tables and quiet hours, mess timings that fit lectures, rules on late entry and leave, and how parents can reach the warden. Many students start looking around admission time, so keep vacancies current from then on. A short "for parents" section with the warden's introduction, the safety list and the visiting rules answers their questions in one place.

For working professionals

Cover commute times to the office areas you serve, flexible meal plans, Wi-Fi that copes with video calls, late entry for shift workers if you allow it, housekeeping and laundry, and the notice period. If an outside laundry collects from the building, say on which days; websites for laundry and dry cleaning services shows what such services offer PG owners.

Location and commute, measured honestly

"Walking distance from the metro" is a line people have learned to distrust. Measure the journeys residents will actually make, at the times they will make them:

  • Walking time to the nearest metro, bus stop or station, and whether the route is lit at night
  • Travel time at peak hours to the colleges, coaching centres or office areas you name
  • Nearby essentials: grocery, pharmacy, ATM, gym and places to eat
  • Directions from a well-known landmark for the first visit

An embedded map with the building pinned correctly, plus these details as text, helps visitors and Google alike; adding Google Maps to your website covers the embed. If you run several buildings, give each its own page, address, photos and Google Business Profile, as websites for businesses with multiple branches explains.

Hostels that also take short stays through travel sites face double bookings unless rooms sync. A booking engine and channel manager keeps one calendar across every channel.

Visits, video tours and holding a bed

For most PGs the visit is the step just before a booking, so every page should lead to it. Keep the visit form short:

  1. Name and phone number
  2. Who is looking: the resident, a parent, or both
  3. Preferred room type and move-in date
  4. Student or working, and which college or office area
  5. Preferred visit day and time, or "video tour on WhatsApp"

Families from another city often cannot visit before term starts, so offer a live video tour on WhatsApp and keep a short walk-through video on each room page. If you let people hold a bed with a token amount, send a payment link and state in writing how long the bed is held, whether the amount counts towards rent and when it is refunded. Do not ask for Aadhaar or other ID documents through a website form; collect them at move-in, store them securely and tell residents why you need them, in line with India's data protection law.

Photos and reviews from real residents

Use photos of the actual rooms, bathrooms, kitchen, dining area and common spaces, taken in daylight on an ordinary day, and replace them after any renovation. Caption shared rooms with their room type so nobody mistakes a triple for a double. Ask satisfied residents, including those moving out, to review you on Google, and show those reviews through a widget rather than retyped quotes; showing Google reviews on your website compares the options. Reply to critical reviews calmly, since parents read the replies too, and never write reviews yourself or offer discounts for good ones.

FAQs

Should a PG website show the rent?

Usually, yes, at least as a starting rent for each room type, along with the deposit and what the rent includes. If you prefer "rent on request", say how to ask and reply quickly.

Can residents pay a booking amount online?

Yes. A payment link or a payment form on the site can take a token amount by UPI or card, with a confirmation that states the room, the amount paid and your refund terms.

Is a listing on property apps enough, or do I need my own website?

Listings reach people browsing many options at once. Your own site is where you can show full rules, menus, safety details and real photos, and it is what people find when they search your PG's name after seeing a listing. The two work well together.

Do I need a separate website for each PG building?

No. One website with a page for each building, with its own address, rooms, photos and map, is easier to keep current.

Can I mark beds as filled myself?

Yes. Room types and vacancies can be set up as fields you edit from the dashboard, so whoever handles bookings can update them from a phone.
