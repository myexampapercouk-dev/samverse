How to Choose a WordPress Maintenance Provider: Questions, Reports and Red Flags

How to compare WordPress maintenance providers: the questions to ask, what a monthly report should show, response times to agree in writing, and red flags.

Key takeaways
  • Ask how updates are tested, where backups are stored and what happens if the site is hacked before you sign.
  • A useful monthly report names the updates, backups, uptime incidents, form tests and next steps, not just "all good".
  • Agree response times for critical, high and routine issues in writing, and watch for lock-in contracts or shared admin logins.

Handing your website to a maintenance provider means trusting someone with your admin access, your backups and, when something breaks, your enquiries. Most providers promise the same things: updates, backups, security and support. The real difference shows up in how they do the work and how they behave when something goes wrong. Here's how to compare them before you sign.

Start with what your site actually needs

A five-page brochure site, a busy WooCommerce store and a membership site need very different levels of care. Before you speak to anyone, note down:

  • What the site does for your business: enquiries, bookings, orders or just credibility
  • How often you need content changes such as prices, photos or new pages
  • What would hurt most if it stopped working, and for how long you could live with it
  • Who has access today: an old developer, a marketing agency, your own team

For the tasks a plan should cover each month, see what a monthly WordPress maintenance plan should include. This guide is about choosing the person or company who does them.

Questions to ask before you sign

  1. How do you test updates? Look for a backup before every update and a check of key pages afterwards, ideally on a staging copy for bigger updates. "We turn on auto-updates" is not a process.
  2. Where are backups stored, and how often are they taken? They should be off-site, with several copies kept. Ask when they last restored one.
  3. What happens if my site is hacked? Is clean-up included, charged separately or not offered at all?
  4. What are your response times? For emergencies and for routine changes, and whether that means working hours or any time.
  5. How many content changes are included? In hours or tasks, and what happens to unused time.
  6. How will you access my site? A provider should have its own user account, not share yours.
  7. Who pays for premium plugin licences, and in whose name are they?
  8. Do you take on sites you didn't build? If yes, ask what the first health check involves.
  9. What happens if I leave? Access, backups and licence details should be handed over without drama.
  10. Can I cancel monthly, or is there a minimum term?

Vague answers to the first three questions tell you more than any sales page.

What a monthly report should show

A report is how you know the work is actually happening. It doesn't need to be long, but it should be specific.

SectionA useful report showsWarning sign
UpdatesWhich plugins, themes and core versions were updated"All updates done" with no detail
BackupsDates, where they're stored and the last restore testNo mention of where backups live
UptimeAny downtime, how long it lasted and the causeA percentage with no explanation of incidents
SecurityScan results and anything that needed actionScary numbers of "blocked attacks" with no context
SpeedKey pages tested and changes since last monthA single score with no pages named
Forms and checkoutTest enquiries or orders sent and receivedNot tested at all
Work and next stepsIssues fixed, changes made and recommendationsNo recommendations, month after month

Ask to see a sample report before you sign. Anyone doing the work properly will have one ready.

Response times: agree them in writing

"Quick support" means different things to different people. Agree severity levels like these, with a response time for each:

  • Critical: site down, hacked, or checkout and payments broken
  • High: contact form not sending, a key page broken on mobile
  • Routine: content changes, new photos, price updates

Then check the details:

  • Response or resolution? "We'll reply within an hour" is not the same as "we'll fix it within an hour".
  • Working hours and holidays. Does support pause on Sundays, during Diwali or over long weekends? For overseas businesses, which time zone applies?
  • How to reach them. WhatsApp, email or a ticket system, and which one counts for emergencies.

Red flags

  • Asking for your personal admin login instead of creating their own account; see WordPress user roles explained
  • Backups kept only on the same server as the website
  • Using nulled (pirated) premium plugins to save on licences
  • Registering your domain or hosting in their own name
  • Long lock-in contracts, or a large annual payment upfront with no trial period
  • "Unlimited" everything at a price that can't cover real work
  • No reports, or reports that never change from month to month
  • Being unable to explain in plain language what they did and why

Whoever you hire, run through the website ownership checklist so your domain, hosting and key accounts stay in your name.

Freelancer, agency or your hosting company?

OptionStrengthsWhat to check
FreelancerDirect contact with the person doing the work, who gets to know your siteWho covers emergencies when they're unavailable
AgencyA bigger team and more formal processesWho actually works on your site, and how requests are passed on
Hosting company's managed serviceServer-level backups, security and sometimes automatic updatesWhether fixing plugin conflicts, broken layouts or forms is included; plans vary, so check current details

Many businesses combine them: good hosting for the server, and a developer for everything inside WordPress.

What a good first month looks like

  1. A full backup before anything is touched
  2. A health check of plugins, themes, PHP version, user accounts and security
  3. A list of problems found, sorted by urgency, with what's included and what would cost extra
  4. Uptime monitoring and off-site backups set up and confirmed
  5. A first report that gives you a baseline to compare future months against

If a provider updates everything on day one without a backup or a health check, that's probably how they'll work every month.
