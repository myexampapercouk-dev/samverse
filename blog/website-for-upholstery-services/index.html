Websites for Upholstery and Sofa Repair Services: Repair vs Reupholster Advice, Fabric Catalogues, Home Visits and Before-and-After Galleries

What an upholstery or sofa repair website needs: repair vs reupholstery advice, a fabric catalogue, home visit booking and your own before-and-after work.

Key takeaways
  • Help visitors decide between repair, reupholstery and buying new, honestly; that advice earns more trust than any slogan.
  • Show real fabrics, your own before-and-after work (published with the customer's permission) and a clear home visit or photo estimate process.
  • Explain what drives the price instead of printing figures that go stale, and name the areas you really cover for pickup and home visits.

A good upholstery website helps someone with a tired sofa answer three questions: should I repair, reupholster or replace it, roughly what will it involve, and how do I get someone to look at it? It does that with honest advice on each option, a fabric catalogue with real swatches, a simple way to send photos or book a home visit, before-and-after photos of your own work, a plain explanation of what affects the price, and a clear list of the areas you serve.

Most of these customers search on a phone with phrases like "sofa repair near me" or "sofa reupholstery in" plus their area, look at a few photos and decide whom to message. The site I would build for an upholstery business through WordPress website development is designed around that short journey: proof first, then an easy WhatsApp or booking step.

Repair, reupholster or replace: answer the customer's real question

Almost every enquiry starts with uncertainty. A page that explains the options honestly, including when buying new makes more sense, sets you apart from competitors who say everything is worth saving.

OptionWhat it usually involvesOften makes sense whenMay not make sense when
RepairFixing a torn seam, sagging seat, broken spring or webbing, loose legs, refilling cushionsThe fabric is still in good condition and the problem is localThe fabric is worn everywhere or badly stained
ReupholsteryStripping to the frame, replacing foam and padding as needed, new fabric, sometimes frame repairsThe frame is solid, the piece has sentimental value, or it is an unusual size or quality that is hard to replaceThe frame is weak, damaged by termites or poorly made
Fabric cover or recovering onlyA new cover over existing foamComfort is fine and only the look needs updatingThe foam has collapsed; a new cover won't fix comfort
ReplaceBuying newThe frame is failing, or the cost of reupholstery is close to a comparable new pieceThe piece is well built and the customer likes its shape

Give this advice a page of its own, linked from the homepage. Mention that the final advice follows an inspection, because frame and spring problems often only show once the old fabric comes off.

A fabric catalogue people can actually use

Fabric drives both the look and the cost, and customers often feel lost. A catalogue page turns that confusion into a reason to contact you.

  • Group by type: cotton blends, linen look, velvet, chenille, jacquard, leatherette and any others you stock.
  • Real photos: a close-up for texture and a photo on a finished piece for scale, taken in daylight. Mention that screen colours vary.
  • Practical notes: how each type handles pets, children, sun-facing rooms and humid monsoon weather, and how it is cleaned. Describe properties fairly; avoid words like "stain-proof" unless the supplier's own documentation says so.
  • A price band, not a price: "standard", "mid" and "premium" ranges help people choose without you publishing figures that change with every supplier price list.
  • How to see swatches: visit your showroom, see sample books on a home visit, or receive swatch photos on WhatsApp.

If you accept customer-supplied fabric, say so, and explain that you will advise on the quantity needed after measuring.

Home visit booking and photo estimates

Two routes cover most customers: send photos for a first idea, or book a home visit to measure and show fabrics. Offer both.

Photo estimates

A WhatsApp button with a pre-filled message is usually the easiest route; WhatsApp on your business website covers the setup. Tell people exactly what to send:

  • Photos from the front, side and back, plus close-ups of damage
  • Approximate dimensions and number of seats
  • What they want done, and any fabric preference
  • Area or pin code

Home visits

A short booking form or calendar asks for the address, preferred day and time window, number of pieces and whether anyone will be home to show the furniture. If you charge for a visit, or adjust that charge against the order, say so clearly before they book. Online appointment booking covers reminders and calendar tools if you want slots to be booked directly.

Then explain the whole process in steps: estimate, fabric choice, pickup with wrapping, workshop work with a progress photo if wanted, quality check and delivery. Give timelines in stages, and name what can extend them, such as fabric availability or repairs found after stripping.

Pricing without publishing numbers

Upholstery prices depend on too many variables for a single figure to be honest. Explain the factors instead, so the eventual quote makes sense:

FactorWhy it changes the quote
Size and designMore seats, curves, tufting, piping or loose cushions take more fabric and labour
FabricFabric types and ranges vary widely in price and in how much is needed for patterned designs
Foam and paddingNew foam adds material cost but often makes the biggest difference to comfort
Frame and springsStructural repairs add time and materials, and may only show after stripping
Pickup distance and accessUpper floors without a lift, or far-off areas, may add transport cost

State that photo estimates are a range and the final quote follows inspection. Showing prices on your website explains when ranges and "starting from" figures help and when they backfire.

Before-and-after photos: your own work, with permission

Transformation is what sells upholstery, so the gallery is your most important content. Make it trustworthy:

  • Only show jobs your workshop has done; never use supplier or stock images as your work. Stock photos vs real photos explains why customers notice.
  • Ask the customer before publishing photos taken in their home, and crop out family photos, documents and anything identifying.
  • Shoot before and after from the same angle and in similar light.
  • Caption each pair: what was done, the fabric used and roughly how long it took.
  • Add a few work-in-progress shots, such as a stripped frame or new foam, so people see the work that is hidden once the fabric goes on.
  • Resize and compress every photo before upload, so a long gallery still opens fast on a phone; see preparing photos for your website.

Service areas, stated honestly

Upholstery is local, and pickup of bulky furniture has real limits. On an "Areas we serve" section, list the localities or pin code ranges you cover for home visits and pickup, any areas where a transport charge applies, and whether small repairs can be done at the customer's home. Avoid creating a near-identical page for every locality; that pattern tends to be treated as doorway pages. Local landing pages done right shows what to do instead.

Keep your Google Business Profile in step with the website, with the same name, phone and hours, and add recent before-and-after photos to it regularly.

Commercial work: cafes, hotels and offices

Restaurant booths, hotel headboards and office seating are larger jobs that can repeat. A separate page should cover the kinds of work you take on, working in phases or outside business hours, fabrics suited to heavy use, bulk quotes with GST invoices, and a single point of contact. Show commercial projects only with the client's permission. A project form with fields for the number of pieces, photos and a preferred schedule keeps these leads apart from household ones.

Reviews and trust

Customers are handing over furniture they may have owned for decades. Ask happy customers for Google reviews soon after delivery, ideally with their own after photos; how to get more Google reviews has wording that works. Show your workshop and team, and mention a workmanship warranty only if you actually offer one, with its terms.

Launch checklist

  • Repair vs reupholster vs replace advice on its own page
  • Separate pages for sofa repair, reupholstery and chairs
  • Fabric catalogue with real photos and price bands
  • WhatsApp photo estimate and home visit booking
  • Process and staged timelines explained
  • Gallery of your own work, published with permission
  • Service areas listed, matching your Google Business Profile
  • Fast loading on mobile with compressed images

FAQs

Should an upholstery business publish prices on its website?

Usually not as fixed figures, because size, fabric, foam and frame condition change the price a lot. Explain the factors, show fabric price bands, and offer a photo estimate followed by a final quote after inspection.

Can I use photos from my fabric supplier?

Use supplier photos only for fabric swatches, and only if the supplier allows it. Never present them as your finished work; your gallery should show jobs your workshop did.

Do I need a separate page for each area I serve?

No. A clear list of areas on your contact or service pages is usually better than many near-identical locality pages, which can be treated as doorway pages.

How should customers send photos for an estimate?

WhatsApp is easiest for most people. Ask for front, side and back photos, close-ups of damage, rough dimensions and their area, so your first reply can be a useful range.

Is a landing page useful for an upholstery business?

Yes, for campaigns such as pre-festival sofa makeovers or a commercial offer. A focused page that matches the ad's promise usually converts better than sending ad traffic to the homepage.
