Agency Handoff Checklist: What to Give Your WordPress Developer Before the Build Starts

An agency handoff checklist for outsourced WordPress builds: design files, content, access, integrations, SEO and sign-off, so your partner can start.

Key takeaways
  • Most outsourced WordPress projects slip because of missing inputs such as unlicensed fonts, unspecified forms or late hosting access, not because of the code.
  • Hand over one approved design file with every state, final content, all access through proper user accounts, and a written list of integrations and SEO requirements.
  • Agree who answers questions, how feedback is collected and what counts as done before the build starts, so review rounds stay short and predictable.

An agency handoff checklist is the package you give your WordPress developer once a project is sold and the design is signed off: final design files, content, access, integrations, SEO requirements and the rules for review. Get it complete on day one and an outsourced build runs smoothly; leave gaps and the timeline slips while everyone waits for a font licence or a login.

This is different from the website brief template, which helps a business get an accurate quote. The handoff comes later, when the job is confirmed and the work is ready to start. If you are an agency outsourcing WordPress builds to a partner in India, this is the list to work through before you send the kickoff email.

Why handoffs go wrong

Developers rarely lose days on the hard parts of a build. Days disappear on small, unanswered questions: what the form should do after submission, which email address receives enquiries, whether the blog needs categories, what the mobile menu looks like. Each question means a message, a wait and often a call with the end client. Multiply that by twenty and a three-week build becomes six.

A good handoff answers those questions in advance. It also protects your margin, because the hours your partner spends waiting or redoing work are hours someone eventually pays for.

1. Design files, with every state

  • One approved file in Figma or XD, with final frames clearly marked and old explorations moved to a separate page.
  • Desktop and mobile frames for every template, plus tablet where the layout changes in a meaningful way.
  • Interactive states: hover and focus for buttons and links, open menus, dropdowns, accordions, tabs and sticky header behaviour.
  • Form states: errors, success messages and what happens on the thank-you page.
  • Easily forgotten templates: single blog post, blog archive, search results, 404, legal pages and, for stores, product, category, cart, checkout and account pages.
  • Empty states such as no search results or an empty cart.
  • Dev Mode or inspect access so spacing, colours and exported assets can be checked without guessing.

Designers preparing these files will find more detail in how designers should prepare Figma files for WordPress.

2. Brand assets and licences

  • Logo files in SVG, plus a favicon or square icon.
  • Font files or the Google Fonts names, and confirmation that any paid font is licensed for web use on the client's domain.
  • Original images and videos at full resolution, not screenshots from the design file.
  • Licences or receipts for stock photos and icon packs, so nothing has to be swapped out after launch.

3. Final content, not lorem ipsum

Placeholder text hides layout problems. A heading that fits neatly in Figma may wrap to three lines with the client's real wording, and a service page designed for four paragraphs may arrive with twelve. Ideally, hand over approved copy for every page in a shared document, labelled by page and section. If some content is still with the client, say which pages are final, which are drafts and when the rest is due, and agree whether the developer builds with placeholders or waits.

4. Access, set up the right way

Every account should belong to the client or the agency, and access should be given through user accounts, not shared passwords sent over chat. The website ownership checklist explains why this matters long after launch.

5. Functionality and integrations, written down

Designs show what pages look like, not what they do. List each piece of behaviour in plain sentences:

  • Forms: fields, required fields, who receives each form, auto-reply text and where entries are stored.
  • Integrations: CRM, email marketing tool, WhatsApp chat button, booking system, payment gateway or shipping service, with logins or API access.
  • Content types: anything the client will add repeatedly, such as projects, team members, jobs or testimonials, so they can be built as editable post types rather than hand-built pages.
  • Plugins the client already pays for, such as Elementor Pro or a forms plugin, and who holds the licence.
  • Languages, currencies and regions, if the site serves visitors outside India.

6. SEO and redesign requirements

If the project replaces an existing site, SEO needs its own handoff section, because this is where redesigns most often lose traffic:

  • A list of current URLs, ideally with traffic data, and the new URL each one maps to.
  • Page titles and meta descriptions, or a note that the developer should carry over the existing ones.
  • Which SEO plugin to use and who owns Search Console.
  • Any schema, such as LocalBusiness or Product, and any tracking events the client's reports rely on.

7. Communication, feedback and sign-off

  • One point of contact on the agency side who can make decisions, and whether the developer may ever speak to the end client directly.
  • Working hours and response times, which matter if your team is in the UK, US or Australia and your partner is in India.
  • How feedback arrives: one consolidated list per round, with screenshots and page URLs. How to give website feedback to a developer explains what makes feedback quick to act on.
  • Number of review rounds and what counts as a change request rather than a fix.
  • Definition of done: browsers and devices to test, speed expectations, accessibility basics and who signs off before go-live.

Choosing a build approach before you hand over

Agree the build method at handoff, not halfway through. Elementor suits clients who want to edit layouts visually; a block theme or custom blocks suit clients who want speed and tighter control of the design. The trade-offs are covered in Figma to WordPress: Elementor, block theme or Bricks. Whatever you choose, tell the developer how much editing freedom the client should have, because that changes how pages are built.

If you are still deciding whether to outsource at all, outsourcing WordPress development to India covers how agencies usually structure the relationship, and the WordPress and Shopify developer in India page explains how remote projects are run across time zones. One habit worth copying from agencies that outsource well: keep the handoff as a reusable template, update it after every project with the questions that came up, and the next handoff gets faster each time.

FAQs

What should an agency give a WordPress developer before the build?

An approved design with mobile frames and interactive states, brand assets and font licences, final content, access to hosting and other accounts, a written list of functionality and integrations, SEO requirements for any redesign, and clear rules for feedback and sign-off.

Can development start before all the content is ready?

Yes, as long as everyone agrees which pages use placeholders and when real content arrives. Expect some layout adjustments when the final copy goes in, so leave time for that in the schedule.

Should the developer have direct contact with our client?

It depends on your model. In a white-label setup the developer usually works only with the agency. Some agencies allow direct technical calls, for example with the client's IT person about DNS or email. Decide this in writing at handoff.

How do we share passwords safely?

Create a separate user account for the developer wherever the platform allows it, and use a password manager's sharing feature for anything that only has one login. Remove or downgrade access once the project and its support period end.

Who should own the hosting and plugin licences?

Ideally the end client, or your agency on the client's behalf, never the developer's personal account. That keeps the site transferable if you change partners later.
