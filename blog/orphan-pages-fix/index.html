Orphan Pages: What They Are and How to Find and Fix Them

How to find orphan pages in WordPress using your sitemap, Search Console and a crawler, then fix each one by linking, merging, redirecting or removing it.

Key takeaways
  • An orphan page has no internal links pointing to it, so visitors cannot reach it and Google has little reason to treat it as important.
  • Find orphans by comparing the URLs in your sitemap and Search Console with the URLs a crawler reaches by following links from the home page.
  • For each orphan decide: link it from relevant pages, merge it into a stronger page with a 301 redirect, or remove or noindex it if it has no value.

An orphan page is a page on your website that no other page links to. It may be in your sitemap and even indexed, but no menu, button or text link leads to it. To find orphan pages in WordPress, compare the URLs in your sitemap and Google Search Console with the URLs a crawler reaches by following links, then link, merge, redirect or remove each one.

Most business sites have a few, usually created by accident during a redesign, a campaign or a migration. This guide explains why they matter, how to find them and how to decide what to do with each. For the basics, start with internal linking explained.

Why orphan pages are a problem

Search engines discover and understand pages mainly by following links. When nothing on your site points to a page:

  • Google may crawl it rarely. If it only knows the URL from the sitemap, it has little signal that the page is important. Such pages often appear under "Discovered, currently not indexed" or "Crawled, currently not indexed" in Search Console.
  • It gets none of your site's authority. Links from your home page and popular posts help other pages rank. An orphan gets no share.
  • Visitors never see it. A useful service or product page does no work if people on your site cannot reach it.
  • It goes out of date. Forgotten pages keep old prices, discontinued services or a wrong phone number for years.

How pages become orphans

Orphan pages are rarely created on purpose. Common causes on WordPress sites:

  • A redesign removed pages from the menu, but the pages were never deleted or redirected.
  • Landing pages for Google Ads or a festival offer were built outside the menu and forgotten after the campaign.
  • A product or service was discontinued and its links were removed, but the page stayed live.
  • Blog posts were published with no links to them except the blog archive, which then paginated them deep out of reach.
  • A migration changed URLs, and old internal links still point to the previous address.
  • Page builders or plugins created test pages, thank-you pages or duplicate drafts that got published.

How to find orphan pages in WordPress

The method is simple: get a list of every URL that exists, get a list of every URL reachable by links, and compare.

Step 1: List every URL that exists

Start with your XML sitemap, usually at /sitemap_index.xml or /wp-sitemap.xml depending on your SEO plugin. Add URLs from Search Console's Pages report and the Performance report, which can reveal pages Google knows about that are missing from the sitemap. In WordPress itself, the Pages and Posts lists show everything published. The guide to XML sitemaps explains what the sitemap should contain.

Step 2: List every URL reachable by links

Run a crawler, such as a desktop SEO crawler, starting from your home page. It follows links the way Google does and lists every page it reaches. Make sure it is set to respect the same rules as a normal visitor and does not crawl from the sitemap, otherwise orphans will appear reachable.

Step 3: Compare the two lists

Paste both lists into a spreadsheet and find URLs that appear in the first list but not the second. Those are your orphan candidates. Some SEO crawlers can do this comparison for you if you connect the sitemap or Search Console.

Step 4: Check analytics

Pages that get traffic in analytics only from ads, email or direct visits, and never from internal navigation, are often orphans or near-orphans. Pages with no visits at all for months are also worth checking.

After any redirect, update internal links so they point to the final URL directly rather than through the redirect. If you are changing addresses, the guide to SEO-friendly URLs covers safe redirects.

How to link an orphan page properly

Adding one random link in the footer is not much help. Good internal links come from pages on the same topic, use descriptive anchor text and sit in the main content. For example, an orphaned page about "AMC for solar panels" should be linked from the main solar service page and from blog posts about solar maintenance, with anchor text that describes it.

  • Link important pages from the main menu or a hub page such as "Services" or "Locations".
  • Add two or three contextual links from related posts.
  • Use breadcrumbs, so every page links up to its parent.
  • Add a related posts section to blog posts.

Orphan pages on WooCommerce stores

Stores create orphans easily. Products removed from all categories, out-of-stock items hidden from catalogue pages, and attribute or filter pages can all end up unlinked. Check that every product you sell sits in at least one visible category, and decide what happens to discontinued products: redirect to a replacement or category, or keep the page with a clear "no longer available" note and links to alternatives. The WooCommerce SEO guide covers store structure in more detail.

Orphan pages after a redesign or migration

The most common time for orphans to appear is right after a site is rebuilt or moved to new hosting. The new design has a cleaner menu, a few old pages are left out, and nobody notices because the old pages still load. Months later, Search Console shows a handful of URLs getting impressions that no visitor can reach from the site.

A simple routine prevents this:

  • Before the redesign, export every URL from the sitemap, Search Console and analytics into one sheet.
  • For each URL, write where it will live on the new site: same URL, new URL with a redirect, merged into another page, or removed.
  • After launch, crawl the new site and check that every URL in the sheet is either reachable by links or redirected.
  • Two to four weeks later, check Search Console again for old URLs that still get impressions.

The guide to redesigning a website without losing rankings covers the wider process.

A quick monthly check for small sites

If your site has fewer than a hundred pages, you do not need complex tools every month. Open the Pages list in WordPress, sort by date, and for each recent page ask one question: which other page links here? If the answer is none, add a link from a related service page or post before moving on. For blogs, check that each new post links to at least one older post and that at least one older post links back to it. This takes a few minutes and stops orphans from building up between proper audits.

Preventing orphan pages in future

  1. Every time you publish, add at least two internal links to the new page from older relevant pages.
  2. Before a redesign, export the full URL list and map where each page will go.
  3. After campaigns, decide the future of each landing page: keep, merge, redirect or remove.
  4. Run a crawl and sitemap comparison every few months.
  5. Watch Search Console's indexing reports; the guide to page indexing errors explains the statuses.

FAQs

How do I find orphan pages in WordPress?

Export all URLs from your sitemap and Search Console, crawl the site from the home page with an SEO crawler, and compare the two lists. URLs that exist but are not reached by the crawl are orphan pages.

Are orphan pages bad for SEO?

They are not penalised, but they are weak. Without internal links they get little authority, may be crawled less often and are often left out of the index or rank poorly.

Should I delete orphan pages?

Only if they have no value. Useful pages should be linked, overlapping pages merged and redirected, and old pages redirected to the closest current page.

Is a page in my sitemap still an orphan?

Yes. A sitemap helps Google discover a URL, but it does not replace internal links from other pages.

Are Google Ads landing pages orphan pages?

Often, and that can be intentional. If a landing page is only for ads, it can stay out of the menu and be set to noindex.
