How to Update Old Blog Posts to Win Back Traffic: Find, Refresh, Merge and Measure

How to update old blog posts to win back traffic: find slipping posts in Search Console, refresh or merge them, handle dates and redirects, and measure.

Key takeaways
  • Start with posts that are losing clicks, sit just off page one or get impressions without clicks in Search Console, and give priority to posts that bring enquiries.
  • Decide whether each post should be updated, merged, redirected or removed, then refresh it in place: answer first, correct the facts, cover current questions and add internal links.
  • Keep the URL, show an honest updated date, request indexing, and compare the same length of time before and after so you know whether the refresh worked.

To update an old blog post, find posts that are losing clicks or sitting just off page one in Google Search Console, check what the search results reward today, then improve the post in place: correct outdated facts, answer the questions it now appears for, tighten the opening, sharpen the title and description, and add internal links. Keep the same URL, show an honest "updated" date and ask Google to recrawl it. Posts that are too thin or overlap with others are better merged into one, with redirects, and posts that no longer fit your business can go.

Updating often beats writing something new, because the old URL already has history, some visibility and perhaps links. A slow decline is different from a sudden drop. If traffic fell sharply over a few days, work through why website traffic suddenly drops first.

Why old posts lose traffic

  • Outdated information. Steps, menu names, prices or screenshots no longer match reality. A guide still describing Universal Analytics, which Google switched off in 2023, is a typical example.
  • Better competition. Other sites published fuller, clearer or more recent answers.
  • A change in intent. Google now prefers a different kind of page for the search, such as a comparison instead of a definition.
  • New features in the results, such as People Also Ask boxes or AI Overviews, answering simple questions before anyone clicks.
  • Your own posts competing, when several articles cover the same topic and none is clearly the main one.
  • Technical wear: broken links, missing images, slow pages and outdated internal links.

Step 1: Find posts worth updating

In Search Console, open Performance > Search results, click the date filter, choose Compare and pick "last 3 months vs previous period" (or the same months last year, for seasonal businesses). Switch to the Pages tab and sort by the clicks difference to see which posts are slipping. Then look for these signals:

SignalWhere to find itLikely fix
Clicks falling compared with the previous periodPerformance, compare mode, Pages tabA full refresh
Average position roughly 5 to 20 with steady impressionsPages tab, with Average position switched onDeeper content and more internal links; it is close to page one
Many impressions, few clicksPages tab, sorted by impressions, with CTR switched onA better title and meta description
Searches the post appears for but doesn't answerClick the page, then the Queries tabNew sections or FAQ answers
Posts that bring enquiriesGA4: Reports > Engagement > Landing page, with key eventsProtect and improve these first

Also make a note of posts with an old year in the title, outdated screenshots or prices, and posts you know are wrong. Finding content ideas and pages to improve in Search Console goes deeper into these reports.

Step 2: Decide what each post needs

DecisionWhen it fitsWhat to do
UpdateThe topic still matters to your customers and the post has some impressions, traffic or linksRefresh it in place, at the same URL
MergeSeveral thin or overlapping posts cover the same questionCombine the best of each into the strongest URL and 301-redirect the others to it
RedirectA newer, better page now covers the topic fully301-redirect the old post to it
RemoveIrrelevant to your business, with no traffic and no linksDelete it and let it return a 404 or 410
Leave aloneAccurate and performing wellCheck it again next review

Merging is often the biggest win on older blogs, where the same subject was written about three times over the years. It also removes the competition between your own posts; topic clusters and pillar pages explains how to organise what's left. Don't delete posts in bulk just because they are short: check each for traffic, links and enquiries first.

Step 3: Check what searchers want now

Search the post's main query on your phone, in a private window, and compare your post with what ranks today. Which subtopics do the top results cover that you don't? Are they guides, comparisons, checklists or videos? Which questions appear under People Also Ask? If the type of page Google shows has changed, a light edit won't be enough; see search intent explained.

Step 4: Refresh the content

  • Answer first. Open with a direct two- or three-sentence answer to the main question, then the detail.
  • Correct everything that changed: facts, steps, plugin menus, prices, laws and screenshots. Mark third-party details that change often with "at the time of writing".
  • Cover the questions you found in the Queries tab and People Also Ask, as new sections or FAQ answers.
  • Add what only you know: examples from your own work, photos, the mistakes you see customers make, how you handle the problem.
  • Cut as well as add. Remove padding, repeated points and sections that no longer apply. A tighter post often performs better than a longer one.
  • Improve the structure: clear headings, short paragraphs, tables and numbered steps where they help.
  • Fix links and images: replace broken links, link to newer posts, compress images and write useful alt text. Finding and fixing broken links shows how to catch them without a heavy plugin.

Step 5: Rewrite the title and meta description

If the post gets impressions but few clicks, the title and description are the first thing to change. Put the main search phrase near the start of the title, make the promise specific, and keep it within about 60 characters so it isn't cut off. In Yoast or Rank Math, edit the SEO title and meta description in the plugin's panel below or beside the editor. How to write meta titles and descriptions has examples.

Step 6: Strengthen internal links both ways

Link from the refreshed post to the service page it relates to and to newer articles on the same subject. Then add links to it from two or three related posts that already rank, using descriptive anchor text such as "updating your WordPress plugins safely" rather than "click here". Those incoming links are a strong signal that the post matters.

Step 7: Keep the URL and handle dates honestly

  • Don't change the URL of a post that has rankings or links. If the slug contains a year that makes it look stale and you must change it, set up a 301 redirect from the old address; see 301 vs 302 redirects.
  • Show an updated date when the changes are meaningful. WordPress records the modified date whenever you save, and Yoast and Rank Math include it in the page's structured data and sitemap automatically. Many themes, and the post date options in Elementor Pro, can display an "Updated on" date for readers.
  • Don't fake freshness. Changing the published date or the year in the title without real changes misleads readers, and Google has said that changing dates alone doesn't help.

Step 8: Ask Google to recrawl, then measure

  1. Inspect the URL in Search Console and click Request indexing. The daily quota is small, so use it on your most important updates; getting pages indexed faster explains the limits.
  2. Record the date and what you changed in a simple spreadsheet.
  3. After several weeks, filter the Performance report to that page and compare the same number of days before and after the update. Allow for seasons: a festival month or exam period can distort the comparison.
  4. If nothing has moved after a couple of months, look again at intent, depth and links, or consider merging it into a stronger page.

How often to review old posts

A workable rhythm for a small business blog: check your top posts and biggest decliners every quarter, review every post once a year, and update straight away when something important changes, such as a tool's menus, a price, a regulation or a Google feature your post describes. Put the review in your content calendar alongside new posts, so refreshing doesn't depend on remembering.

FAQs

How often should I update old blog posts?

Update when there is a reason: the information has changed, clicks are falling or the post sits just off page one. For most small business blogs, a quarterly check of top and declining posts and a yearly review of everything is enough.

Does updating old content help SEO?

It can, when the update makes the page genuinely more accurate and useful for the searches it targets. Cosmetic changes, such as a new date with the same content, don't help.

Should I change the published date when I update a post?

Keep the original published date and show a separate "updated" date when you've made real changes. WordPress tracks the modified date automatically, and SEO plugins pass it to Google.

Should I delete old blog posts with no traffic?

Not automatically. If a post is relevant, improve it or merge it into a related post with a redirect. Delete only posts that are irrelevant to your business and have no traffic or links.

Do I need to request indexing after updating a post?

It isn't required, because Google recrawls pages and reads your sitemap's updated dates, but requesting indexing in URL Inspection can get an important refresh seen sooner.
