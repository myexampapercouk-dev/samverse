How to Add a Blog to Your Existing Business Website: Subfolder or Subdomain, WordPress Setup, Design, Categories and Your First Posts

How to add a blog to your existing website: subfolder vs subdomain, WordPress or not, matching the design, categories, first posts and links to services.

Key takeaways
  • Put the blog in a subfolder such as yourdomain.com/blog/ on the same platform as your site where you can; it keeps everything one site to manage, measure and link.
  • Match the design, set up a few categories that mirror your services, and handle sitemaps, Search Console and SEO settings before the first post goes live.
  • Start with a handful of posts that answer the questions customers ask before they buy, and link every post to the service page it relates to.

To add a blog to an existing website, put it in a subfolder such as yourdomain.com/blog/ rather than on a subdomain, and build it on the same platform as the rest of the site if that platform has a decent blog. Match its design to your site, set up a few categories that mirror your services, and launch with a handful of posts that answer the questions customers ask before they buy, each linking to the service page it relates to. On WordPress, most of this is configuration; on other platforms, you choose between the built-in blog, WordPress alongside your site, or moving the whole site.

If your site already runs on WordPress, adding a blog is mostly settings, templates and a menu link. If it doesn't and the platform is holding you back, rebuilding on WordPress with redirects for every URL that changes is part of WordPress website development. For the search side, such as which post should target which search and how posts link to services, see WordPress SEO services; I don't write blog articles, but I can tell you which answers are missing and what each should cover.

Not sure a blog is worth it yet? Many service businesses can rank well without one; ranking without a blog explains when a blog becomes worth the effort.

Google has said it can work with either a subfolder or a subdomain, so this isn't about a penalty. The practical case for a subfolder is that everything stays one site: one navigation, one analytics setup, and links between posts and service pages that sit naturally together. A subdomain is a reasonable fallback when the main site's platform can't host a blog in a folder.

If your site is already on WordPress

  1. Create a page called Blog under Pages, Add New, and leave it empty.
  2. Set it as the posts page in Settings, Reading: choose "A static page", keep your homepage as it is, and select Blog as the posts page.
  3. Decide the post URL format in Settings, Permalinks. If posts should sit under /blog/, a custom structure does it. On an established site, change this only before you publish posts, because it changes post URLs.
  4. Create your categories under Posts, Categories, and delete or rename "Uncategorized".
  5. Design the post and archive templates in your theme or page builder, so posts match the rest of the site.
  6. Check your SEO plugin settings for posts: title format, schema type, whether category and tag archives are indexed, and that posts appear in the sitemap.
  7. Add Blog to the menu, and a "Latest articles" block to the homepage if it suits the design.
Settings, Permalinks, Custom structure:  /blog/%postname%/
Result for a post:     yourdomain.com/blog/how-to-choose-a-physiotherapist/
Pages are unaffected:  yourdomain.com/contact/

With a custom structure that starts with /blog/, WordPress also puts category archives under /blog/ by default, which keeps everything tidy.

One limitation catches people out: on hosted builders such as Wix or Shopify, you don't control the server, so you can't install WordPress in a folder of that site. Serving a separately hosted blog at /blog/ through a reverse proxy is possible, but it's an advanced setup with its own maintenance. If you're weighing a full move, moving from Wix to WordPress covers the process.

Make the blog look like part of your site

  • The same header, menu, footer, logo, colours and fonts, so readers never feel they've left.
  • A readable layout: a comfortable line length, clear headings and body text large enough on a phone.
  • The author's name and a short bio linking to your About page, plus the publish or last-updated date.
  • A call to action inside and after each post, pointing to the related service rather than a generic contact page.
  • Related posts at the end, so readers keep exploring.
  • Fast pages: compressed images, no heavy sliders, and only the scripts the blog needs.

Set up categories that mirror your services

Categories organise the blog for readers and show Google how topics relate. Keep to a few broad ones, usually three to six, that line up with what you sell. A physiotherapy clinic might use Back and Neck Pain, Sports Injuries, Post-Surgery Rehab and Clinic News. Avoid one category per post, and don't create dozens of tags that each hold a single article, because those archives become thin pages. SEO for category and tag pages explains when to index, noindex or improve them.

Your first posts: what to write

Good first posts answer questions you already hear from customers on the phone, on WhatsApp and at the counter. Five to ten genuinely useful posts make a better start than thirty thin ones.

Link every post to a service page

A business blog earns its keep by sending readers to the pages that bring enquiries. Every post should link to its related service page early, in context, and again at the end. Service pages should link back to the most helpful posts. Over time, a group of posts around one service, all linking to it and to each other, forms a topic cluster; topic clusters and pillar pages shows how to plan one, and internal linking explained covers anchor text and how many links to add.

Before the first post goes live

  • Posts appear in the XML sitemap, and the sitemap is submitted in Google Search Console.
  • Each post has its own title and meta description, one H1 and a clean URL.
  • Article schema is added, usually by your SEO plugin.
  • Comments are switched off, or moderated, so the blog doesn't fill with spam.
  • The author bio and About page explain who is writing and why they know the subject.
  • Analytics records visits to posts and any enquiries that start from them.

A publishing rhythm you can keep

Consistency matters more than volume. Pick a pace you can sustain alongside running the business, whether that's one post a month or more, and plan topics a few months ahead in a content calendar. After a few months, Search Console shows which posts get impressions and for which searches, which tells you what to write next and which older posts to improve. Updating a post that already gets impressions is often worth more than writing a new one.

FAQs

Should my blog be on a subdomain or a subfolder?

A subfolder such as yourdomain.com/blog/ is usually the practical choice for a business, because the blog and your service pages stay one site. A subdomain is a reasonable fallback when your main platform can't host a blog in a folder.

Can I add a WordPress blog to a Wix or Shopify site?

Not in a folder of that site, because you don't control their servers. You can use the platform's built-in blog, put WordPress on a subdomain, or move the whole site to WordPress if the platform limits you in other ways too.

How many posts do I need before launching a blog?

There's no fixed number. A handful of genuinely useful posts that answer common customer questions is a better start than many short ones, because each post should be worth reading on its own.

Does a blog help SEO for a service business?

It can, when posts answer real questions and link to your service pages. It's not a requirement: strong service pages, local SEO and reviews often matter more at first, and a blog adds most once those are in place.

Will adding a blog slow down my website?

It shouldn't, if images are compressed and the blog uses the same lightweight theme as the rest of the site. Heavy related-post plugins, sliders and social widgets are the usual culprits.
