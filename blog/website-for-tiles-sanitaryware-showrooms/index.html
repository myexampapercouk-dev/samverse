Website for Tiles and Sanitaryware Showrooms: Catalogues, Authorised Brands, Visits and Builder Enquiries

How to plan a website for a tiles showroom in India: catalogues by room and size, authorised brands, visit bookings and builder or contractor enquiries.

Key takeaways
  • Organise the catalogue the way buyers think, by room, look, size and finish, and give each product practical details such as coverage per box and where it can be used.
  • List only brands you are authorised to sell, and never call yourself official or exclusive unless the brand has confirmed it in writing.
  • Make the showroom visit the main call to action for homeowners, and give builders, contractors and architects a separate route for project quantities and BOQs.

A website for a tiles showroom should help buyers shortlist from home, plan a showroom visit and, for builders, send project quantities quickly. That means a catalogue organised by room, look and size, honest brand listings, real installation photos, a simple visit booking and a separate trade enquiry route, all on pages that stay fast despite hundreds of product images.

People buying tiles, sanitaryware and bathroom fittings rarely decide in one visit. They browse on their phones for weeks, share options with family, an architect or a contractor, and only then come in to see colours and finishes in person. If you supply a wide mix of building materials, start with websites for hardware and building material suppliers; this guide is for showrooms where display and selection are the business.

What showroom buyers are trying to work out

Visitors usually fall into two groups. Homeowners renovating a bathroom or kitchen, or building a house, want ideas and reassurance. Builders, contractors, architects and interior designers want availability, rates and delivery. Both ask similar questions:

  • Do you have the look I have in mind, in the size and finish I need?
  • Which brands do you stock, and are you an authorised dealer?
  • How much will I need, and can you supply the whole project from the same batch?
  • When can I visit, and is there parking?
  • Do you deliver to site, and do you give GST invoices?

Organise the catalogue by room, style and size

Customers think in rooms and looks, not supplier product codes. Let them browse in several ways:

  • By room: bathroom, kitchen, living and bedroom floors, balcony and parking, outdoor and elevation walls.
  • By type and look: vitrified, ceramic, porcelain, GVT and PGVT, wood-look, marble-look, stone-look and anti-skid.
  • By size and finish: common sizes such as 600 x 600 mm or 600 x 1200 mm, and matt, satin or glossy finishes.
  • Sanitaryware and fittings: wall-hung and floor-mounted WCs, wash basins, faucets, showers, concealed cisterns and bath accessories.

Each product page should show a close-up and, ideally, an installed photo, plus size, thickness, finish, where it can be used, pieces per box and the area each box covers. For WCs and fittings, add dimensions and practical details such as outlet type (S-trap or P-trap) or whether a concealed cistern is needed, with a note to confirm with their plumber.

Be honest about colour. Screens vary and shades can differ between batches, so encourage visitors to check in the showroom before ordering large quantities.

Filters that work on a phone

A catalogue with hundreds of tiles is only useful if filtering is quick. Keep filters to what buyers actually choose by: room, type, size, finish, colour family and brand. Show the number of results, let people clear filters easily and keep the filter panel usable with one thumb. If the catalogue runs on WooCommerce, the guide to WooCommerce product filters covers plugin choices and how to stop filter pages creating thousands of thin URLs in Google.

A simple coverage calculator, where a visitor enters room dimensions and sees an approximate number of boxes with a wastage allowance, is popular and easy to add. Label results as estimates and suggest confirming with the contractor.

Show brands only where you are authorised

Brand names drive many searches, so it is tempting to list every brand you can get. Don't. List the brands you are actually authorised to sell, and if you can source others on order, say so separately and plainly.

  • Ask your brand contacts about their rules for logo use, product photos and descriptions; many provide dealer assets.
  • Never call yourself "official" or "exclusive" unless the brand has confirmed it in writing.
  • Add your own notes to each range, such as what it suits and what customers like, rather than copying the brand catalogue word for word.

Visualisers and inspiration, used sensibly

Room visualisers that let buyers try a tile on a sample room, or on a photo of their own room, can help some customers decide. Some tile brands offer their own and third-party tools exist, so check what your brands allow and what any tool costs. These tools are often heavy, so keep them on a dedicated page rather than the homepage. Simpler options often work just as well:

  • Galleries of real installations, tagged with the products used.
  • Curated collections such as "warm wood-look bathrooms" or "easy-clean kitchen walls".
  • A "send us a photo of your room" option, so your staff can suggest a few combinations.

Make the showroom visit the main call to action

  • Address, timings, parking, a map and photos of your displays, including any working bathroom setups.
  • A short "book a showroom visit" form asking for the rooms involved, rough area, preferred time and whether an architect or contractor will join.
  • A shortlist feature, so visitors can save products and show the list to your staff when they arrive.
  • A WhatsApp button that pre-fills the product name or code; see adding WhatsApp to your website.

If you are comfortable showing price bands per square foot, they help filter mismatched enquiries. Keep them updated, because supplier prices change; see should you show prices on your website.

A separate route for builders, contractors and architects

Trade buyers do not want inspiration galleries. Give them a clear "project and bulk enquiries" page with a form for project type, location, quantities per area, timeline and an option to upload a BOQ or drawing. Explain what you can offer, such as samples, site delivery, batch-matched supply for large orders, GST invoices and credit terms if you provide them. The structure in RFQ forms for B2B websites works well here.

A short list of projects you have supplied, shared with permission, and details of any architect or designer programme you run will help you win repeat trade business.

Keeping the catalogue up to date

A tile catalogue goes stale quickly. Ranges are discontinued, new sizes arrive and seasonal designs come and go. An out-of-date site frustrates buyers who arrive asking for a product you no longer stock. Build simple routines:

  • Mark discontinued products as unavailable, or redirect their pages to the closest current range, rather than deleting them and leaving broken links.
  • Add new arrivals with a tag so they appear in a "new this month" section on the homepage.
  • Let staff update stock status or "available on order" notes without touching the design.
  • Import product data from a spreadsheet when a brand sends a new range, instead of entering hundreds of tiles by hand. This can be set up for WooCommerce or a custom post type catalogue.

Assign one person in the showroom to own these updates, with a short monthly check of the top-visited product pages. A clean, current catalogue is what makes the website a tool your sales staff use every day, showing products on a tablet or sending links on WhatsApp, rather than a brochure nobody trusts.

Local SEO and speed for an image-heavy catalogue

Most showroom customers search locally: "tiles showroom in [area]", "sanitaryware shop near me", "bathroom fittings in [city]" or "[brand] dealer in [city]".

  • Keep your Google Business Profile complete, with the right categories, timings and plenty of showroom photos; see the Google Business Profile checklist.
  • Create brand pages only for brands you are authorised to sell, each with your own content.
  • Publish buying guides that answer real questions: vitrified vs ceramic tiles, choosing anti-skid bathroom tiles, wall-hung vs floor-mounted WCs.

Hundreds of large product photos can make a catalogue painfully slow on mobile data. Resize and compress images, use modern formats such as WebP and make sure filters respond quickly; see image optimisation for WordPress.

FAQs

What should a website for a tiles showroom include?

A catalogue by room, look and size, product pages with coverage details, authorised brand pages, real installation photos, a showroom visit booking and a separate builder and contractor enquiry page.

Should a tiles showroom sell online?

Large tile orders usually need a visit and site measurements, so most showrooms focus on enquiries. Selling fittings and accessories online can work alongside that.

Can I list every tile brand I can source?

List only brands you are authorised to sell. Mention on-order sourcing separately, and never claim official or exclusive status without written confirmation.

How do I keep a large tile catalogue fast?

Compress and resize images, use WebP, keep heavy visualisers on their own page and use efficient filters.

Should I show tile prices on the website?

Price bands per square foot help filter enquiries if you can keep them current. Otherwise, explain what affects the price and invite a visit.
