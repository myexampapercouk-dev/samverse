Domain Renewal and SEO Scam Emails: How to Spot Fake Expiry Notices, "SEO for Your Domain" Invoices and Cold Audit Emails

How to spot domain renewal and SEO scam emails: fake expiry notices, "domain registration authority" invoices, brand domain warnings and fake SEO audits.

Key takeaways
  • Only the registrar you actually pay can renew your domain; an invoice from any other company is a sales trick or a scam, however official it looks.
  • Look for the giveaways: a company you have never used, urgency, a payment link, small print saying "this is not a bill" and senders on free email addresses.
  • Never click login or payment links in these emails; go to your registrar, host or Google account directly, and if you paid, call your bank and report it on 1930.

Most domain renewal and SEO scam emails follow a pattern: an official-sounding company you've never dealt with warns that your domain is expiring or your site has problems, and asks you to pay through a link. Your domain can only be renewed by the registrar you bought it from, so any invoice from another company is at best an overpriced sales pitch and at worst a scam. Check by logging into your registrar account directly, never through the email.

Below are the most common types aimed at website owners, with the giveaways for each and what to do if you've already clicked or paid. Most of these work because owners aren't sure who their registrar and host are; on my WordPress maintenance plans, domain, hosting and WordPress accounts stay in the client's name, which makes a fake notice much easier to recognise.

Fake domain expiry and renewal notices

These arrive by email and sometimes by post, from a name such as "Domain Registration Authority", "Domain Services" or "Domain Listing Service". They copy your domain name and registration details from public WHOIS records and look like an invoice, often with an expiry date that sounds alarming. Read the small print and you'll usually find it's for a search engine listing or "domain services", and that it isn't a bill. Some versions are worse: paying, or signing a form, transfers your domain to them at a higher renewal price.

How to check a renewal notice is real

  1. Look up your domain on lookup.icann.org, or any WHOIS lookup, to see which registrar it's with and when it expires.
  2. Log into that registrar's dashboard by typing its address yourself, and check the expiry date and auto-renew setting.
  3. Compare the sender's domain with your registrar's real domain. A notice from anyone else isn't a renewal.

Registrars do send genuine reminders before a domain expires, and for .com and similar domains ICANN's rules require them, typically around a month and a week before expiry. They come from the company you pay and point you to your account. If a real reminder has passed and the domain has lapsed, see domain name expired? what to do.

One genuine email that looks like spam: after you register or change a domain's contact details, your registrar may ask you to confirm your email address, and an unconfirmed address can lead to the domain being suspended. It comes from your registrar and doesn't ask for payment.

"SEO for your domain" invoices

A close cousin of the fake renewal: an email titled something like "Domain SEO service expiration" or "Search engine submission for yourbusiness.in", warning that your site will lose visibility unless you pay. There's no such thing as renewing SEO for a domain. Google finds sites by crawling links and sitemaps, and submitting your sitemap in Search Console is free. Paying gets you, at most, a listing on directories nobody visits. If you want to know how a real SEO provider works, and what a fair monthly retainer covers, see SEO red flags and how to choose an SEO provider.

"Someone wants to register your brand" emails

You get an email from a self-styled registrar in Asia saying a company has applied to register your brand name in extensions such as .cn, .asia or .hk, and asking whether you've authorised it. If not, you're invited to register them all yourself, through them, quickly. There's no other applicant. Unless you genuinely trade in those markets, ignore it. If you do, register the domains you need through your usual registrar.

Cold "your website has errors" emails

"I was checking your website and found it's not ranking on page one", "your site has 63 SEO errors", or "your site isn't mobile friendly". These come from free Gmail or Outlook addresses, rarely name a specific page or problem, and end with an offer of a free audit or a cheap monthly package. Automated SEO tools always find "errors", most of them harmless. It isn't always a scam, but it's never a sign of a provider worth hiring. If you want to know whether your site has real problems, check Search Console yourself or ask someone you've chosen.

Phishing: suspension notices, verification and full mailboxes

Emails pretending to be your host, registrar, email provider or WordPress say your account will be suspended, your payment failed or your mailbox is full, and ask you to log in through a button. The page looks real but sits on a look-alike address and captures your password. With your hosting or registrar login, an attacker can redirect your domain, read your email or plant malware on your site.

  • Hover over the button to see the real address, but don't rely on that alone.
  • Never log in from an email link. Open your provider's site from a bookmark or by typing it.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication for your registrar, host, email and WordPress.

Scammers can also send email that pretends to come from your own domain. A DMARC record makes that much harder; see SPF, DKIM and DMARC explained.

Fake copyright and legal notices

A newer trick: an email, or a message through your contact form, claims an image on your site infringes someone's copyright and links to "the evidence". The link downloads malware or leads to a login page. Genuine copyright complaints name the specific image and page and don't require you to download anything. If you're unsure, check the page they mention yourself.

How to protect your business from these emails

  • Keep a list of your registrar, host, email provider and renewal dates, with logins in a password manager; a renewal checklist helps.
  • Turn on auto-renew with your real registrar, and keep the payment card up to date.
  • Make sure the domain is registered to the business, with an email address you'll still have in five years; see regaining control of your website if a former developer holds it.
  • Use the registrar's transfer lock, so nobody can move the domain without the authorisation code.
  • Tell staff who handle accounts payable that domain and SEO "invoices" must be checked before payment.

If you clicked a link or paid

  1. If you paid, call your bank or card issuer straight away. In India, report financial fraud on the 1930 helpline or at cybercrime.gov.in as soon as possible.
  2. If you entered a password, change it immediately from the real site, along with anywhere you reused it, and turn on two-factor authentication.
  3. Check your registrar account for changed contact details, nameservers or a pending transfer, and make sure the transfer lock is on.
  4. Check your hosting and website for new admin users, unfamiliar files or redirects; see signs of a hacked WordPress site.

If the site has been compromised, it needs a proper clean-up rather than just a password change; see hacked site repair.

FAQs

Is "Domain Registration Authority" a scam?

An invoice from a company with a name like that, which isn't the registrar you pay, is not a real renewal. At best it's a paid listing service presented as a bill; at worst it transfers your domain. Check your expiry date in your own registrar account and ignore the email.

How do I know if a domain renewal email is real?

A real one comes from the registrar you bought the domain from and points you to your account there. Look the domain up on lookup.icann.org to confirm the registrar, then log in directly by typing the address. Never pay from a link in the email.

Do I need to pay for search engine registration for my domain?

No. Google, Bing and other search engines find sites for free. Submitting your sitemap in Google Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools costs nothing, and "SEO renewal" invoices are a sales trick.

Someone wants to register my company name as a .cn domain. What should I do?

Usually nothing. These emails invent a rival applicant to pressure you into buying many domains. If you genuinely trade in China or Asia, register the extensions you need through your usual registrar.

Should I reply to emails saying my website has SEO errors?

No need. They're mass-sent templates, and automated tools always find some "errors". If you're worried about your site, look at Search Console or ask a provider you've chosen yourself.
