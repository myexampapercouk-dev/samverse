How to Write Blog Posts That Rank on Google: A Step-by-Step Workflow From Topic to Published Post, With a Template Outline

How to write blog posts that rank on Google, step by step: topic, intent, outline, experience, links, images, publishing and updating, with a template.

Key takeaways
  • Start from a question customers actually search, check what Google already ranks for it, and outline a post that answers it more clearly and more specifically than those pages.
  • Add what only your business knows, such as real examples, local detail and photos of your work, then handle headings, internal links, images and the title before publishing.
  • Publishing is the midpoint: share the post, link to it from related pages, and update it once Search Console shows which searches it appears for.

To write a blog post that ranks on Google, pick a question your customers actually search, look at what Google already ranks for it, and plan a post that answers it better: a direct answer at the top, a section for each follow-up question, and examples only your business can give. Then get the basics right, including the title, headings, internal links and images, publish it, share it, and come back to improve it once Search Console shows which searches it appears for. Most posts that fail skip the first two steps or the last one.

I don't write blog articles as part of my WordPress SEO services, but the audit tells you which posts or answers are missing and what each should cover, and the on-page work makes sure good posts aren't held back by weak titles, missing links or technical problems. This guide is the workflow I'd suggest for writing them yourself.

Step 1: Choose a topic customers actually search

The best topics are the questions customers already ask you. Before an AC repair business in Pune gets a call, people search things like "AC not cooling but fan running" or "how often should a split AC be serviced". Before a CA firm hears from a new client, people search for the documents needed for GST registration. Collect questions from:

  • Sales calls, WhatsApp chats and emails: write down the questions people ask before they buy.
  • Search Console: the Queries tab shows searches you already appear for, often deep in the results, which an article could answer properly.
  • Google itself: autocomplete suggestions and "People also ask" boxes show how people phrase the question.

Prioritise questions close to what you sell. A post that answers "how much does terrace waterproofing cost" is far more likely to bring an enquiry than a general post about monsoon tips. Give each question one post: two posts targeting the same search compete with each other.

Step 2: Check search intent before you write

Search your main phrase in a private window and look at what ranks. Are the top results step-by-step guides, lists, comparisons, product pages, videos or a map of local businesses? Google is showing you the format searchers want. If the results are all service pages from local businesses, a blog post is the wrong format; that search belongs on your service page instead.

Note what the top results cover and what they miss. Your post needs to cover the same ground and add something they don't: a clearer answer, a worked example, a table that makes a decision easier, or experience from actually doing the work.

Step 3: Outline before you write

An outline stops you wandering and makes sure you answer the question in the order readers ask it. This is a template that suits most practical business posts:

  1. Title: the main question or task, with the main phrase near the start.
  2. Opening: a direct answer in two or three sentences, then one line on what the post covers.
  3. First section: the most important sub-question, such as what it is, why it happens or who it's for.
  4. Steps or options: a numbered list or a comparison table.
  5. An example from your own work: what happened, what you did and why.
  6. Costs, timelines or what affects them: only what you can stand behind.
  7. Mistakes to avoid or when to call a professional.
  8. FAQs: three to five questions from People also ask and your own calls, each answered in a few sentences.
  9. Close: one clear next step, linking to the relevant service page.

Each heading should make sense on its own, because many readers scan the headings before deciding to read. Write them as questions or clear statements, not clever phrases.

Step 4: Add experience only you have

Generic advice is everywhere, and AI tools can produce it in seconds. What makes a post worth ranking is what only someone doing the work knows:

  • A real job: "a society in Kothrud called us after three monsoons of leaks; the cause was the drain outlet, not the terrace coating."
  • Local detail: areas, building types, seasons, regulations and suppliers your customers deal with.
  • Opinions and trade-offs: what you'd recommend and when you wouldn't.
  • Photos and screenshots from your own work, not stock images.
  • Mistakes you've seen customers make, and what fixing them involved.

Never invent examples, figures or results to make a post look authoritative. If you use AI to help draft, check every fact and add the experience yourself; using AI tools for website content responsibly covers a safe workflow.

Step 5: Write for people who scan

  • Answer first, then explain. Each section should open with its main point.
  • Short paragraphs of two to four sentences, especially for phone readers.
  • Lists and tables for steps, options and comparisons.
  • Customers' words, not industry jargon; explain a technical term the first time you use it.
  • Specific over vague: "replace the filter every two weeks in summer" beats "maintain your AC regularly", as long as it's accurate.

Step 6: Internal links and images

Link to the relevant service page early in the post, where a reader would naturally want help, and to two or three related posts where the topic comes up. Then do the step most people skip: edit older posts on the same subject and add a link to the new one. A new post with no links pointing to it can wait a long time to be crawled. Topic clusters and pillar pages shows how to organise posts around your services so the links follow a plan.

Use original images where you can, such as photos of jobs, before-and-after shots or annotated screenshots. Compress them, give them descriptive file names and write alt text that describes what the image shows. Image SEO covers formats, alt text and getting images found.

Step 7: On-page basics before you publish

  • Title tag of roughly 50 to 60 characters, starting with the main phrase.
  • Meta description that says what the post offers and gives a reason to click; Google may rewrite it, but a good one often gets used. How to write meta titles and descriptions has examples.
  • Short URL with the main words, such as /blog/ac-not-cooling/, decided before publishing so it never needs to change.
  • One H1, then H2s for sections and H3s within them, in order.
  • Author and date visible, with an honest "updated" date when you revise it.

Your SEO plugin, such as Yoast or Rank Math, handles the Article markup and sitemap once configured. The on-page checklist linked above has the full list of checks.

Step 8: Publish and promote

  • Request indexing for important posts with URL Inspection in Search Console, once the internal links are live.
  • Share it where your customers are: LinkedIn for B2B, WhatsApp status or a broadcast list for local customers, your email newsletter, and a Google Business Profile post where it fits.
  • Use it in sales: when a customer asks the question the post answers, send them the link. It saves you typing and shows expertise.
  • Mention it to partners, such as suppliers or associations, who might genuinely find it useful.

Step 9: Measure and update

After four to eight weeks, open Search Console, filter the Performance report to the post's URL and look at the Queries tab. You'll often find it appearing for questions you didn't answer directly. Add a short section or FAQ for each, tighten the title if impressions are high but clicks low, and add links from any newer posts. Updating is part of writing, not an afterthought; the guide to updating old posts linked above covers when to refresh, merge or leave a post alone.

How long should a blog post be?

As long as it takes to answer the question completely, and no longer. Word count isn't a ranking factor; covering what searchers need is. As a guide:

Common mistakes

  • Writing about what you want to say rather than what customers search
  • Several posts targeting the same search, each weaker than one combined post
  • A long introduction before the answer
  • No link to the service the post relates to, and no links pointing to the post
  • Stock photos and generic advice with nothing from your own experience
  • Publishing and never looking at the post again

FAQs

How long does it take for a blog post to rank?

There's no fixed time. A post on an established site with good internal links may appear for specific searches within weeks; on a new site with few links, it can take months. Competitive topics take longer.

How many blog posts should a small business publish?

Consistency and usefulness matter more than volume. A few genuinely helpful posts a month, each answering a real customer question, beats daily thin posts.

Should every blog post target a keyword?

Every post should answer a question people actually search, which in practice means a main phrase and its variations. Use it naturally in the title, opening and headings, not repeated for its own sake.

Can I use AI to write blog posts that rank?

AI can help with outlines and first drafts, but unedited AI text tends to be generic. Check every fact, add real experience and examples, and take responsibility for what you publish.

Do blog posts help a local business get enquiries?

Yes, when they answer questions customers ask before buying and link clearly to the relevant service page. Posts unrelated to what you sell may bring visits but rarely enquiries.
