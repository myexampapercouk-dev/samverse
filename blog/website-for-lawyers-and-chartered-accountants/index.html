Websites for Lawyers, CAs and Consultants: What Clients Look For, What the Rules Allow and How to Handle Enquiries Confidentially

Websites for law firms and CA practices: practice area pages, verifiable credentials, Bar Council and ICAI limits, confidential enquiries and DPDP basics.

Key takeaways
  • Clients arrive with a specific problem, so give each practice area a page that describes the situations you handle, the process, the documents needed and the limits of your scope.
  • Show credentials clients can verify, such as enrolment or membership numbers, and keep wording within Bar Council or ICAI rules: factual, with no success claims for advocates.
  • Treat every enquiry as confidential from the first click: ask for an outline only, no documents, run conflict checks before discussing details, and collect as little personal data as you need.

People who visit a lawyer's or chartered accountant's website are usually worried about something specific: a tax notice, a property dispute, a family matter, a company to set up or an audit deadline. They want to know within a minute whether you handle their kind of problem, whether you are properly qualified, how a first consultation works and whether it is safe to describe their situation to you. A website that answers those questions factually, within your professional rules, wins the consultation request.

I build websites for lawyers, CAs and consultants with profile and qualification pages, practice area or service pages, a consultation request form, team profiles, an articles section, office location and local SEO. Here I look at it from the client's side, and at how professional rules and confidentiality shape what it can say.

Start from the client's situation

Clients rarely search for "legal services" or "CA firm". They search for their situation, and the site should be organised the same way:

The homepage then becomes a set of clear routes into those pages, rather than a paragraph about the firm's philosophy.

What the rules allow you to say

What you may say is governed by your professional body far more than by design choices. For advocates, the Bar Council of India's rules prohibit soliciting work and advertising, but factual details on a website, such as who you are, how to reach you, your enrolment, your qualifications and the areas you practise in, are permitted; what the Bar Council rules allow covers this in detail. Chartered accountants follow the ICAI Code of Ethics, along with the institute's guidelines on what members' and firms' websites may carry, which also limit promotional content. Read the latest version before you go live, as these rules are revised from time to time. In practice, that usually means:

  • No "best", "top", "leading" or "expert" used to promote yourself
  • No success rates, case wins or promised outcomes
  • For advocates, no client testimonials or review widgets; for CAs, check current ICAI guidance before adding any
  • No discounts, offers or comparisons with other professionals
  • A disclaimer on law firm sites, often shown before visitors enter, and an "information only, not advice" note on articles

Independent consultants who don't belong to a regulated profession have more freedom, but claims must still be genuine and verifiable.

Practice area pages that clients actually read

Each practice area or service page should help a client recognise their situation and understand what happens next. A structure that works for both advocates and CAs:

  1. Who this is for, in the client's words: "You have received a notice under GST" rather than "Indirect tax litigation".
  2. Common situations you handle, described generally, never as client stories without consent.
  3. Your scope: the courts, tribunals or authorities you appear before, the taxes or filings you cover, and what you don't take on.
  4. How the process usually runs, step by step, without promising timelines or outcomes.
  5. Documents to keep ready for a first consultation.
  6. Related articles, and the consultation request button.

Being clear about scope saves everyone time. A client with a criminal matter who lands on a civil litigation practice's site should be able to tell within seconds that they need someone else.

Credentials clients can verify

Clients and their families often check before they call. Make that easy:

  • Advocates: the State Bar Council you are enrolled with, your enrolment number and year, the courts you regularly appear in, qualifications and the chambers or firm you practise from.
  • Chartered accountants: each partner's membership number, the firm registration number and qualifications such as CA, CS or CMA, plus any certification you genuinely hold, such as an ICAI peer review certificate.
  • Everyone: real photos, the team behind the work, languages spoken, publications or talks stated factually, and years of practice only as they really are.

Specific, checkable details build more confidence than adjectives, and they help search engines understand who is behind the advice; E-E-A-T explained covers why that matters.

Consultation requests that respect confidentiality

An enquiry form for a legal or tax matter is different from a sales form, because the visitor may be about to share something sensitive. Design it to protect both sides:

  • Ask for an outline only: name, phone, city, the type of matter and two or three lines on what it concerns, plus a line asking them to hold back confidential details and documents for now.
  • Mention conflict checks. Law firms check whether they already act for the other side before discussing details, so say that you will confirm you can help before asking for more.
  • No file uploads on public forms. Documents come later, through a channel your office controls.
  • Clarity on the first consultation: whether it is in person, by phone or by video, how long it usually lasts, and whether it is charged, stated factually if your rules permit.
  • Booking tools for consultations, if you use one, embedded with slots that match your real availability; online appointment booking compares the options.

Client data and the DPDP Act

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act sets out duties for anyone collecting personal data, and its rules are being phased in, so check the current position with your adviser. For a professional practice's website, the sensible basics are:

  • Collect only what you need to respond, and add a consent line plus a privacy notice explaining what you collect and why; see the DPDP Act and your website.
  • Send form enquiries to an inbox only the right people can read, not a shared address half the office uses.
  • Keep analytics, chat widgets and session recording tools away from form fields, and choose tools that don't store enquiry text with third parties you haven't vetted.
  • Use HTTPS throughout, keep the site updated, and delete enquiry data you no longer need.
  • Use the practice's WhatsApp Business number for client chats, not a partner's personal number.

Knowledge articles that build trust

Articles are one of the few ways professionals can show their knowledge within the rules, as long as they stay educational. Useful formats include process explainers ("what happens after a GST show cause notice"), annual deadline guides, "what to do if" articles and short notes on changes in the law. Each one should carry a date, the author's name and an "information only" line.

Articles age quickly in law and tax. When the Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act from April 2026, for example, articles citing the old section numbers needed reviewing, and due dates and rates change every year. Set a reminder to review your most-read articles annually; updating old blog posts explains how to refresh them without losing traffic.

Getting found by the right clients

Searches usually combine the problem with a place, such as "lawyer for cheque bounce case in Pune" or "CA for ITR filing near me", so practice area pages should use those words naturally in titles and headings. Keep your Google Business Profile consistent with the site, give each office its own page, and remember that for advocates, actively soliciting reviews may conflict with the rules. FAQs

What should a lawyer's or CA's website include?

Profiles with verifiable credentials, a page for each practice area or service, a consultation request form that asks only for an outline, office details with a map, articles, a privacy notice and, for law firms, a disclaimer.

Can a CA firm show client testimonials on its website?

Check the current ICAI guidance first, because it limits promotional content on members' websites. Advocates should leave testimonials out under the Bar Council rules.

How should we handle an enquiry from someone on the other side of a matter?

Keep the form to a brief outline, run your conflict check before discussing details, and say on the form that you will confirm you can act before asking for more information.

Should the website say whether the first consultation is charged?

Clarity helps clients, so state how the first consultation works and whether it is charged, in factual terms, if your professional rules permit it.

How often should we update our articles?

Review the most-read articles at least once a year and whenever the law, rates or deadlines they mention change, and show the date each was last reviewed.
