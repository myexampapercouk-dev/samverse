Bug or Change Request? How to Tell the Difference, Report Website Issues Well and What Post-Launch Support Covers

Bug vs change request: how to tell them apart, what post-launch support and warranty usually cover, and how to report website issues so they get fixed fast.

Key takeaways
  • A bug is when the site does not do what was agreed; a change request is when you now want it to do something different, and the signed scope settles which is which.
  • A good bug report gives the page link, steps, expected and actual result, device and browser, and a screenshot or screen recording.
  • Agree warranty length, urgency levels and response times in writing before launch so grey-area issues are settled by the contract rather than by argument.

A website bug is something that does not work as agreed in the project scope, such as a form that does not send or a layout that breaks on a supported phone; a change request is anything new or different from what was agreed, such as a new page, a redesigned section or a different workflow. Bugs found during the warranty period are normally fixed at no charge, while change requests are quoted separately, so the signed scope and approved designs decide which is which.

This article gives clear examples, the grey areas that cause most disputes, how support periods usually work, and templates for reporting a bug or requesting a change so both get handled quickly.

Bug vs change request: the definitions

A bug is a defect: the site behaves differently from the agreed specification, the approved design, or the normal expected behaviour of a working website. Examples are errors, broken links, features that fail, and layouts that break on browsers and devices the project said it would support.

A change request is a request to alter something that works as agreed: new features, new pages, different copy or images, a changed design, or a new integration. It may be small or large, but it is new work.

The test is simple: "Did we agree this, and does it fail to do it?" If yes, it is a bug. If the site does what was agreed but you now want something else, it is a change.

The grey areas

Most disagreements come from the middle ground. Common ones and a fair way to handle them:

  • Third-party updates: a plugin or WordPress update after launch breaks something. Within the warranty, many developers fix it if the update happened under their care; after warranty it falls under maintenance. Our guide to plugin conflicts explains why updates cause these issues.
  • Client edits: a layout breaks after the client or another developer edited a page. That is usually paid work, though often quick.
  • "That's not what I imagined": if it matches the approved design, it is a change. If no design was approved, the scope document's description decides.
  • Hosting and email problems: if the host or email provider is at fault, the developer can help diagnose, but the fix sits with the provider.
  • Content added later: long titles or large images added by the client break a design that worked with the original content. A robust design should cope with reasonable variation, so this deserves discussion.

The easiest way to avoid grey areas is a detailed brief and scope at the start. Our website brief template lists what to agree up front.

How post-launch support and warranty periods usually work

Most developers and agencies include a bug-fix warranty after launch, commonly a few weeks to a few months. Typically it covers defects in the work delivered, not new requests, content updates or problems caused by later changes. Read your contract for:

  • The warranty length and when it starts (launch date or sign-off date).
  • What counts as a defect and which browsers and devices are supported.
  • Response and fix times, and whether they apply on weekends and holidays.
  • What happens after the warranty: a monthly maintenance plan, an hourly rate or a block of hours.

If the contract says nothing about this, agree it in writing now. It protects both sides.

How to report a bug so it gets fixed quickly

A developer cannot fix what they cannot reproduce. "The site is broken" can take an hour of back-and-forth; a clear report can be fixed in minutes. Include:

  1. Page link: the full URL where it happens.
  2. Steps: what you clicked or entered, in order.
  3. Expected result: what should have happened.
  4. Actual result: what happened instead, including any error message word for word.
  5. Device and browser: for example "Samsung phone, Chrome" or "Windows laptop, Edge".
  6. When: date and time, which helps match server logs.
  7. Proof: a screenshot or a short screen recording. Phones can record the screen in a few taps.
  8. Impact: is it stopping sales or enquiries, or is it cosmetic?

Before reporting, try a quick refresh with the cache cleared or a private window. Many "the change isn't showing" issues are caching; see WordPress changes not showing.

How to request a change so it's quoted accurately

For change requests, describe the goal rather than only the solution. "We want more enquiries from the services page" lets a developer suggest options; "add a red button" may not solve the real problem. Include:

  • What you want and why (the business reason).
  • Where on the site, with links or screenshots marked up.
  • Any content, images or examples you like.
  • Your deadline and whether it is fixed (a launch, an ad campaign) or flexible.

Ask for a written quote and timeline before work starts, and group small changes together so they can be done in one batch. Batching usually costs less than many separate tiny requests.

Keep a simple change log

Keep one shared sheet or task board listing each issue: date reported, type (bug or change), urgency, status and outcome. It avoids repeated requests, shows what was done for invoices, and helps the next developer if you ever switch. Agencies can extend this into a ticket system, as described in our notes on agency WordPress care plans.

What to do when you disagree

Go back to the documents: the signed scope, approved designs and any written messages. If the scope covers it, it is a bug. If it is genuinely unclear, a fair outcome is often to split the difference or treat it as a small change at a reduced rate. Keep it calm and written; the relationship is usually worth more than one small task. Two more categories: content updates and external issues

Not everything is either a bug or a change. Two other categories are worth naming in your agreement:

  • Content updates: changing prices, timings, team photos or blog posts on a working site. These are routine tasks, usually covered by a maintenance plan's monthly hours or done by your own team after training.
  • External issues: problems caused outside the website itself, such as an expired domain, a hosting outage, a payment gateway changing its API, or Google changing how a feature works. The developer can diagnose and help, but the cause and often the fix sit with someone else.

Why things break when nobody touched the site

Clients often say nothing changed, but it broke. Something usually did change, just not on the site's own pages:

  • Browser and phone updates: a new Safari or Chrome version handles some code differently.
  • Hosting changes: the host upgrades PHP or changes server settings; see updating the PHP version.
  • Automatic updates: WordPress or plugins updated themselves overnight.
  • Third-party services: a map, chat widget, payment or email service changed on its side.

None of these makes the original work defective, which is why most warranties exclude them and maintenance plans exist to cover them.

One issue per report

Send each problem as a separate message or ticket, even if you notice five at once. A combined list of mixed bugs, changes and questions gets partly fixed and partly forgotten, and it is hard to tell which items are free and which need a quote. Give each one a short clear title, such as "Contact form not sending from Pune branch page".

Use a staging site for testing fixes and changes

For anything beyond a tiny fix, ask for changes to be made and checked on a staging copy before going live. You review the result there, approve it, and only then is it pushed to the real site. This avoids new bugs appearing on the live site during trading hours. See staging sites explained.

FAQs

What is the difference between a bug and a change request?

A bug is when the website does not work as agreed in the scope or approved design. A change request is when it works as agreed but you want something new or different. Bugs in warranty are usually free; changes are quoted.

How long should a website warranty last?

It varies by developer and project size, commonly from a few weeks to a few months after launch. What matters most is that the length, start date and coverage are written in the contract.

Is a problem caused by a plugin update a bug?

It depends on the agreement. During warranty, many developers fix issues from updates they made. After the warranty, or if someone else updated the site, it usually falls under paid maintenance.

How do I report a website bug?

Send the page link, the steps you took, what you expected, what happened, your device and browser, the time, and a screenshot or screen recording. Say how much it affects sales or enquiries.

Are content updates covered by post-launch support?

Usually not under a bug warranty, because they are new work. Many maintenance plans include a set amount of content update time each month.

Who pays when a client edit breaks the site?

Normally the client, as it is not a defect in the delivered work. Good developers fix these quickly and may suggest locking layouts or training to prevent repeats.
