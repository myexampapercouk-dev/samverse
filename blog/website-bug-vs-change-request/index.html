Bug or Change Request? How to Tell the Difference, Report Website Issues Well and What Post-Launch Support Covers

Key takeaways
  • A bug is something that does not work as agreed or as it reasonably should; a change request is anything new or different from the agreed scope.
  • Bugs in the original work are normally fixed without charge during the support period set out in your agreement; changes are quoted or covered by a maintenance plan's allowance.
  • Report one issue at a time with the page URL, steps to reproduce, what you expected, your device and browser, and a screenshot or screen recording.

A bug is something that doesn't work as agreed, or as it reasonably should: a contact form that doesn't send, a layout that breaks on certain phones, or a feature in the agreed scope that fails. A change request is anything new or different from what was agreed: a new page, a redesigned section, a new feature or different behaviour. Bugs in the original work are normally fixed without charge during the post-launch support period in your agreement, while changes are quoted separately or covered by a maintenance plan's allowance.

Most disagreements after launch come from the space between those two definitions, and a written scope settles most of them. Below are clear examples, the common grey areas, how support periods usually work and how to report an issue so it gets fixed quickly. Bug vs change request: the definitions

  • Bug (or defect): the site doesn't do what was agreed in the scope or approved design, or fails in a way any reasonable person would call broken, such as errors, missing emails or pages that don't load.
  • Change request: you'd like the site to do something it was never agreed to do, or to look or work differently from what was approved. It may be a great idea, but it's new work.
  • Content update: changing words, images, prices or details within the existing design. Usually small, and often covered by a maintenance plan, but not a bug.
  • External issue: something outside the original work changed, such as a plugin update, a hosting change or a third-party service. Who fixes it, and on what terms, depends on your agreement.

The grey areas

  • Plugin and WordPress updates. Software keeps changing after launch. A layout that worked at handover can break after an update six months later. That's not a defect in the original work, but it does need fixing, which is exactly what a maintenance plan is for. Plugin conflicts explains why updates can break things.
  • Browser and phone updates. New versions of browsers occasionally change how something displays. Whether that's covered depends on the support terms and how long ago launch was.
  • Changes made by someone else. If you, a staff member or another developer edited code, settings or plugins, problems that follow are usually treated as new work.
  • Hosting changes. A host upgrading PHP or moving servers can break older code. Again, not a defect in the build, but something to fix.
  • Unstated expectations. "I assumed it would send me a WhatsApp alert too" is a change if it was never in the scope, however obvious it felt.
  • Third-party services. Payment gateways, map services and APIs change their rules or need billing set up. Often the fix is an account setting on your side.

When it's unclear, go back to the written scope, the approved designs and what was signed off on staging. A short, friendly conversation sorts out most of these.

How post-launch support and warranty periods usually work

Many developers and agencies include a period after launch during which bugs in the delivered work are fixed without charge. The length varies a lot between contracts, so check yours rather than assuming. Typically, that support:

  • Covers defects in what was built and agreed
  • Doesn't cover new features, design changes or new content
  • Doesn't cover problems caused by later edits, plugin installs, updates or hosting changes made by others
  • Often includes help with using the dashboard and editing content

After that period, fixes are usually billed, or covered by a maintenance plan. The terms belong in the contract before work starts; the website design contract checklist lists what to agree, and the website handover checklist covers support terms at the end of the project.

How to report a bug so it gets fixed quickly

The most common delay in fixing a bug is not being able to see it. A good report lets the developer reproduce the problem on the first try:

  1. One issue per report. A list of twelve problems in one email is easy to half-finish.
  2. The exact page URL, copied from the address bar.
  3. Steps to reproduce: what you clicked or typed, in order.
  4. What you expected to happen, and what actually happened, including any error message word for word.
  5. Device, operating system and browser, for example "Samsung phone, Android, Chrome" or "Windows laptop, Edge".
  6. A screenshot or screen recording. Phones have built-in screen recording, and a ten-second video often explains more than a paragraph.
  7. When it happened, and whether it happens every time or only sometimes.
  8. How urgent it is: is it stopping enquiries or sales?

A simple template you can copy:

Page: https://yourdomain.com/contact/
Steps: Filled in the form, chose "Service: Repairs", clicked Send
Expected: Thank-you message and an email to the office
Actual: Thank-you message shown, but no email arrived
Device: Android phone, Chrome. Also tried on a Windows laptop, same result
When: Every time since Monday
Urgency: High, we may be missing enquiries
Screenshot: attached

How to request a change so it's quoted accurately

  • Explain the goal, not just the solution: "more people should call from the service page" leaves room for a better idea than "make the button bigger".
  • Say which pages are affected, and share examples you like from other sites.
  • Have the content ready, such as text, photos and prices, so the quote doesn't need to allow for chasing it.
  • Mention any deadline, such as a festival offer or a trade show.
  • Group small changes into one request where you can.

Whether a change is quoted as a fixed price or by the hour depends on its size and how clear it is; see fixed price vs hourly website projects.

Keep a simple change log

A shared spreadsheet with the date, the request, whether it was a bug, change or content update, its status and any notes is enough. It shows what was fixed and what was new work, helps with budgeting, and makes the next problem easier to trace, because the first question is always "what changed recently?"

FAQs

Are website bugs fixed for free?

Bugs in the original work are normally fixed without charge during the post-launch support period in your agreement. After that, or for problems caused by later changes, updates or other people's edits, fixes are usually billed or covered by a maintenance plan.

How long is a website warranty or support period?

It varies by developer and contract, so there's no standard length. Check your agreement for how long bug fixes are covered and what's excluded, and agree it in writing before the project starts.

Is it a bug if a plugin update breaks my site?

Not a bug in the original build, because the software changed after launch. It still needs fixing, and handling updates safely, with a backup first and checks afterwards, is the core of a maintenance plan.

Is updating content a change request?

It's usually treated as a content update rather than a bug or a new feature. Small updates are often included in a maintenance plan's monthly allowance; larger content projects are quoted separately.

What should a good bug report include?

The page URL, the steps to reproduce the problem, what you expected and what happened, your device and browser, a screenshot or screen recording, and how urgent it is.
