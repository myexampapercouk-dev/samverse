Websites for Music and Dance Academies: Courses by Instrument and Style, Age Groups, Batch Timings, Trial Classes, Grade Exams, Recitals and Teacher Profiles

Plan a music or dance academy website: courses by instrument or style, age groups, batch timings, trial class booking, teachers, recitals and fees.

Key takeaways
  • Give each instrument, vocal style or dance form its own course page with age groups, levels, batch timings and what a beginner learns in the first months.
  • Make the trial class the main goal of the site, with a short form that asks for the student, the course and preferred days rather than just a phone number.
  • Mention grade exams only if you genuinely prepare students for them, film recitals with parental consent, and introduce every teacher by training and teaching style.

A music or dance academy website has one main job: get a parent or adult learner to book a trial class. To do that it needs a page for each course, honest information on age groups and batch timings, teachers worth meeting, and a short trial booking form. What parents look for on a school or coaching website applies here too. Grade exams, recitals and fees all support that decision, so present them carefully.

The structure follows the education websites I build for schools and coaching institutes: courses and batches pages, an enquiry form, faculty profiles, a notice board for holidays and events, and a photo and video gallery. An academy is more personal than a coaching centre, though. Parents are choosing the person who will sit beside their child every week, and adults are often nervous about starting late, so warmth and clarity matter more than a list of achievements.

For admission season or a summer workshop, a dedicated campaign landing page about that one offer, with the trial form on it, will usually outperform sending ad clicks to the homepage.

Decide what the site is organised by

Academies teach very different combinations, and the menu should reflect how your students choose. Three patterns work:

  • By art form, when you teach several: Music (keyboard, guitar, Hindustani vocal, tabla) and Dance (Bharatanatyam, Kathak, contemporary, Bollywood). This is the most common and the easiest to search.
  • By learner, when your audience is split: Kids, Teens, Adults. Useful if adult beginners are a big part of your business and need reassurance of their own.
  • By goal, when you run distinct tracks: hobby classes, exam preparation, performance training and wedding choreography.

Whichever you choose, every course still needs its own page. Someone searching for "Kathak classes for kids" in your area should land on a page about exactly that, not a combined page that mentions Kathak once.

The course page, section by section

A course page answers the questions that otherwise arrive one by one on WhatsApp. Plan each one with these sections:

Batch timings that stay current

An outdated timetable costs enquiries. A parent who sees a Saturday 10 am Kathak batch, enquires, and is told it closed last term may not ask again. Keep timings in one place that staff can edit, show "batch full" or "waitlist open" honestly, and add a line under each timetable saying when it was last updated. Holidays, exam weeks and festival breaks belong on a notice page parents can check without calling.

If you have branches, give each one its own page with its batches, address and map, and connect each to its own Google Business Profile.

The trial class is the conversion that matters

Very few families enrol straight from a website. They book a trial, meet the teacher and decide. Make that step as easy as possible:

  1. Say what the trial is: its length, whether it is free or paid, whether parents can watch, and whether it is a real batch or a separate demo.
  2. Keep the form short: student name, age, course of interest, any previous training, preferred days, parent name and mobile number.
  3. After submission, tell the family who will get in touch and roughly how soon.
  4. Confirm the slot on WhatsApp with the address, a map link and what to wear or bring.
  5. After the trial, send a short message with batch options and the enrolment link.

Online fee payment after the trial saves an awkward conversation at the front desk. Taking online payments on WordPress explains gateways, UPI and fees.

Grade exams: describe what you prepare for, not an affiliation you lack

Many families look for exam-based progress, whether a graded music exam from an international board or a certificate from an Indian music or dance institution. If your academy prepares students for such exams, say which exams and which levels, and explain how preparation fits into normal classes.

Be careful with wording. Preparing students for an exam is not the same as being an approved centre or an affiliated institute. Use a board's name and logo only as its rules allow, mention a centre registration only if you hold one, and do not publish pass rates or distinctions as percentages. A short line such as "Students may sit graded exams when their teacher feels they are ready" is honest and reassuring. If you do not offer exams at all, there is no need to mention them; many students learn purely for enjoyment, and you can say so.

Teachers are often the reason families choose you

A teacher profile does more than a paragraph of brand copy. For each teacher include:

  • A real photo, ideally while teaching
  • Training: their guru or gharana for classical forms, institution or diploma for others, stated as it actually is
  • Performance experience, described factually
  • The ages and levels they teach, and the languages they teach in
  • A short note in their own voice on how they teach beginners

Avoid "world-renowned" or "best in the city". Parents notice the difference between a description and a boast, and a clear, specific profile reads as more confident.

Recitals, video and children on camera

Nothing sells a dance academy like a clip from the annual showcase, and nothing sells a music school like a student playing a piece they have just learned. Video belongs on the site, but it needs care:

  • Get written consent from a parent before filming or publishing a minor, and record what the consent covers: the website, social media, ads
  • Avoid publishing children's full names, schools or anything that identifies where they can be found
  • Host videos on YouTube or Vimeo and embed them, so the page stays fast; using video on a business website explains why
  • Keep clips short on the course pages and put full recitals on a separate gallery page
  • Remove a video promptly if a family asks

A page for upcoming events, with the date, venue and how families can attend, also tells prospective parents that students actually perform.

Fees on request, without being evasive

Some academies publish fees, others prefer to discuss them after the trial because they vary by level, teacher and batch type. Either can work. If you keep figures off the site, still explain the structure: monthly or term fees, any registration or annual charge, costume and exam fees, and what happens to missed classes. Parents who know the shape of the cost are far more likely to book a trial than those who suspect a hidden bill. The same applies to refund and break policies during summer holidays.

Content that brings students in

Short articles answer the questions behind local searches: the right age to start a classical dance form, keyboard or guitar for a first instrument, how much daily practice a beginner needs, what to expect from a first year of vocal training. They are also easy for your teachers to help write. If your teachers also choreograph for weddings and sangeets, that work deserves separate pages; websites for wedding choreographers covers how it is usually presented. For academies that also run art, abacus or holiday camps, websites for kids' activity classes is the closer guide.

Sports coaching has similar needs; see websites for sports academies.

FAQs

Should a music academy website show batch timings?

Yes, and keep them current. Timing is one of the first things parents check, and showing which batches are full or open saves both sides a phone call.

Can we mention exam boards on our website?

Mention an exam only if you genuinely prepare students for it, and use the board's name and logo only as its rules allow. Do not suggest an affiliation or centre status you do not hold.

Is it safe to post videos of child students?

Only with written parental consent covering where the video will appear. Avoid full names and identifying details, and remove a video quickly if a family asks.

Should we offer online classes on the same website?

Yes, if you teach online. Mark which courses run online, explain the setup a student needs, and give online batches their own timings and trial booking.

Do we need a separate page for each dance form or instrument?

Yes. Each one has different students, ages and questions, and a separate page is far more likely to appear when someone searches for that class in your area.

An academy site built this way spends less time listing everything you teach and more time getting families through the door for a trial.
