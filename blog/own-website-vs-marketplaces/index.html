Own Website vs Amazon, Flipkart and Other Marketplaces: Where Should You Sell?

Own website vs marketplaces for Indian sellers: compare control, fees, customer data, brand and SEO, and how a hybrid strategy lets you use both well.

Key takeaways
  • Large marketplaces bring ready buyers but take fees on every sale, set the rules and keep most of the customer relationship.
  • Your own store gives you margin, customer data, brand control and search visibility, but you have to bring the traffic and run payments, shipping and support.
  • Many brands use both: marketplaces for discovery and volume, their own site for the full range, bundles and repeat customers, with stock kept in sync.

Sell on large marketplaces when you need buyers quickly, compete mainly on price, or are testing a new product; build your own online store when you have branded or niche products, want repeat customers, and want to keep more of each sale and the customer relationship. For most growing brands the answer is both: marketplaces for discovery and volume, and your own store for margin, loyalty and the full range, with stock and pricing managed across the two.

Indian sellers can reach very large numbers of buyers through marketplaces, so why build your own store at all? The answer depends on your products, margins and long-term plans. Selling directly on your own website means no marketplace commission on each order, and that is the case I make for WooCommerce development; but a store only pays off if you can bring shoppers to it, so this guide compares the two honestly.

What large marketplaces do well

  • Ready demand. Shoppers already trust the platform and search it directly.
  • Logistics and payments handled or simplified, including returns pickup in many cases.
  • Fast to start, with no website to build or promote.
  • A good testing ground for new products and price points.

The trade-offs are real, though:

  • Fees on every order reduce your margin, and marketplace advertising adds to them.
  • Price competition is intense, sometimes from sellers listed on the same page as you.
  • Branding and storytelling are limited to the listing format.
  • You don't own the customer relationship, and rules on contacting buyers are strict.
  • Policy, fee or algorithm changes can affect your sales overnight, and account suspensions happen.

What your own store does well

  • Better margin per order, with no commission taken.
  • Full control over pricing, bundles, offers, policies and the checkout.
  • Customer data, with consent, for repeat orders through email and WhatsApp.
  • A brand asset you own, with content that can rank in Google for years.
  • Room for things marketplaces don't allow, such as subscriptions, dealer pricing, gift cards or product customisation.

And its challenges:

  • You must bring your own traffic, and that takes money, time or both.
  • Building trust as a new brand takes reviews, clear policies and a professional checkout.
  • You handle payment gateway approval, shipping, COD rules, returns and support.
  • The store needs looking after: updates, backups and security.

Comparing the real costs

Fees vary by category and change often, so compare them for your own products rather than relying on general figures. For each channel, work out what you actually keep from a typical order:

  • On a marketplace: the sale price minus commission or referral fees, fixed or closing fees, shipping and fulfilment charges, the cost of returns under the marketplace's policy, and any money spent on marketplace ads.
  • On your own store: the sale price minus gateway charges, shipping, packaging, COD and RTO costs, and a share of your hosting, plugins, maintenance and marketing.

The marketing line is the one owners underestimate. A store with no traffic costs money every month and sells nothing. WooCommerce store running costs lists every ongoing cost so you can build the sheet.

A hybrid strategy that works

  1. Use marketplaces for discovery and volume on your best-known products.
  2. Make your own store the home of the full range, with bundles, gift sets, new launches and subscriptions that make it worth visiting.
  3. Make sure your brand name leads to your store. Shoppers who meet you on a marketplace often search your brand on Google next; ranking for your business name covers how to be the first result.
  4. Build a direct relationship the right way. Collect email and WhatsApp consent from customers who buy on your own site, and use them for repeat orders. Marketplace agreements usually restrict contacting their buyers or promoting other channels in parcels, so read yours before adding inserts or messages.
  5. Set a clear pricing policy across channels, and check your marketplace agreements for any pricing or branding rules.
  6. Invest in search for your own store through product, category and buying-guide content; the WooCommerce SEO guide shows where to start.

Running both without chaos

  • Stock: sync inventory across channels, or keep a buffer, so you never sell what you don't have; see WooCommerce inventory management.
  • Product data: keep one master spreadsheet of SKUs, prices and descriptions, and adapt it for each channel.
  • Orders and dispatch: one daily routine covering every channel, so marketplace and website orders don't compete for the same stock.
  • Accounts and GST: marketplaces generally deduct tax and fees before paying out and provide their own reports, so give your CA both channels' reports every month.

Adding your own store when you already sell on marketplaces

If marketplaces bring most of your orders today, you don't have to choose. A sensible way to start:

  1. Launch with your best sellers, not the whole catalogue, with your own photos and descriptions rather than the listing text.
  2. Write your policies first: shipping, returns, refunds, privacy and terms. Payment gateways usually check for them before activating your account.
  3. Apply for a payment gateway early, in your business name, because KYC approval is in the provider's hands.
  4. Decide your COD rules and shipping zones, so the checkout matches what you can deliver.
  5. Tell the people who already know you: Instagram followers, WhatsApp contacts and customers who have opted in.
  6. Give them a reason to buy direct, such as bundles, early access to new launches or products you don't list elsewhere.
  7. Review after a few months: orders, repeat customers and cost per order on each channel, then decide where to put more effort.

When your own store isn't worth it yet: if you have no budget or time to bring traffic, sell only commodity products where price is the only factor, or can't yet handle payments, shipping and support yourself. In that case a simple brand website with links to where you sell may be the better first step.

FAQs

Is it better to sell on marketplaces or my own website?

It depends on your products and goals. Marketplaces suit fast sales of known or price-led products; your own store suits branded products, repeat customers and better margins. Many brands use both.

Can I sell on marketplaces and my own website at the same time?

Yes, and many brands do. Keep stock in sync, set a clear pricing policy across channels, and check each marketplace agreement for rules on pricing, branding and contacting buyers.

How do I get traffic to my own online store?

Through a mix of Google search, ads, Instagram, WhatsApp, email and word of mouth. Search takes months to build but keeps working; ads and social bring faster but paid or effortful traffic.

Can I move my marketplace listings to my own website?

Product details can usually be exported and imported, but marketplace listings normally need reworking into proper product pages, with your own descriptions, photos and specifications.

Is my own store cheaper than paying marketplace fees?

Per order it usually is, because there's no commission. Overall it depends on how much you spend bringing shoppers to the store, so compare what you keep per order on each channel.
