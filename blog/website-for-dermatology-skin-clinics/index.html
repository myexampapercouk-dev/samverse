Websites for Dermatology and Skin Clinics: Concern-Led Pages, Medical and Cosmetic Treatments, Before-and-After Consent and Local Search

Plan a dermatology or skin clinic website: concern-led pages, medical and cosmetic treatments, before-and-after photo consent, booking and local SEO.

Key takeaways
  • Patients search by concern, such as hair fall or dark patches, so map each concern to condition pages and then to the treatments a dermatologist may suggest.
  • Give every procedure a plain fact panel covering who performs it, sessions, downtime, aftercare and who it does not suit, before any persuasion.
  • Publish before-and-after photos only with written consent, consistent lighting and honest captions, after checking current medical advertising and ad platform rules.

A skin clinic website has two kinds of visitor: people with a medical problem, such as eczema or a rash that will not settle, and people looking for a cosmetic change, such as laser hair reduction or help with pigmentation. Both arrive with a concern rather than a treatment name, and both want to know a qualified dermatologist is in charge. The site that gets the consultation explains concerns in plain language, sets out each procedure honestly and handles photos with consent.

Skin clinics also tend to advertise single treatments, so campaign pages built through landing page design often sit beside the main site.

Map concerns to conditions, then to treatments

Nobody searches for "post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation". They search for "dark marks after pimples". Plan the site from the words patients use, link each concern to the condition pages behind it, and link those to the treatments a dermatologist may consider:

Concern people searchCondition pagesTreatment pages linked from them
Pimples and marksAcne, acne scars, marks left after acneMedical acne treatment, chemical peels, scar procedures
Dark patches on the faceMelasma, pigmentation after inflammation, sun damagePrescription creams, peels, laser where suitable
Hair fall or thinningPattern hair loss, stress-related shedding, patchy hair lossMedical treatment, PRP, hair transplant consultation
Itchy rashEczema, fungal infections, hivesMedical treatment, patch testing
White patchesVitiligoMedical treatment, phototherapy if offered
Unwanted hairExcess facial or body hair, and its possible medical causesLaser hair reduction

List only treatments your clinic actually provides, and handle "where suitable" with care: the doctor decides after an examination, and the page should say so. The last row matters too. Excess facial hair can have a hormonal cause, and a laser page that suggests getting that checked shows medical judgement a salon cannot offer.

Medical dermatology deserves more than one line

Many skin clinic sites mention "eczema, psoriasis, infections" in a footer and give every other page to cosmetic procedures. That ignores a large share of patients and searches. Chronic conditions such as psoriasis, eczema, vitiligo and recurring fungal infections bring patients back for years, and these pages are where a dermatologist's training shows most. A useful condition page covers:

  • What the condition looks like, with a consented clinical photo or a careful illustration, and what it is often mistaken for
  • Common triggers and what makes it worse, in plain terms
  • How it is diagnosed, including tests such as a skin scraping or patch test
  • Treatment approaches in general terms, and that long-term conditions are managed over time rather than fixed in one visit
  • Signs that need a doctor urgently
  • What to bring to the first visit, such as creams and medicines already tried

Avoid "permanent cure" language for chronic conditions. Patients with psoriasis or vitiligo have usually heard it before, and it costs you their trust.

Procedure pages: a fact panel before the persuasion

Cosmetic procedure pages tend to open with a glowing model and a string of adjectives. Open with facts instead, as a short panel at the top of each procedure page:

  1. Who performs it: the dermatologist, or trained staff under the dermatologist's supervision, stated accurately
  2. Who it suits and who it does not: skin types, pregnancy, certain medicines or conditions, as your doctors advise
  3. Sessions: a range, with a note that it varies by person and area
  4. Downtime: the redness, peeling or swelling to expect, and for how long, in your doctors' words
  5. Possible side effects: stated plainly, including the less common ones
  6. Aftercare: sun protection, products to pause, when to come back
  7. Cost: a starting price per session or package, or how the cost is worked out

Then explain how the procedure works and what a session feels like. The Dr. Sunaina Dental Care site I built for a dental clinic in Agra answers one worry per treatment in a single sentence, and its FAQ asks "Is the treatment painful?" outright. Skin patients carry similar worries about pain, scarring and whether people will notice, and a procedure page should answer them just as directly.

Before-and-after photos: consent, consistency and context

Comparisons persuade, and they are also where a skin clinic website can mislead or cause harm. Professional conduct guidance for doctors and advertising rules both bear on how results are shown, and the guidance has been revised in recent years, so check the National Medical Commission's current position and your ad platforms' policies before publishing any. If you do show results, a written process protects patients and the clinic:

  1. Take written consent that names where the photo will appear (website, social media, ads) and lets the patient withdraw it later
  2. Use the same camera position, lighting and background before and after, with no filters or retouching
  3. Crop out faces and identifying marks unless the patient agrees to be recognisable
  4. Caption each pair with the treatment, the number of sessions and the time between the photos
  5. State clearly that results differ from person to person
  6. Take photos down promptly when consent is withdrawn

Never present images from manufacturers, other clinics or stock libraries as your own patients. Large galleries also slow pages on phones, so compress every image; image optimisation for WordPress explains how.

Show clearly who is in charge

Skin clinics compete with salons that also offer peels and laser, so patients want proof they are seeing a dermatologist. Put the dermatologist's name, degrees with university and year, and registration number on the homepage and on every procedure page, not only the About page. If other doctors or trained therapists carry out procedures, name their roles honestly, and keep "skin specialist" for qualified dermatologists. If you run a salon or spa alongside the clinic, give it its own pages and tone; websites for salons and spas covers that kind of business.

Selling skincare products: proceed carefully

Many clinics sell sunscreens, cleansers and moisturisers, and some want to sell them online. Keep three lines clear:

  • Prescription medicines do not belong in a simple online cart; selling medicines online is regulated, and the rules have been in flux
  • Cosmetics and over-the-counter products can be sold online if they are properly labelled and your business is set up for it; check the details with your CA and suppliers
  • Product claims should match what the manufacturer can support; "recommended by our dermatologists" is fine if true, "removes pigmentation" is not

If the products grow into a brand of their own, websites for beauty and cosmetics brands covers what that involves.

Bookings and campaign pages

Let patients choose the type of visit when they book: a medical consultation, a cosmetic consultation or a follow-up. Keep the form to name, phone, visit type and preferred time; skin photos and medical history belong in the consultation, not an open form. A WhatsApp button suits people who want to ask about a treatment before committing.

For ads, build a focused page for each treatment you promote, such as laser hair reduction, carrying the fact panel, the doctor's details, the price basis and a booking form. Google and Meta both have policies on advertising health and cosmetic procedures, so check them before a campaign goes live.

Local search for a skin clinic

Most searches are local: "dermatologist near me", "skin clinic in Aundh", "laser hair removal in Viman Nagar". A Google Business Profile with the right primary category, real clinic photos and accurate hours, linked to the site, does much of the work. Give each condition and procedure its own page with headings in patients' words, and if you have several branches, give each one a page with its own doctors and timings. FAQs

Can a dermatology clinic show before-and-after photos?

Only with written consent, consistent lighting, no editing and honest captions, and after checking the current professional guidance for doctors and your ad platforms' rules.

Should a skin clinic show treatment prices?

Starting prices per session or package, with what is included, reduce hesitation and repeat calls. Explain what changes the price, such as the area treated or the number of sessions.

Should medical and cosmetic dermatology have separate websites?

Usually not. One site with clear sections for skin conditions and cosmetic procedures, led by the same dermatologist, builds more trust than two thin ones.

Can patients send photos of their skin through the website?

Better not through an open form. Skin photos are health data, so ask patients to show the problem at the consultation or to use the channel your clinic already uses for patient records.

Do we need a landing page for laser hair reduction ads?

If you run ads for it, yes. A focused page with the procedure facts, the price basis and a booking form gives ad visitors what they clicked for, instead of leaving them to search the homepage.
