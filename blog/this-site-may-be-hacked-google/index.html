"This Site May Be Hacked" in Google Results: What It Means and How to Remove It

What Google's "This site may be hacked" label means, how to confirm it in Search Console, clean up your WordPress site, request a review and stop it returning.

Key takeaways
  • The label means Google believes a third party changed pages on your site or added spam, so confirm it in Search Console's Security issues report and check for rogue owners and spam sitemaps.
  • Clean the site completely, including backdoors and the entry point, make spam URLs return 404 or 410 (never block them in robots.txt), then request a review that explains what you found and fixed.
  • Reviews and recrawling take time, often longer for spam hacks, so focus on prevention afterwards: updates, no nulled software, two-factor login, a firewall, off-site backups and Search Console alerts.

Seeing "This site may be hacked" under your website in Google's search results is alarming, and it makes searchers far less likely to click. Google shows this label when it believes a third party may have changed pages on your site or added new spam pages, often in other languages or stuffed with pharmacy, gambling or counterfeit-goods keywords.

The short answer on removing it: confirm the problem in Google Search Console's Security issues report, lock out the attacker, clean the site completely and close the way they got in, then request a review. Once Google confirms the site is clean, the label is removed from results. The steps below cover each stage, how long it can take and how to stop it happening again.

What the warning means

  • Google has detected hacked content: spam pages, injected links, changed titles or redirects that someone other than you added
  • It affects searchers, not just you: the label appears in search results for anyone who sees your listing
  • Your site may look normal to you: many spam hacks hide their content from logged-in owners and show it only to Google or to visitors arriving from search
  • It won't go away by itself quickly: the hack needs cleaning, and Google needs to see the clean site

It is different from other warnings you might see:

WarningWhere it appearsUsually means
"This site may be hacked"Under your listing in Google search resultsSpam content, links or redirects added by a hacker
"Deceptive site ahead" or "Dangerous site"A full-page red warning in the browserPhishing pages or malware; see fixing browser warnings
Hosting account suspendedYour site is offline or shows a host noticeThe host found malware or spam sending on its server

All of them mean the site needs cleaning, but the search result label most often relates to SEO spam.

Step 1: Confirm it in Search Console

If you haven't already, verify ownership of your site in Google Search Console; see setting up Search Console. Then:

  1. Open Security & Manual Actions, then Security issues
  2. Read the issue type, which may be something like content injection, code injection or URL injection, and note the sample URLs Google lists
  3. Check the Manual actions report too, since a separate spam penalty needs its own fix
  4. Look at the Performance report for unfamiliar queries or pages, especially in other languages

Google's Safe Browsing site status page, part of its Transparency Report, is a useful second check. For a tour of the other reports, see Search Console reports explained.

Step 2: Lock the attacker out

  • Search Console users: in Settings, then Users and permissions, remove anyone you don't recognise. Also remove their verification method, such as an HTML file or meta tag on your site, or they can verify again. Delete any sitemaps you didn't submit
  • WordPress users: remove unknown administrators and check every account with editing rights
  • Passwords: change WordPress, hosting, SFTP/FTP, database and email passwords, and reset the security keys (salts) in wp-config.php to log everyone out
  • Take a copy of the hacked site before cleaning, so you can investigate what happened if needed

Step 3: Find the hacked content

  • Search site:yourdomain.com in Google and look for pages you didn't create
  • Use URL Inspection on Google's sample URLs and run a live test; "View tested page" shows what Googlebot sees, which reveals cloaked spam that only appears to Google
  • Check for unfamiliar files, recently modified files and PHP files in the uploads folder
  • Look in the database for spam posts, injected scripts and changed site settings
  • Check .htaccess and other server config files for redirects aimed at search visitors

The most common patterns have their own guides: fixing SEO spam hacks and the WordPress redirect hack. If you're not sure what you're looking at, start with the signs a WordPress site has been hacked.

Step 4: Clean the site completely

Deleting the spam pages you can see isn't enough. If the way in and the backdoors remain, the spam comes back within days.

  1. Restore a clean backup from before the hack if you have one and know when it started, then update everything
  2. Otherwise, reinstall WordPress core, plugins and themes from official sources, and remove anything unused, unknown or nulled
  3. Remove injected code from the database, theme files and configuration files
  4. Find and remove backdoors, the hidden files that let attackers back in; see finding and removing backdoors
  5. Make spam URLs return a 404 or 410 status. Don't block them in robots.txt, because Google needs to crawl them to see they're gone, and don't redirect them to your homepage
  6. Identify and fix the entry point, such as an outdated plugin, a stolen password or a nulled theme
  7. Scan again with a server-side scanner, and check the site as a logged-out visitor and as Googlebot

The full process is in removing malware step by step.

Step 5: Request a review

When you're confident the site is clean, go back to the Security issues report, confirm you've fixed the issues and request a review. In the description, explain briefly:

  • What you found, such as spam pages, injected links or a malicious plugin
  • What you removed and how you cleaned the site
  • How you closed the entry point and what you've done to prevent it happening again

Only request a review once the site is genuinely clean; if Google still finds hacked content, the request is rejected and you start again. Search Console shows the outcome and usually sends an email too.

How long removal can take

Timing varies and isn't in your control. Google's own guidance has said that reviews for sites hacked with spam can take longer than other security reviews, potentially several weeks, so check the current Search Console documentation for up-to-date expectations. After a successful review, the label is removed as results update, and spam URLs drop out of the index as Google recrawls them.

If spam URLs are still showing for your brand searches, the Removals tool in Search Console can hide specific URLs temporarily (for around six months, at the time of writing) while Google catches up. It hides them from results; it doesn't replace cleaning them.

If the label appears without a Security issues report

Sometimes the label shows in results but the report has no details, or the site was never verified. Clean the site anyway, make sure spam URLs return 404 or 410, submit your clean sitemap and monitor results as Google recrawls. Once Search Console is verified, check the report again, because the issue may appear there after verification. If the label persists long after a thorough clean, Google's Search Central help community is the place to ask.

Recover your traffic

After the label goes, check your important pages are indexed, that their titles and descriptions are yours again, and that rankings are returning; see recovering rankings after a hack.

How to stop it happening again

  • Keep WordPress, plugins and themes updated, and delete anything you don't use
  • Never install nulled (pirated) themes or plugins
  • Use unique passwords and two-factor login for every admin and hosting account
  • Give each person only the access they need, and remove old accounts
  • Add a firewall; see WordPress firewalls explained
  • Keep automatic off-site backups, so a clean restore point always exists
  • Make sure Search Console alerts reach an inbox someone actually reads

The WordPress security checklist covers the full set of protections.

Common mistakes

  • Requesting a review before the site is fully clean
  • Deleting spam pages but leaving the backdoor that created them
  • Blocking spam URLs in robots.txt, so Google can't see they're gone
  • Restoring a backup that was already infected
  • Missing a rogue Search Console owner who keeps submitting spam sitemaps

FAQs

How long does it take to remove "This site may be hacked"?

It depends on how quickly the site is cleaned and how long Google's review and recrawl take. Spam-hack reviews can take longer than other security reviews, sometimes weeks, so clean thoroughly first to avoid a rejected request.

Will my Google rankings come back after the hack?

Often they recover once the site is clean and the label is gone, but there's no guaranteed timeline. Fixing titles, removing spam URLs and checking indexing helps.

Can I remove the label without Search Console?

Google can remove it after recrawling a clean site, but Search Console shows you the details, lets you request a review and alerts you if it happens again. Verifying it is strongly worth it.

Should I delete spam pages or redirect them?

Delete them so they return a 404 or 410. Redirecting spam URLs to your homepage or other pages can confuse Google and keep them in the index longer.

I have a security plugin. Why was my site still hacked?

Security plugins reduce risk but can't fix an outdated plugin, a reused password or a nulled theme. Updates, strong logins and good backups matter as much as the plugin.
