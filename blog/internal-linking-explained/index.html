Internal Linking Explained: Anchor Text, Hub-and-Spoke Structure, How Many Links and a WordPress Workflow

Internal linking explained for small business sites: anchor text, hub-and-spoke structure, how many links per page and a simple WordPress linking workflow.

Key takeaways
  • Contextual internal links with descriptive anchor text tell Google and visitors how your pages relate.
  • Group pages into hubs and spokes, and link new pages from your strongest existing pages so they get found.
  • There is no fixed link count; link where it helps the reader and fix orphan pages and broken links regularly.

Internal links are links from one page on your website to another. They help Google discover your pages, understand what each one is about from the link text and the words around it, and judge which pages matter most, and they guide visitors to the next useful page. Good internal linking comes down to descriptive anchor text, a clear hub-and-spoke structure, links from your strongest pages to newer ones, and contextual links inside the body text, added every time you publish.

Internal linking is part of the setup in my WordPress SEO services: the audit looks for pages with no links pointing to them, key pages buried several clicks deep and blog posts that never link to the service they relate to. It is one of the few ranking signals entirely in your control, and this guide shows how to work on it yourself.

If you already suspect pages nothing links to, start with how to find and fix orphan pages. For the other checks to run on every page, see the on-page SEO checklist.

What internal links do

  • Discovery: Google finds most pages by following links. A page that is only in your sitemap is known, but has little sign of importance, and often waits under "Discovered, currently not indexed".
  • Context: the anchor text and surrounding sentence tell Google what the destination is about, which is why "AC repair in Pune" is more useful than "click here".
  • Importance: pages that receive links from many relevant pages, and from strong ones like your homepage, are treated as more important within your site.
  • Depth: pages several clicks from the homepage tend to be crawled less often. Links bring them closer.
  • Visitors: a reader who finishes an article about a problem should be one click from the service that solves it.

Navigation links vs contextual links

TypeWhere it appearsWhat it is good for
Main menuEvery pageYour most important pages: main services, shop categories, about, contact
FooterEvery pageSecondary pages and policies; keep it short rather than stuffing in dozens of links
BreadcrumbsTop of each pageShowing where a page sits in the structure, with links back up to its parents
Contextual linksInside the body textThe strongest signal of how two pages relate, and the most useful to readers
Hub and category pagesListing pagesLinking to every page in a group, so none is left stranded
Related postsEnd of articlesA useful extra, but chosen automatically, so it doesn't replace deliberate links

Navigation links appear on every page, so they say less about any single relationship. Contextual links are where most of the meaning is. Breadcrumbs explained covers setting breadcrumbs up in WordPress.

Anchor text that helps

Anchor text is the clickable wording of a link. In the HTML it looks like this:

<p>Most cooling problems need a technician, so see our
<a href="https://example.com/services/ac-repair-pune/">AC repair in Pune</a>
page for visit charges and timings.</p>
  • Describe the destination: "WordPress speed optimization", "how to choose a solar inverter", "our Kothrud clinic", not "click here" or "read more".
  • Vary it naturally: different pages can link with slightly different wording; forcing the identical keyword into every link reads badly.
  • One phrase, one destination: don't use the same anchor for two different pages, or Google gets mixed signals about which page owns the topic.
  • Image links count too: for a linked image, the alt text acts as the anchor, so write it.

Hub and spoke: a structure that scales

A hub is a page that introduces a topic and links to every page about it; spokes are the detailed pages, each linking back to the hub and to closely related spokes. For a small business, hubs are usually service pages, shop categories or an "areas we serve" page. Topic clusters and pillar pages explains how to plan them.

This website, samverse.space, is custom-coded rather than built on WordPress and hosted on Hostinger, but it uses the same structure. The solutions page is a hub listing every service and industry page. Each service page lists up to eight supporting guides from the blog. Articles are grouped into topic hub pages, every article links to its service page and related articles, and previous and next links stay within the same topic. The Samverse case study describes how this is generated. Category archives work as hubs when they have a real introduction; SEO for category and tag pages covers when to index them.

Link from strong pages to new ones

A new page starts with no links. The quickest way to get it found is to link to it from pages Google already visits often and values:

  • Your homepage and main service pages
  • Pages with the most impressions and clicks in Search Console's Performance report
  • Pages other websites link to, listed under Top linked pages in the Links report
  • Older articles on the same topic that already rank

A "latest posts" block on the homepage helps briefly, but the link disappears as soon as newer posts push it off. Permanent contextual links from two or three related pages last.

How many internal links?

There's no fixed number. Link wherever it genuinely helps the reader: a long guide may have several contextual links, a short service page a few, plus navigation. Two practical rules matter more than a count. Every important page should have links from several relevant pages, not just the menu. And no paragraph should become a list of links; when every phrase is linked, none of them stands out.

Finding internal linking opportunities

A site: search shows which of your pages mention a topic, which are natural places to add a link to the page about it:

site:example.com "solar inverter"
site:example.com/blog/ "net metering"
  • Search Console Links report: under internal links, see which pages have the most links and click any page to see what links to it. Important pages near the bottom of the list need more.
  • Search Console Performance: pages averaging positions around 8 to 20 for useful queries often move with better internal links and content.
  • SEO plugins: Yoast SEO and Rank Math show link counts and offer internal link suggestions, some features depending on the plan at the time of writing. Dedicated linking plugins exist too; treat any suggestion as a prompt, not an instruction.
  • A crawler: desktop crawlers list how many internal links point to each URL and flag links to redirects and broken pages.

A WordPress internal linking workflow

  1. Before publishing, decide the hub the new page belongs to and two or three existing pages that should link to it.
  2. In the new page, link to its hub or service page and to related pages where the topic comes up. In the block editor, select the words, press Ctrl+K and search for the page by title.
  3. Edit the source pages and add a sentence that leads naturally into the new page, rather than a bare "see also".
  4. Link to final URLs, not addresses that redirect. After changing a URL, add a 301 redirect and update internal links to the new address.
  5. Request indexing for important new pages in URL Inspection once the links are live.
  6. Check monthly for broken internal links; see how to find and fix broken links.

Common internal linking mistakes

  • Pages that nothing links to, or that only the sitemap mentions
  • Links added only by JavaScript, such as inside tabs or sliders that load later, which may not be followed reliably
  • Nofollow on internal links, which wastes the signal you control
  • Linking to old URLs that redirect, creating chains
  • The same anchor text pointing to different pages
  • Footers stuffed with dozens of keyword links

Internal linking for local service businesses in India

For a small Indian business site, the most useful internal links usually run between three kinds of page: service pages, location pages and helpful articles. A pattern that works well:

  1. Every article links to its service page with natural wording, for example an article on slow WooCommerce stores linking to a speed optimisation service.
  2. Each service page links to a few supporting articles that answer the questions buyers ask before they enquire.
  3. Location pages link to the services offered there, and the services link back where the location genuinely matters.
  4. The homepage links to the main service hubs, not to every page on the site.

Keep the anchor text honest: if the page is about one city, say so; if it covers India generally, do not imply a local office. Internal links also need maintenance, because pages get renamed and deleted. A quarterly crawl catches broken links and orphan pages before they pile up.

FAQs

What is the difference between internal and external links?

Internal links point to other pages on your own website. External links point to a different website. Both help readers, but internal links are the ones you fully control and can use to show which of your pages matter most.

Do internal links help SEO?

Yes. They help Google find pages, understand what each is about and see which pages matter most. They won't rescue thin content, but they help good pages get crawled and ranked.

How many internal links should a page have?

There is no set number. Link where it helps the reader, make sure each important page has links from several relevant pages, and avoid paragraphs crammed with links.

Should internal links be nofollow?

No. Nofollow on your own links only stops signals flowing between your pages. If a page shouldn't be indexed, use noindex on that page instead.

Do menu and footer links count?

They count and help discovery, but because they appear on every page they say little about how two pages relate. Contextual links in the body carry more meaning.

Is a related posts plugin enough?

It is a useful extra, but it chooses links automatically and often misses the service page an article should point to. Add deliberate links in the text as well.
