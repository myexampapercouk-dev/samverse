Privacy Policy and Cookie Consent for Indian Business Websites: DPDP Basics, GA4, Meta Pixel and Consent Mode v2

Key takeaways
  • Any Indian site with forms, orders, analytics or a pixel needs a plain-language privacy policy explaining what it collects and why.
  • List every third-party tool that receives visitor data, from chat widgets and maps to couriers and CRMs, and update the list when tools change.
  • Cookie banners and Consent Mode v2 matter mainly for EU and UK visitors; Indian sites still need honest consent wording on forms.

If your website collects names, phone numbers or emails through a form, takes orders, or runs Google Analytics or the Meta Pixel, it needs a privacy policy that explains what you collect, why, who you share it with, how long you keep it and how people can contact you about it. India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act points the same way. India has no cookie-specific law, but if you get visitors from the EU or UK, or advertise to them, you'll generally need a consent banner and Google's Consent Mode v2 for those visitors.

This is a practical overview from a web developer, not legal advice; check your situation with a lawyer or CA. Does an Indian business website need a privacy policy?

In practice, yes, if it collects any personal data. Several things point the same way:

  • The DPDP Act expects a clear notice whenever you ask people for their personal data, explaining what you collect and why.
  • Older IT Act rules from 2011 already expected businesses handling certain sensitive personal data to publish a privacy policy. They're expected to give way to the DPDP framework, so check the current position with your lawyer.
  • The tools you use require it. At the time of writing, Google Analytics' terms require you to disclose that you use it, and Meta's business tools terms expect a privacy notice too.
  • Payment gateways ask for it. Gateways commonly check for privacy, terms, refund and contact pages before approving your website; see accepting online payments in India.

The DPDP Act in two minutes

The Act was passed in 2023, and its implementing rules were notified in November 2025, with most obligations for businesses phased in over a transition period rather than all at once. The ideas that matter for a website:

  • Notice and consent: consent should be free, specific, informed and given through a clear action, such as ticking an unticked box, and people must be able to withdraw it as easily as they gave it.
  • Purpose limits: data collected for one purpose shouldn't quietly be reused for another, such as adding enquiry numbers to WhatsApp promotions.
  • Security and breaches: reasonable safeguards are expected, and breaches must be reported.
  • Rights: people can ask to access, correct or erase their data, raise a grievance and nominate someone to act for them.
  • Children: data about under-18s generally needs verifiable parental consent.

There's no blanket exemption for small businesses. Dates and details can change, so treat this as orientation; what the DPDP Act means for small business websites goes deeper into terms, consent and security.

WordPress and WooCommerce tools that help

  • Settings > Privacy lets you choose your privacy policy page and shows a policy guide with suggested text from your plugins. The suggestions are a starting point, not a finished policy.
  • Tools > Export Personal Data and Tools > Erase Personal Data handle requests from people who want a copy of their data or want it deleted.
  • WooCommerce > Settings > Accounts & Privacy sets the privacy text shown at checkout and how long to keep inactive accounts and old orders.

Consent on forms: where most sites go wrong

  • Put a short line next to the submit button explaining how the details will be used, with a link to the policy.
  • Ask separately, with an unticked checkbox, before adding anyone to newsletters or promotional WhatsApp or SMS messages.
  • Don't bundle marketing into an "I agree to the terms" box, and never pre-tick consent.
  • Remove fields you don't need. Every extra field is more data to protect.

Wording along these lines works for most enquiry forms:

We'll use these details only to reply to your enquiry. See our privacy policy. [ ] Also send me occasional offers on WhatsApp (optional)

Cookies: India versus the EU and UK

India has no cookie-specific rules like Europe's. Where tracking tools collect data that can identify a person, though, that's likely to be personal data under the DPDP Act, so whether you need consent before analytics or advertising tags run for Indian visitors is a question for your lawyer.

The EU and UK are clearer: non-essential cookies such as analytics and advertising generally need opt-in consent before they're set, rejecting must be as easy as accepting, and boxes can't be pre-ticked. If you advertise abroad, export, or get noticeable traffic from Europe, plan for this.

Setting up a consent banner on WordPress

  • Use a consent plugin such as Complianz, CookieYes, Cookiebot or Real Cookie Banner, at the time of writing. If you show Google ads through AdSense to EU or UK visitors, Google requires a certified consent platform, so check the plugin's certification.
  • Target by region if that fits your advice. Many plugins can show the banner only to visitors from regions that require it. Agree the default for Indian visitors with your lawyer.
  • Make sure it really blocks scripts. Many banners look right but let Analytics run anyway. In your browser's developer tools, open Application, then Cookies, load the site in a private window and check that no _ga or _fbp cookies appear before you click Accept.
  • Load each tag once. Analytics added by a theme setting, a plugin and Google Tag Manager at the same time defeats the banner and doubles your numbers; Google Tag Manager basics explains how to keep tags in one place.

Google Consent Mode v2 for EU and UK visitors

Consent Mode passes each visitor's choice to Google's tags through four signals: ad_storage, analytics_storage, ad_user_data and ad_personalization. At the time of writing, Google requires these signals from advertisers who want to keep using its measurement, remarketing and personalisation features with visitors from the European Economic Area and the UK.

  • Basic mode doesn't load Google's tags at all until the visitor accepts.
  • Advanced mode loads the tags straight away, sending cookieless pings when consent is denied, and Google models the missing conversions. It recovers more data, but sends more to Google, so discuss which mode suits you with your lawyer.

Most consent plugins set Consent Mode up for you, and Google Tag Manager has consent settings built in. Done by hand, the default has to load before your Google tag:

<script>
  window.dataLayer = window.dataLayer || [];
  function gtag(){dataLayer.push(arguments);}
  gtag('consent', 'default', {
    ad_storage: 'denied',
    ad_user_data: 'denied',
    ad_personalization: 'denied',
    analytics_storage: 'denied',
    region: ['GB', 'IE', 'FR', 'DE', 'NL']
  });
</script>

The region list is shortened here; a real set-up lists every EEA country plus the UK. When a visitor accepts, the banner sends a consent update setting the relevant signals to granted. The Meta Pixel has an equivalent: it can be held back until consent and then granted, as explained in Meta Pixel and Conversions API explained. Google's Tag Assistant shows which consent state your tags see, so test both accepting and rejecting.

A practical checklist

  1. List every form, plugin and third-party tool that collects or receives personal data.
  2. Write or update the privacy policy to match, with a privacy contact and a "last updated" date.
  3. Link it from the footer, every form and the checkout.
  4. Add separate, unticked marketing consent and remove unneeded fields.
  5. Decide on cookie consent for each audience, with advice, and set up the banner and Consent Mode where needed.
  6. Confirm tracking scripts really wait for consent where they should.
  7. Set retention periods and delete old enquiries and data you no longer need.
  8. Have a lawyer or CA review the policy, and revisit it whenever you add a new tool.

Third-party tools to list in your privacy policy

Most small Indian websites share data with more services than the owner remembers. Before writing or updating the policy, open your site and list every tool that receives visitor data: Google Analytics and Tag Manager, the Meta Pixel, a WhatsApp chat widget, embedded YouTube videos and Google Maps, your form plugin and the email service it sends through, a CRM, the payment gateway and, for shops, the courier or shipping app. Each one belongs in the policy in plain words, with what it receives and why. Review the list whenever a plugin or marketing tool is added.

FAQs

Is a privacy policy mandatory for websites in India?

If your site collects personal data, the DPDP Act expects you to give people a clear notice, and payment gateways and tools like Google Analytics expect a privacy policy too. A website with any form, order or analytics should have one. Check the specifics with a lawyer.

Do Indian websites need a cookie banner?

Indian law has no cookie-specific rule, but tracking data can be personal data under the DPDP Act, so get advice for your Indian visitors. Visitors from the EU and UK generally require opt-in consent before analytics and advertising cookies are set.

What is Google Consent Mode v2, and do I need it?

It's how your consent banner tells Google's tags whether a visitor has agreed to analytics and advertising. If you use Google Ads or Analytics with visitors from the EEA or UK, Google requires it at the time of writing to keep using features like remarketing and conversion measurement for those visitors.

Can I copy a privacy policy from another website?

It's a bad idea. It may be protected by copyright, and it describes someone else's tools and practices, not yours. Use a template only as a checklist and rewrite it to match what your site actually does.

Does WordPress create a privacy policy for me?

WordPress creates a draft privacy page and a guide with suggested text from your plugins under Settings > Privacy. It's a useful start, but you still need to edit it to describe your own business, forms and tools.

Who should write my privacy policy?

You know what data you collect, your developer knows which tools and plugins handle it, and a lawyer or CA knows what the law requires. The best policies come from all three; this article isn't legal advice.
