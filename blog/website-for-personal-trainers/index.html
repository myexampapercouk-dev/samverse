Websites for Personal Trainers: Packages, Consultations and Honest Results

How personal trainers and fitness coaches can plan a website: packages described by scope, home and online coaching, free consultations and honest results.

Samverse 11 October 2026 · 6 min read
On this page
  1. Show who you train and where
  2. Describe packages by scope, not promises
  3. Credentials, stated honestly
  4. Transformation photos and testimonials, handled honestly
  5. Make the free consultation easy to book
  6. Give online coaching its own page
  7. Get found locally and build authority
Key takeaways
  • Say clearly who you train and where, whether in person, at home or online, and describe each package by sessions, support and validity rather than promised results.
  • List only certifications you genuinely hold, with the issuing body and year, and be clear about your scope on injuries and nutrition.
  • Use transformation photos and testimonials only with written consent, unedited and with honest context, and make the free consultation easy to book.

Someone looking for a personal trainer isn't just choosing a place to work out; they are choosing a person. They want to know whether you train people like them, where and when you can train them, what they get for their money and whether you are qualified and safe. Some will invite you into their home or follow your plan from another city, so trust matters even more than it does for a gym. If you run a gym or studio with several trainers, see websites for gyms, yoga studios and fitness trainers; this guide is for independent trainers and coaches.

Show who you train and where

Start with the two questions every visitor has: "Can you help someone like me?" and "Can you train me where I am?" Name the people you work with best, such as beginners, busy professionals, women returning to exercise after pregnancy, older adults or people training for a sport, but only the groups you are genuinely trained and experienced to help.

Then be clear about the formats you offer:

In person at a gym or studioWhich gym, whether they need a membership there, available timingsLocation, any gym entry rules, typical slots
Home visitsWhether you cover their area, what equipment you bring, how much space is neededAreas covered, travel policy, equipment list, what to prepare

Describe packages by scope, not promises

"Fat loss transformation programme" tells a client very little. A package page should explain exactly what they are buying:

  • Number and length of sessions, and how often they happen
  • Whether a written workout plan, progress check-ins or chat support between sessions is included
  • How long the package stays valid, and the rescheduling and cancellation policy
  • Whether diet guidance is included, and what kind (more on this below)
  • What happens at the end: a review, a new plan or a renewal option

Describe outcomes as goals you work towards ("build strength safely", "feel comfortable in the gym", "prepare for a running event"), not guaranteed results with a number of kilos and a deadline. If you prefer not to publish fees, explain what affects them: format, frequency, travel distance for home visits and package length. Either way, a client should be able to tell which package fits them before they message you.

Credentials, stated honestly

Clients often can't judge a fitness certificate, but they notice when details are vague. List each qualification you genuinely hold with the name of the course, the organisation that issued it and the year. Do the same for specialist training, such as working with older adults, pre- and postnatal clients or injury rehabilitation alongside a physiotherapist. A current first aid and CPR certificate is worth mentioning if you have one.

  • Don't list courses you are still studying as if they are complete; say "currently studying" instead
  • Don't call yourself a nutritionist, dietitian or physiotherapist unless you are qualified as one
  • Be clear about your scope: you don't diagnose injuries or medical conditions, and you will ask clients to check with their doctor when needed

Food is the usual grey area. General healthy-eating guidance is one thing; meal plans for medical conditions belong with a qualified professional. Many trainers work alongside a nutritionist for this, and if you do, say so; see websites for nutritionists and dietitians for how they present their services.

Transformation photos and testimonials, handled honestly

Before-and-after photos are powerful, which is why they need care. They show people's bodies, so treat them as sensitive personal data:

  • Get written consent that says which photos you will use, where (website, Instagram, ads) and for how long, and that the client can withdraw it
  • Remove photos promptly when someone asks, and crop out faces if a client prefers
  • Never edit, filter or retouch body photos, and take before and after shots in similar light and poses
  • Add honest context: the time period, the training format and anything else that played a part
  • Say plainly that results vary from person to person

Testimonials work the same way. Use real words from real clients, with permission to use their name or initials, and never write them yourself or polish them into claims the client didn't make. A short quote about how you helped someone feel confident, stay consistent or train around a bad knee is often more persuasive than a dramatic weight-loss figure. See how to collect and display customer testimonials for a simple process.

Make the free consultation easy to book

For most trainers, the first conversion isn't a package sale but a free consultation or trial session. Give it a clear button on every page and explain what happens: a short call or meeting to talk about goals, training history and any injuries, followed by an honest recommendation.

  • Let people pick a slot from a booking calendar instead of messaging back and forth; see online appointment booking on your website
  • Keep the form short: name, phone, goal, preferred format, area (for home visits) and preferred times
  • Ask health questions later, through a pre-exercise questionnaire that you store securely, and collect only what you need
  • Add a WhatsApp button for quick questions about areas, timings and packages

Give online coaching its own page

Online clients can't see you in person, so the page has to do more explaining. Cover how the programme is delivered (an app, shared plans or live video sessions), how often you check in, how clients send videos for form feedback, how quickly you reply and what happens if they miss a week. If you coach clients in other countries, mention time zones and currencies. Take payment through a gateway that supports UPI and cards, and publish a clear refund and pause policy before anyone pays.

Get found locally and build authority

  • Set up a Google Business Profile as a service-area business if you visit homes, without showing your home address
  • Create pages for what people search, such as "personal trainer in {area}", "home personal trainer {city}" and "online fitness coach"
  • Publish helpful content: beginner routines, exercise technique videos and honest answers to common questions, with sensible safety notes
  • Ask happy clients for Google reviews, and reply to every one
  • Keep pages fast and mobile-friendly, because most people will find you on their phone
