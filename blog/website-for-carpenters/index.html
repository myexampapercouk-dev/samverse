Websites for Carpenters and Custom Woodwork Workshops

How carpenters and woodwork workshops can win better jobs online: portfolios by project type, materials explained, a clear process and forms asking for sizes.

Sameer Gupta 1 October 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways
  • Organise your portfolio by project type and label each job with the material, finish and area.
  • Explain materials, finishes and the measurement-to-installation process so customers trust what they are paying for.
  • Use enquiry forms that ask for rough dimensions, photos and deadlines so you can reply with useful answers.

Most people looking for a carpenter want something made to fit: a wardrobe for an awkward alcove, a bed with storage, a TV unit, a pooja unit, new doors or furniture for a new flat. Before they call, they want to see that you've done similar work, understand what it will be made of, and know how the job will run. A good website shows all three, and brings you enquiries that are already halfway to a confirmed order. If you mainly sell modular kitchens and wardrobes from a showroom, see the guide to websites for modular kitchen and wardrobe companies; this one is for carpenters and custom woodwork workshops.

What customers want to know first

  • Have you made something like what I need, and is it well finished?
  • What materials do you use, and how will it look in a few years?
  • Do you work on-site, in your workshop or both?
  • Do you supply the material, or work on labour only with material I buy?
  • How long will it take, and how much dust and disturbance should I expect?
  • Who do I call if a hinge or drawer needs adjusting later?

Answer these on your site and you'll spend far less time on calls that go nowhere.

Organise your portfolio by project type

Your work is your best sales tool, but a single gallery of hundreds of mixed photos is hard to browse. Group projects the way customers think:

  • Wardrobes and lofts
  • Beds, side tables and bedroom furniture
  • TV units, crockery units and storage
  • Pooja units and mandirs
  • Doors, windows, frames and wooden partitions
  • Office and shop furniture
  • Repairs, re-polishing and restoration

For each project, add a short note: what was made, the wood or board used, the finish, the area, and anything tricky you solved, such as an uneven wall or a narrow staircase. Use real photos of your own work, taken in good daylight, with close-ups of joints, edges and hardware. If you show a drawing or 3D render, label it clearly so no one mistakes it for finished work. Explain materials and finishes in plain language

Customers hear a lot of confusing terms and worry about being sold a cheaper material than they paid for. A clear materials page builds trust and saves explaining the same things on every visit.

TopicWhat to explain
Solid woodThe timbers you work with, where each suits, and how solid wood can move with changes in humidity
BoardsThe plywood grades, MDF or other boards you use, and which you recommend for kitchens, bathrooms and other damp areas
Surface finishesLaminate, veneer, paint or other options you offer, with photos of real samples
Polish and coatingsThe polish types you offer, how they look, how they wear and how to care for them
HardwareHinges, channels, handles and locks, and the brands or grades you normally fit

Only list what you actually use, and be honest about trade-offs rather than claiming one option is best for everything. A short "looking after your furniture" page is also handy to share on WhatsApp after handover.

Show the process from measurement to installation

People commissioning custom work worry about delays and surprises. Lay out the steps on their own page:

  1. Enquiry: the customer shares the requirement, rough sizes and photos
  2. Site visit and measurement: say whether there's a charge and whether it's adjusted against the order
  3. Design and material choice: a sketch or drawing, with materials, finish and hardware agreed in writing
  4. Quote and advance: what's included, payment stages and accepted methods such as UPI or bank transfer
  5. Making: in your workshop, on-site or both, with an honest typical timeline
  6. Installation and finishing: fitting, polishing and cleaning up
  7. Handover and after-service: adjustments, any warranty on workmanship, and how to ask for help later

Mention what can cause delays, such as pending civil or electrical work at the site, or waiting for a particular material or hardware to arrive. Being upfront here prevents most disputes later.

Enquiry forms that ask for sizes and photos

A vague "contact us" form gets vague enquiries. A form designed for carpentry gets enquiries you can roughly assess before the visit:

  • What they need (wardrobe, bed, door, repair or a full home) and their area
  • Approximate dimensions, with a note that rough sizes are fine
  • A photo upload for the space or a reference design
  • Material preference, or "not sure, please advise"
  • Labour only or with material
  • When they need it, such as a possession date or a wedding in the family

Keep a WhatsApp button next to the form, because many customers find it easier to send photos and a quick voice note; see adding WhatsApp to your website. Then reply quickly, ideally the same day.

Trust and local search

  • Workshop page: photos of your workshop, tools and team, so people know you're an established business
  • Reviews: genuine Google reviews, with customer photos where they're happy to share them
  • Trade work: if you work with interior designers, builders or contractors, a separate page and enquiry route for them
  • Google Business Profile: the right category, service areas, your workshop address if customers visit, and fresh project photos

Target the searches people actually make: "carpenter in {area}", "custom wardrobe maker in {city}", "wooden bed made to order {city}". A page for each main project type, with real photos and descriptions, gives you a far better chance than one generic services page.

Keep a photo-heavy site fast

Portfolios slow sites down quickly. Resize and compress every photo before uploading, use modern image formats, and lazy-load long galleries; see image optimization for WordPress. A fast gallery keeps people browsing long enough to send an enquiry.
