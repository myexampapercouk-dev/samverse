Websites for Taxi and Car Rental Services: Trip Types, Fare Display, Pickup and Drop Booking, Drivers and Cars, Corporate Accounts and Honest Route Pages

Plan a taxi or car rental website: local, outstation and airport trips, clear fares or estimates, booking forms, corporate accounts and route pages.

Key takeaways
  • Organise the site by trip type, local, outstation, airport and events, because each customer worries about different things and needs different booking fields.
  • Show what makes up a fare, such as driver allowance, tolls, parking and state taxes, whether you publish fixed fares, give estimates or quote on request.
  • Show your own cars and verified drivers, describe permits in general terms, and write route pages only for routes you drive regularly, with details that are specific to each.

People book a taxi or rental car on a website for trips they plan: an early airport run, a weekend outstation trip, a family wedding or a month of office pick-ups. They choose the operator whose site shows the right car for the trip, explains the fare without surprises, and takes the booking with pickup, drop and time in one go, then confirms the driver and vehicle before the day.

That is a well-planned business site rather than an app, of the kind described on my WordPress website development page: trip and fleet pages your office can edit, a booking form, tap-to-call and WhatsApp, and SEO foundations for the searches customers use. For seasonal campaigns, such as airport transfers or hill station packages, a separate landing page whose headline matches each ad will usually convert better than the homepage.

Start with the trip, not the fleet

Many taxi sites open with a row of cars. Customers, though, think first about the journey. Lead with trip types, and let each one carry its own details and form:

Trip typeWhat the customer worries aboutWhat the page should show
Local hourly rentalOvertime charges and whether the car will waitPackages such as an 8-hour, 80 km block, and how extra hours and kilometres are charged
Outstation one-wayPaying for the empty returnWhether one-way fares are available on that route, and what they include
Outstation round tripMinimum daily kilometres, night charges and the driver's stayThe daily minimum, driver allowance, and who arranges the driver's food and rest
Airport and railway transfersDelays, early mornings and finding the driverFlight or train tracking, free waiting time if any, the pickup point and name board
Weddings and eventsDecorated cars, multiple vehicles arriving on timeFleet for events, decoration options, a coordinator for multi-car bookings

If groups and tempo travellers make up much of your business, give them their own section; the guide for tempo traveller and bus operators deals with seating layouts and group quotes.

Showing fares: fixed, estimated or on request

Fare surprises are the biggest source of bad reviews in this business. Whichever way you price, publish what a fare is made of:

  • The base rate, per kilometre or per package, by car type
  • Minimum kilometres per day on round trips
  • Driver allowance, and night charges if the driver works late or starts early
  • Tolls, parking and airport entry fees, and whether they are included or paid on the way
  • Interstate taxes or permit charges on outstation trips, where they apply
  • Waiting time, extra hours and extra kilometres
  • GST, shown clearly

Then choose one of three approaches. Fixed fares suit airport transfers and a few popular routes, where you know the distance and conditions well. Estimates suit outstation trips, as long as the page says the final fare follows the trip sheet and explains what can change it. Quotes on request suit weddings, multi-day tours and corporate contracts.

On the Sahni Power Solutions site, each capacity page answers that band's own questions; the 82.5 to 125 kVA page, for instance, sets out what affects the hire charge: how long the hire lasts, who supplies fuel, whether an operator comes with the machine, and how far the site is from the yard. A taxi site benefits from the same habit: explain the price drivers on each trip page, so customers understand a quote before they ask for one.

A fare estimator, used carefully

A calculator that takes pickup and drop and returns an estimated fare can work well, but it needs a maps service to measure distance, which may carry its own usage charges, and rules for tolls and taxes that you keep up to date. A simpler alternative is a table of fixed fares for your most common routes, plus a quick quote form for everything else. A booking form that captures pickup and drop properly

A form asking only for name and phone forces a call for every booking. Ask for what the dispatcher needs, in this order:

  1. Trip type: local, outstation one-way, round trip, airport or railway, event
  2. Pickup address with a landmark, and the drop address or destination city
  3. Date and pickup time, and the return date for round trips
  4. Flight or train number for transfers, so the driver can track delays
  5. Passengers and luggage, which decide the car more than anything else
  6. Car type preferred, with "suggest one" as an option
  7. Name, mobile number and consent for WhatsApp updates

Address suggestions as people type help reduce wrong pickups, though they add a maps service to the setup. After submission, show a thank-you page explaining when the booking will be confirmed, and send the driver's name, phone number and vehicle number on WhatsApp before the trip. If you take advances, a payment link sent with the confirmation keeps the form itself short.

Cars and drivers, shown honestly

Customers have been sent a different car from the one in the photo often enough to be suspicious. Photograph your own vehicles, inside and out, and list each car type with its seating, luggage space and air conditioning. If you assign "this model or similar", say so on the page rather than letting the customer discover it at the gate.

For drivers, describe what you actually do: how you verify them, including police verification if you carry it out, the licence and badge requirements they meet, their experience on outstation routes, the languages they speak and the dress code. If your cars have tracking devices or panic buttons, which the rules require in many public service vehicles, say what you have and how it helps passengers. Do not claim features you have not fitted.

Corporate accounts

Companies booking regular airport runs, employee pick-ups or visitor transport are valuable, steady customers with their own needs. Give them a page and a form that cover:

  • Monthly billing with GST invoices in the company's name and GSTIN
  • Duty slips or trip sheets with start and end kilometres and times, signed or confirmed by the passenger
  • How employees or the admin team book, and who confirms changes
  • A named coordinator and an out-of-hours number
  • The car types available for executives and for staff transport

Ask for the company name, contact person, the likely number of trips a month and the cities involved, so you can reply with a proper proposal rather than a rate card.

Permits and paperwork, in general terms

Customers rarely ask about permits, but corporate buyers and careful travellers do, and claims about them should be accurate. Commercial taxis need commercial registration and the permits that apply to their work; for outstation trips across states, operators may use an all-India tourist permit, and some trips still involve state taxes or entry charges. Self-drive rental is regulated separately, and operating it generally needs its own licence from the transport department. These rules differ by state and have been changing, so describe your arrangements in general terms, mention a permit only if your vehicles hold it, and check the current position with your state transport department. Route pages that are not doorway pages

Pages titled "[city] to [city] taxi" can bring steady bookings, and they are also the most abused page type in this trade, with hundreds of near-identical pages that only swap city names. Google treats those as doorway pages. Create a route page only for routes you drive regularly, and make each one specific:

  • The road distance and a realistic range for driving time, including the usual traffic at the start and end
  • The route your drivers prefer, rest stops and the best times to leave
  • Whether one-way fares are available, and the fare or how it is worked out
  • The cars most customers choose for that trip, and why
  • Reviews from customers who took that route, shared with permission

A few dozen genuine route pages are worth more than a thousand copies. How to build local pages without doorway spam sets out where the line sits.

Calls, WhatsApp and local search

Many customers still prefer to call, especially for same-day trips. Keep tap-to-call and WhatsApp visible on every page, and track which pages and ads produce those calls; call tracking for small businesses explains the options. Keep your Google Business Profile hours, phone number and photos of the actual fleet in line with the website, and ask passengers for reviews after a good trip.

FAQs

Should a taxi website show fixed fares?

Fixed fares work for airport transfers and a few regular routes. For other trips, show what a fare is made of and give estimates or quotes, explaining what can change the final amount.

What should a cab booking form ask?

Trip type, pickup and drop with landmarks, date and time, flight or train number for transfers, passengers and luggage, car preference and contact details.

Can customers pay online when they book?

Yes. A payment link sent with the confirmation, or a gateway on the booking page, can take an advance or the full fare.

Do we need a page for every city-to-city route?

No. Create route pages only for routes you drive regularly, each with details specific to that route, and handle other trips through the quote form.

Should we mention permits on the website?

Only in general, accurate terms. Mention a specific permit only if your vehicles hold it, and check current rules with your state transport department.
