Websites for Speech Therapy, Occupational Therapy and Child Development Centres

What parents look for on a speech therapy, OT or child development centre website: therapist credentials, clear assessments, online sessions and privacy.

Key takeaways
  • Worried parents want to see who will work with their child, so show each therapist's qualifications, registration, focus areas and languages.
  • Explain assessments and online sessions in plain language, and keep booking short with WhatsApp and phone options.
  • Protect children's data, never publish a child's photo or story without written consent, and avoid promising outcomes.

Parents searching for a speech therapist or child development centre are usually worried. A teacher may have mentioned a speech delay, a paediatrician may have suggested an assessment, or they've simply noticed their child isn't doing what other children of the same age do. Your website has to answer their questions calmly, show that your team is qualified, and make the first step feel manageable.

What parents look for first

  • Who will work with my child? Names, qualifications and experience of each therapist
  • Do you help with my child's concern? Speech and language delay, stammering, feeding difficulties, sensory issues, handwriting, attention or autism support
  • What happens at the first visit? A clear explanation of the assessment
  • Where, when and how? Location, timings, session length and whether online sessions are available
  • How do I get started? A simple way to call, WhatsApp or book an assessment

Many parents read on their phone late at night once the children are asleep, so this information should be easy to find on a small screen, without downloading a PDF brochure.

Show your therapists' credentials clearly

Credentials are the biggest trust signal in this field. Give each therapist a profile with:

  • Qualifications, such as BASLP or MASLP for speech-language pathologists and BOT or MOT for occupational therapists
  • Registration details with the relevant professional council, where one applies (check which body governs each discipline)
  • Areas of focus, for example early language, fluency, sensory integration or feeding
  • Languages they can do therapy in, which matters a lot for a child who speaks Hindi, Tamil or Marathi at home
  • A real, friendly photo

If you work alongside developmental paediatricians, psychologists or special educators, explain how the team works together. A multidisciplinary approach is reassuring, but only describe it if that's genuinely how you work.

Explain assessments in plain language

The assessment is usually the first thing a parent books, and the thing they feel most anxious about. A dedicated page should explain:

  1. Who the assessment is for and what it looks at, such as speech and language, motor skills, sensory processing, play and social skills
  2. How long it takes and whether a parent stays in the room
  3. What to bring: previous reports, school feedback, a list of words the child uses, short videos of the child at home
  4. What happens afterwards: a discussion of findings, a written report, and recommendations on whether therapy is needed and how often

Avoid jargon. If you use terms like "receptive language" or "sensory integration", explain them in a sentence. A short FAQ helps too, because questions like "my child is two and not talking yet, should I wait?" are exactly what parents type into Google.

Parent-friendly information and resources

Good educational content builds trust and brings in search traffic. Useful topics include:

  • Typical speech and language milestones by age, with a clear note that every child develops differently
  • Signs that it may be worth getting an assessment
  • How parents can support therapy at home through everyday play and routines
  • The difference between speech therapy, occupational therapy and special education
  • Common worries such as screen time and bilingual homes

Have a qualified therapist write or review each article, show their name and the date, and keep the tone informative rather than diagnostic. Always point parents towards a professional assessment instead of self-diagnosis. For more on why this matters, see E-E-A-T explained for small business websites.

Online sessions and booking

Many centres now offer online therapy or parent coaching, which helps families in smaller towns or those living abroad. If you offer it, add a page covering:

  • Which services work online and which need in-person visits
  • What families need: a laptop or tablet, stable internet, a quiet space and often a parent sitting with the child
  • Which video platform you use and how session links are shared
  • Session length, time zones for overseas families, and how payment works (UPI, payment links or cards)

For bookings, let parents request an assessment slot online, with WhatsApp and a phone number for those who prefer to talk first. Keep the form short: the child's age, the main concern and a preferred time are enough to start. Automated reminders help reduce missed sessions; see online appointment booking.

Protect children's data and privacy

A child development centre handles sensitive information: developmental history, diagnoses, reports and sometimes videos. Treat it carefully:

  • Collect only what you need through the website; detailed history can wait for the intake session
  • Don't ask parents to send reports or videos through an ordinary contact form or a shared inbox; use a secure method and restrict who can access files
  • Never publish a child's photo, name, video or story without written parental consent, and ask whether it's needed at all; photos of your therapy rooms and materials often work just as well
  • Keep advertising pixels and unnecessary tracking off pages where parents describe their child's needs
  • Publish a clear privacy policy explaining what you collect and why

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act has specific requirements for children's data, including parental consent. See the DPDP Act and your website, and check the current rules with your lawyer.

Avoid outcome promises

Every child progresses differently, and worried parents are vulnerable to claims that sound too good. Avoid wording like "your child will talk in three months", "cure" or "guaranteed results". Instead:

  • Describe your approach, how you set goals with families and how you track progress
  • Share parent testimonials only with consent, and keep them about the experience (communication, support, warmth) rather than promised outcomes
  • Be honest that the length of therapy depends on the child

Honest wording builds more trust than big promises, and it keeps you in line with professional ethics. Check whether your professional council has rules on advertising.

Local SEO and a fast mobile site

Most families search locally: "speech therapist near me", "occupational therapy for kids in Pune" or "child development centre in Andheri". To show up:

  • Complete your Google Business Profile with the right categories, hours, photos of the centre and a booking link
  • Create a separate page for each main service, and a page for each branch if you have more than one
  • Mention the areas you serve and nearby landmarks naturally
  • Ask satisfied parents for Google reviews, without offering incentives

Keep the site light and fast, with readable text and large buttons. Parents are often browsing with a toddler on one arm.
