"Deceptive Site Ahead" Warning on Your Website: How to Find the Phishing Pages, Clean Up and Get Google to Remove It

How to remove deceptive site warning screens in Chrome: find the flagged phishing pages in Search Console, clean the site, close the hole, request review.

Key takeaways
  • To remove the deceptive site warning, find the flagged URLs in Search Console's Security issues report, delete the phishing pages and how the attacker got in, then request a review.
  • The warning will not fade while the bad pages remain, and a review submitted before the cleanup is finished usually fails.
  • After Google clears the site, check Edge and antivirus blocklists separately, change every password and keep updates, backups and monitoring running.

To remove deceptive site warning screens ("Deceptive site ahead" in Chrome), open the Security issues report in Google Search Console, delete the phishing pages it lists along with whatever let the attacker in, then click Request Review. Once Google confirms the site is clean, Chrome and other browsers using Google Safe Browsing stop showing the red screen, usually within a few days for phishing.

Most of the time the cause is a hacked site: someone has uploaded a fake bank, courier, webmail or payment login page into your hosting account and is sending people to it from emails or text messages. What the "Deceptive site ahead" warning means

Google Safe Browsing keeps a list of web pages that trick people or harm their devices. Chrome, Firefox and Safari all check pages against Google's list at the time of writing, so a single flag can block most of your visitors at once. The wording tells you what Google thinks it found.

What visitors seeWhat Google thinks it foundCategory in Search Console
"Deceptive site ahead"Phishing: pages that trick people into entering passwords, card details, OTPs or personal informationSocial engineering (deceptive pages)
"The site ahead contains malware"Pages that try to install malicious softwareMalware
"The site ahead contains harmful programs"Downloads of unwanted or deceptive softwareUnwanted software or harmful downloads
"This site may be hacked" (in search results only)Spam pages, links or redirects added by a hackerHacked: content, code or URL injection

Newer versions of Chrome may show a shorter "Dangerous site" heading for the same problems. Microsoft Edge is different: it uses Microsoft Defender SmartScreen, which keeps its own list, so Edge can show a warning when Chrome does not, or the other way round.

Step 1: Confirm the warning and find the flagged URLs

  1. Open Search Console. Go to Security & Manual Actions, then Security issues. Note the issue type and the sample URLs. The samples are examples, not a complete list.
  2. Verify the whole domain if you haven't. A Domain property, verified with a DNS TXT record, covers every subdomain, which matters because phishing kits often sit on a forgotten subdomain or an old test site. See setting up Search Console.
  3. Check Google's Safe Browsing site status page in its Transparency Report for a second opinion on the domain.
  4. Look at the flagged URLs safely. Use a private window where you are not logged in to WordPress, or a URL scanner, rather than your everyday admin browser.

For a step-by-step method to trace and delete the page, then clear it from Google, see how to remove a fake web page on a WordPress site.

Step 2: Find the phishing pages and how they got there

Phishing kits are usually uploaded as a zip file and unpacked into a folder, so they sit outside WordPress and don't show up in your Pages list. Look in these places:

  • New folders in public_html named after a bank, courier, webmail provider or tax refund, or a random string of letters
  • Deep inside WordPress folders such as wp-content/uploads, wp-includes or wp-admin, where a stray folder is easy to miss
  • The .well-known folder, which exists for SSL checks and is rarely opened by owners
  • Add-on domains, subdomains and old installs in the same hosting account
  • Zip files anywhere in the account, which are often the kit itself
  • Injected redirects that send visitors from your pages to a phishing site hosted elsewhere
  • Third-party scripts and ads that show fake download buttons or "your phone has a virus" messages, which Search Console may describe as deceptive embedded resources

Without SSH, use your host's File Manager and sort folders by last modified date. With SSH, these commands, run from the site's root folder, list recently changed files, archives, PHP files in uploads and every administrator, and check WordPress files against the official copies:

find . -type f -mtime -30 -not -path "./wp-content/cache/*" | head -100
find . -name "*.zip"
ls -la .well-known
find wp-content/uploads -name "*.php"
wp core verify-checksums
wp plugin verify-checksums --all
wp user list --role=administrator --fields=ID,user_login,user_email

Your access log shows where the stolen details were being sent. Form submissions to paths you don't recognise are a strong clue:

grep "POST" access.log | awk '{print $7}' | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -20

Also work out how the attacker got in. The usual causes are an outdated plugin or theme, a nulled theme, a stolen hosting, cPanel or FTP password, or another neglected site in the same account.

Step 3: Clean the site and close the way in

  1. Back up the infected copy first, labelled clearly, so you can see later how the kit arrived.
  2. Delete every phishing folder and file, not just the sample URLs. The removed URLs should return a 404 or 410 error. Don't redirect them to your homepage, and don't block them in robots.txt, because Google needs to recrawl them to see they've gone.
  3. Remove backdoors. Attackers leave small scripts that let them re-upload the kit after you delete it; see finding and removing backdoors.
  4. Reinstall WordPress core, plugins and themes from official sources, and delete anything unused, unknown or pirated.
  5. Change every password: hosting, cPanel, FTP or SFTP, database, email and each WordPress administrator. Replace the security keys and salts in wp-config.php and turn on two-factor authentication.
  6. Clean the rest of the hosting account, including add-on domains and old copies, because one infected site can write to the others.

The full process, including the database and scheduled tasks, is in how to remove malware from WordPress step by step. If your host has already switched the account off, start with what to do when hosting is suspended for malware.

Step 4: Request a review in Search Console

When you're confident every phishing page is gone, return to the Security issues report, tick the box confirming you have fixed the issues and click Request Review. Keep the description short and factual:

We found a phishing kit imitating a bank login in /wp-content/uploads/old/ and a second copy on the test subdomain. Both folders and the zip files have been deleted and now return 404. The entry point was an outdated file manager plugin, which has been removed. We have reinstalled WordPress core and plugins from official sources, removed a backdoor, changed all hosting, FTP, database and WordPress passwords, and turned on two-factor authentication.

If you can't use Search Console, or you believe the flag is a mistake, Google's Safe Browsing site has a form for reporting an incorrect phishing warning. Search Console is still the better route, because it shows exactly what Google found and tells you the outcome.

How long does it take for the warning to go away?

Google's help pages give rough review times by issue type. These are Google's figures at the time of writing, so check the current Search Console documentation:

Issue typeTypical review time (Google's guidance)If the review fails
Phishing (social engineering)About a dayGoogle lists URLs that still have problems; fix them and request again
MalwareA few daysSame: fix the listed URLs, then request again
Hacked with spamSeveral weeksSee the "This site may be hacked" guide linked above

After a successful review, browsers stop showing the warning as their copies of the Safe Browsing list refresh, which can take a little longer for some visitors. Be careful with your first request: Google has a repeat-offender policy, and a site that is flagged again soon after passing a review may have to wait longer before it can ask for another one. Google has mentioned 30 days; check the current policy.

Edge, antivirus and other blocklists

  • Microsoft Edge: if Edge still warns after Google clears you, Microsoft has its own process for site owners to report an incorrect SmartScreen warning.
  • Antivirus and security vendors: a URL scanner such as VirusTotal shows which vendors still flag the domain. Each vendor has its own review or dispute form.
  • Email: if the kit was also used to send phishing emails from your server, check your domain and server IP on a blacklist lookup tool such as MXToolbox.

FAQs

How do I remove deceptive site warning from my website?

Find the flagged URLs in Search Console's Security issues report, delete the phishing pages and the backdoor or weak login that let them in, update everything and change passwords, then click Request Review. Chrome stops warning once Google confirms the site is clean.

How long does Google take to remove a deceptive site warning?

Google's guidance has said phishing reviews take about a day and malware reviews a few days, though times vary. A rejected review restarts the wait, so make sure the site is fully clean before you request one.

Why does Chrome say my site is deceptive when I can't see anything wrong?

Because the phishing pages usually live in a folder or subdomain you never visit, not on your normal pages. Your homepage can look perfect while a fake bank login sits in an uploads folder. The sample URLs in Search Console show where to look.

How do I remove a fake page someone added to my WordPress site?

If it appears in Pages or Posts, delete it and empty the trash. If it's a folder of files, as most phishing pages are, delete it through File Manager or SFTP. Then look for the backdoor or stolen password that let it in, or it will reappear.

Will the warning hurt my Google rankings?

While the warning is up, most visitors won't get past it, so you lose traffic and enquiries regardless of rankings. Once it's cleared, rankings usually recover as Google recrawls the clean site; see recovering rankings after a hack.
