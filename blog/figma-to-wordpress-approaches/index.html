Figma to WordPress: Elementor, Block Theme, Bricks or a Custom Theme? How to Choose the Build Approach

Figma to WordPress approaches compared: Elementor, block themes, custom themes, Bricks and headless, with the trade-offs and how to choose for your project.

Key takeaways
  • The best Figma to WordPress approach depends on who edits the site afterwards, how strict the design is, and how much speed and long-term maintenance matter.
  • Elementor suits marketing teams who want visual editing; block themes and custom themes suit stricter designs, better speed and fewer plugin dependencies.
  • Whichever approach you choose, insist on design tokens, reusable components, responsive checks against the Figma frames and a handover guide.

There is no single best way to convert Figma to WordPress: Elementor is best when non-technical editors want drag-and-drop control, a block theme with custom blocks is best for speed and a native editing experience, and a custom theme with custom fields is best for strict layouts and long-term stability. Bricks sits between a builder and custom code, and headless only makes sense for app-like projects with a development team.

This guide compares the five approaches on editing, speed, design accuracy, cost and maintenance, then gives a simple way to choose for your project, whether you are an agency handing off a design or a business owner commissioning a build.

Option 1: Elementor and Elementor Pro

Elementor is the most familiar choice for agencies and businesses. A good build uses global colours and fonts taken from the Figma styles, saved templates for headers, footers and repeated sections, and the theme builder for post and archive templates. Done this way, editors can create new landing pages from existing sections without breaking the design.

The downsides are page weight and editor freedom. A poorly built Elementor site with nested containers, unused widgets and many add-on plugins becomes slow and inconsistent. Modern Elementor with flexbox containers and careful asset loading can still be fast. For agency builds, the same structure applies whether the work is white-label or direct, and Elementor development covers the service.

Option 2: Block theme with custom blocks and patterns

A block theme uses WordPress's own editor (Gutenberg) and full site editing. The Figma design becomes a theme.json file (colours, typography, spacing), block patterns for repeated sections and, where needed, custom blocks for complex components such as pricing tables or sliders. Editors work in the native editor with locked patterns that keep the design intact.

This approach produces very lean pages because there is no builder layer, and it follows where WordPress itself is going. It suits content-heavy sites, blogs and businesses that want fewer plugins. Block themes and full site editing and custom Gutenberg blocks explain the parts in more detail.

Option 3: Custom classic theme with custom fields

A classic custom theme turns each Figma template into PHP templates, with content entered through custom fields (for example, Advanced Custom Fields). Editors fill in forms (heading, image, list of features) and the theme controls the layout. Flexible content fields allow editors to stack predefined sections in any order.

This gives the closest match to the design and the most predictable result: editors can't accidentally change spacing or fonts. It is a strong fit for corporate sites, product catalogues and any project where brand consistency matters more than layout freedom. Custom WordPress theme development covers when it is worth the extra build time.

Option 4: Bricks and similar developer-focused builders

Bricks and similar builders offer visual editing with cleaner output, class-based styling and better control over markup than older builders. They suit developers who want visual speed without heavy pages, and clients who are happy with a slightly more technical editor. Check that whoever maintains the site later is comfortable with the builder, because the pool of developers is smaller than for Elementor or native blocks.

Option 5: Headless WordPress

Headless uses WordPress only as a content backend, with a separate front end built in a JavaScript framework. It can deliver very fast, app-like experiences and serve content to mobile apps too. The trade-offs are significant: higher build and hosting cost, loss of many plugin features (forms, SEO previews, page builders) and a dependency on developers for most changes. Headless WordPress for small businesses explains why most business sites don't need it.

What about automatic Figma-to-WordPress plugins?

Tools that export Figma frames straight into WordPress or a builder can save time on simple layouts, but the output usually needs heavy cleanup: absolute positioning, fixed widths that break on mobile, missing hover states and no reusable components. Treat them as a starting point for prototypes, not a production build.

How to prepare a Figma file for any approach

The build goes faster and closer to the design when the Figma file is ready for development:

  • Named colour and text styles that become global styles or theme.json values
  • Components and variants for buttons, cards, forms and navigation
  • Desktop, tablet and mobile frames for key templates, or clear notes on how layouts should collapse
  • Hover, focus, error and empty states
  • Real content length examples, including long headings and missing images
  • Exported assets or clear export settings for icons and images

The Figma to WordPress designer guide goes deeper on file preparation, and the agency developer handoff checklist covers what to send with the file.

How to choose the right approach

Answer four questions:

  1. Who edits the site after launch? A marketing team building new landing pages every month points to Elementor or blocks. A team that only updates text and images points to a custom theme.
  2. How strict is the design? If brand rules are tight, choose an approach that locks layouts (custom fields or locked patterns).
  3. How important is speed? For SEO-led sites or stores, block themes and custom themes have the best starting point. Elementor vs Gutenberg compares the two most common choices.
  4. Who maintains it? Choose something your future developer or agency can support easily.

How the choice affects cost and timeline

Builder-based builds are often quicker to start, especially when sections repeat. Custom themes and custom blocks take longer up front but tend to cost less to maintain, because there are fewer plugin dependencies and fewer chances for editors to break layouts. Headless is the most expensive to build and run. Ask for quotes on the same scope across approaches so you compare like with like.

How each approach handles the hard parts of a Figma design

Some design elements reveal the differences between approaches quickly. Sticky headers that change on scroll are simple in a custom theme, need custom CSS or a pro feature in builders, and need a small script in a block theme. Complex grids that rearrange on mobile are easiest in builders with per-device controls and in hand-written CSS. Repeatable content such as team members, case studies or product specifications is best handled with custom post types and fields in every approach, rather than hand-built on each page. Animations designed in Figma prototypes should be listed separately and agreed before the build, because heavy motion affects both speed and accessibility. Forms designed with custom error states need a form plugin that allows full styling. Ask your developer how they will handle each of these in the chosen approach before work starts, and get the answers in the quote.

Which approach gives the fastest pages?

Speed comes mostly from how carefully a site is built, but the starting point differs. Block themes and custom themes load only the CSS and scripts the page needs, so they start lean. Bricks and similar builders output relatively clean markup. Elementor adds more wrapper elements and scripts, though recent versions with containers and performance settings narrow the gap. In every approach, the biggest speed problems usually come from large images, too many fonts, sliders, third-party scripts and add-on plugins, not the build method itself. If speed is a priority, include Core Web Vitals targets in the brief and test the staging site on mobile before launch; WordPress speed optimisation covers how existing sites are improved.

Approaches for agencies handing off work

Agencies delivering Figma designs to a development partner should state the approach in the brief, not leave it to the developer. Clients who already use Elementor elsewhere, or have in-house marketers trained on it, are usually best kept on Elementor. Clients with strict brand guidelines and infrequent layout changes suit custom themes. New builds without legacy constraints are good candidates for block themes. Agreeing this up front avoids rework and makes quotes from different developers comparable.

Reviewing the build against the Figma file

Agree a review process before development begins. Review on the staging site at the same breakpoints as the Figma frames, list differences in one shared document with screenshots, and separate genuine bugs from design changes made after sign-off. Small differences in font rendering between Figma and browsers are normal; spacing, colours, component states and responsive behaviour should match. Two structured review rounds usually work better than many small rounds of feedback.

Editor training at handover

Whichever approach you pick, plan a short training session and a written guide covering how to add a page from existing sections, update menus, replace images at the right size and what not to change. A recorded screen walkthrough of the actual site is more useful than generic tutorials for the builder or editor.

What should stay the same whichever you choose

Every approach should deliver global styles that match the Figma tokens, reusable components, responsive layouts checked against each Figma frame, accessible markup (headings in order, alt text, focus states), compressed images, a staging site for review and a short handover guide for editors. FAQs

What is the best way to convert Figma to WordPress?

It depends on who will edit the site and how strict the design is. Elementor suits visual editing by marketers, block themes suit speed and native editing, and a custom theme suits strict layouts and long-term stability.

Is Elementor good for Figma to WordPress?

Yes, when built with global styles, templates and containers that mirror the Figma components. It is less suitable when the design must never change or when every millisecond of speed matters.

Should I use Gutenberg or Elementor for a Figma build?

Choose Gutenberg with custom blocks and patterns for leaner pages and native WordPress editing. Choose Elementor if your team already uses it and wants drag-and-drop page building.

Is Bricks better than Elementor?

Bricks often produces cleaner output and suits developers, while Elementor has a larger ecosystem and is easier for non-technical editors. The better choice depends on who builds and maintains the site.

Which Figma to WordPress approach gives the fastest pages?

Block themes with custom blocks and custom classic themes usually give the leanest pages, because they avoid a builder layer. A careful builder build can still reach good Core Web Vitals.

Can I switch approach later?

Yes, but it usually means rebuilding page layouts, since builder content doesn't transfer cleanly. Content such as posts and products moves easily; designed pages need to be rebuilt.
