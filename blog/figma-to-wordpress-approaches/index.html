Figma to WordPress: Elementor, Block Theme, Bricks or a Custom Theme? How to Choose the Build Approach

Figma to WordPress build approaches compared: Elementor, block themes, custom themes, Bricks and headless, with the trade-offs and how to choose.

Key takeaways
  • Elementor suits marketing sites that clients want to edit visually, a block theme with custom blocks suits lighter and more structured sites, and a custom theme suits complex or locked-down builds.
  • Bricks and similar builders give developers cleaner output but have smaller ecosystems, and automatic Figma export tools usually produce heavy, hard-to-edit layouts.
  • Choose by who edits the site after launch, how strict the speed targets are, budget and timeline, and who will maintain it later, then insist on the same design review, staging and pixel QA whichever you pick.

There are four realistic ways to turn a Figma design into WordPress: Elementor (usually with Elementor Pro) for marketing sites that clients want to edit visually; a block theme with custom blocks and patterns for lighter, more structured sites; a custom-coded theme with custom fields for complex or locked-down builds; and developer-focused builders such as Bricks for agencies that want cleaner output. The right choice depends mostly on who will edit the site after launch, how strict your speed targets are, the budget, and who will maintain it later.

For Figma to WordPress conversion I build in Elementor Pro for easy editing, or with Gutenberg blocks when a lighter setup suits the project better. This guide compares all the main options honestly, including the ones I don't use, so you can choose for your own project. If you are the designer preparing the file, read the Figma handoff guide alongside this one.

Option 1: Elementor and Elementor Pro

Elementor maps well onto a modern Figma file. Auto layout frames become Flexbox containers, colour and text styles become global colours and fonts, and repeated components become saved templates or Theme Builder parts such as headers, footers and blog templates, which need Elementor Pro.

  • Pros: the fastest route from design to working site, visual editing clients understand, and a huge pool of developers who can take over later.
  • Cons: heavier pages if built carelessly with deep nesting or several add-on packs, and editing freedom lets clients break layouts if global styles aren't used.
  • Best for: business and marketing sites where the client or agency wants to change pages without a developer.

Two habits keep Elementor builds healthy: use global styles for every colour and font, and add at most one well-maintained add-on, only when Elementor itself lacks a widget. Option 2: Block theme with custom blocks and patterns

A block theme uses WordPress's own editor. Your design tokens go into theme.json (colours, font sizes, spacing and layout widths), sections become block patterns, and anything the core blocks can't do becomes a custom block. Templates and patterns can be locked so clients edit text and images without moving the layout.

  • Pros: light output, no page builder dependency or licence, and a layout system that's hard for clients to break.
  • Cons: custom blocks take more development time, and editing is more structured than drag and drop.
  • Best for: performance-focused sites, content-heavy sites and long-term projects with a stable design system.

More on this approach: block themes explained and custom Gutenberg blocks. Elementor vs Gutenberg compares the two editors from the client's side.

Option 3: Custom classic theme with custom fields

A developer writes PHP templates for each page type and gives editors structured fields, often with a custom fields plugin and flexible content layouts. The front end contains only the code your design needs.

  • Pros: full control over markup and performance, precise implementation and tight editorial rules.
  • Cons: clients fill in fields rather than see the page as they edit, new layouts need a developer, and an undocumented theme ties you to whoever wrote it.
  • Best for: complex functionality, strict performance budgets and teams with ongoing developer support.

See custom WordPress theme development for when the extra build time pays off.

Option 4: Bricks and similar developer-focused builders

Bricks (a theme) and builder plugins such as Breakdance and Oxygen sit between a page builder and a custom theme. They favour class-based styling much like CSS, which suits designers who think in components, and they generally produce leaner markup than older page builders.

  • Pros: clean output, a workflow that rewards a disciplined design system, and good performance when built well.
  • Cons: smaller ecosystems and fewer developers to take a site over, an interface that's less forgiving for non-technical editors, and dependence on one vendor's roadmap.
  • Best for: agencies with in-house developers who standardise on one builder across many client sites.

Licence terms and features change, so check each builder's current plans before committing a client to it.

Option 5: Headless WordPress

WordPress manages content while a separate front end, such as a Next.js site, displays it. It gives complete design freedom and can be very fast, but it costs more to build and host, and many plugins for forms, SEO and previews need extra work. It is rarely needed for a small business site; see headless WordPress for small businesses.

What about automatic Figma-to-WordPress plugins?

Tools that export Figma frames straight into Elementor or HTML look like a shortcut. In practice they tend to produce deeply nested, fixed-size layouts that are heavy, hard to edit and awkward on mobile, because they copy positions rather than understanding the design system. Building by hand from your styles and components takes longer upfront but gives a lighter site that your client can actually maintain.

Also consider dynamic content. Blogs, case studies, team members and product catalogues need templates and custom post types whichever way you build, and WooCommerce has its own template layer.

How the choice affects cost and timeline

The number of unique templates matters more than the number of pages, because ten pages built from one template go much faster than ten different layouts. For the same design, Elementor is usually quickest, a block theme with custom blocks takes longer, and a custom-coded theme takes longest. Licences add a running cost for Elementor Pro and commercial builders, while block themes and custom themes rely on WordPress alone. Missing mobile frames, complex interactions and late content add time on every approach.

What should stay the same whichever you choose

  • A design review before quoting, with questions sent back as one list
  • Figma styles turned into global styles before any page is built
  • Headers, footers and repeating sections built as templates first
  • The build on a private staging site
  • Side-by-side comparison with the frames at every breakpoint, and testing on real phones
  • A walkthrough, a short editing note and every licence in your or your client's name

FAQs

What is the best way to convert Figma to WordPress?

For most business sites, a hand build in Elementor or a block theme, starting from the design system rather than individual frames. Elementor suits clients who want visual editing; a block theme suits sites where speed and consistent layouts matter most.

Is Elementor good for Figma to WordPress?

Yes, when built carefully. Auto layout maps onto Flexbox containers and styles onto global colours and fonts, so a well-prepared Figma file can be matched closely. The risks are deep nesting and too many add-ons, both avoidable.

Should I use Gutenberg or Elementor for a Figma build?

Choose Elementor if the client wants to rearrange layouts themselves. Choose Gutenberg with a block theme if you want lighter pages, no builder licence and layouts that clients can't easily break.

Is Bricks better than Elementor?

For developer-led builds, Bricks often produces cleaner markup. Elementor has the larger ecosystem, more developers who can take a site over, and an editor non-technical clients find easier. "Better" depends on who builds and who edits.

Which approach gives the fastest pages?

A custom theme or a block theme usually produces the lightest pages, because they load only what the design needs. A carefully built Elementor site can still pass Core Web Vitals; the difference is how much care it takes to keep it lean.

Can I switch approach later?

Only by rebuilding the affected pages, because each approach stores layouts differently. Content such as text, images and posts carries over; the layouts don't. It is worth choosing carefully at the start.
