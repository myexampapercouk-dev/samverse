WordPress User Roles and Capabilities Explained: Administrator, Editor, Author, Shop Manager and Who Should Get Which

WordPress user roles explained: what Administrators, Editors, Authors, Contributors and Shop Managers can do, and which role to give staff and developers.

Key takeaways
  • WordPress roles are bundles of capabilities: Administrators control everything, Editors manage all content, Authors publish their own posts, Contributors submit drafts and Subscribers only manage their profile.
  • Give each person the lowest role that fits, remember that Authors cannot edit pages, and use Shop Manager rather than Administrator for WooCommerce staff.
  • Keep administrators to the owner and the current developer, protect Editor and Shop Manager accounts with 2FA, and remove access the day someone leaves.

A standard WordPress site has five user roles: Administrator, Editor, Author, Contributor and Subscriber. Each role is a bundle of permissions, called capabilities, that decides what a person can do, from installing plugins and changing settings (Administrator) down to editing only their own profile (Subscriber). The safe rule is simple: give each person the lowest role that lets them do their job, keep administrators to a minimum, and never share one login between several people.

This guide explains what each role can and can't do, the extra roles that WooCommerce and SEO plugins add, and which role suits staff, writers, agencies and developers. Checking who has administrator access is part of the first health check in my WordPress maintenance plans, because a forgotten admin account is one of the easiest ways into a site.

The five default WordPress roles at a glance

RoleIn plain termsTypical person
AdministratorFull control: settings, plugins, themes, users and all contentBusiness owner; the current developer, on their own account
EditorPublishes and edits all posts and pages, including other people's, and moderates commentsMarketing manager, office staff who update service pages
AuthorWrites, publishes and edits their own posts and uploads images, but can't touch pagesRegular in-house blogger
ContributorWrites and edits their own drafts, but can't publish or upload filesGuest writer, intern
SubscriberReads the site and edits their own profileRegistered members

A WordPress multisite network adds a sixth role, Super Admin, who manages every site in the network and installs plugins and themes for all of them. On multisite, ordinary site Administrators can't install plugins or themes at all.

What each role can actually do

Role names are only labels. What matters is the capabilities behind them. This is how the default roles compare on a standard single-site installation:

CapabilityAdministratorEditorAuthorContributorSubscriber
Write postsYesYesYesDrafts onlyNo
Publish postsYesYesOwn postsNoNo
Edit or delete other people's postsYesYesNoNoNo
Create and edit pagesYesYesNoNoNo
Upload images and filesYesYesYesNoNo
Moderate all comments and manage categoriesYesYesNoNoNo
Add unfiltered HTML, including scriptsYesYesNoNoNo
Install plugins and themes, change settingsYesNoNoNoNo
Add, edit and delete usersYesNoNoNoNo

Two rows that catch people out

Authors can't edit pages. If a receptionist needs to update clinic timings on the Contact page, or a manager changes prices on a service page, they need Editor. Author only covers blog posts.

Editors can add scripts. On a single site, Editors have the same "unfiltered HTML" capability as Administrators, so they can paste embed codes and JavaScript into content. That's handy for maps and booking widgets, but it also means a stolen Editor password can be used to inject malicious code into your pages. Treat Editor accounts as sensitive. A developer can remove the capability for everyone with one line in wp-config.php, though some page builder widgets expect it, so test on staging first:

define( 'DISALLOW_UNFILTERED_HTML', true );

Roles added by WooCommerce, SEO and page builder plugins

WooCommerce: Shop Manager and Customer

WooCommerce adds two roles. Shop Manager can manage products, orders, coupons, reports and WooCommerce settings, and edit customer accounts, without being able to install plugins, change the theme or manage other staff accounts. It also includes most Editor capabilities for posts and pages. Customer is given to shoppers who create an account: it's Subscriber plus their orders, addresses and account details on the My Account page.

Staff who pack orders or answer customer queries should be Shop Managers, not Administrators. Shop Managers can still see every customer's name, address and phone number, so protect those accounts too; the WooCommerce security checklist covers staff accounts and customer data.

SEO plugins

Yoast SEO adds two roles at the time of writing: SEO Manager, who can change the plugin's settings, and SEO Editor, who can edit the SEO fields on posts and pages. Both are built on the Editor role, so they can edit content as well. Rank Math takes a different approach, with a Role Manager that lets you choose which of its features each existing role can use.

Elementor

Elementor has its own Role Manager that can stop chosen roles opening the Elementor editor, and Elementor Pro adds an option to let a role edit content without changing the design, at the time of writing. That suits staff who should update text and images without moving sections around.

Which role to give staff, agencies and developers

  • You, the business owner: Administrator, on a personal account with your own email address. Own at least one admin account even if you rarely log in, so a developer who disappears can't lock you out. If that has already happened, see regaining access from an old developer.
  • Your developer or maintenance provider: Administrator, on their own named account, for as long as the work lasts. For a one-off fix, create the account for the job and remove it afterwards.
  • A marketing or SEO agency: Editor usually covers content and SEO fields. Give Administrator only if they genuinely need to install a plugin or change settings, then downgrade them.
  • Office staff who update timings, prices or team pages: Editor, limited through Elementor's Role Manager if the site uses it.
  • An in-house blogger: Author. Guest writers and interns: Contributor, so someone reviews every post before it goes live. Contributors can't upload images, so the reviewing editor adds them.
  • Store staff: Shop Manager.
  • An accountant who needs sales figures: Shop Manager gives far more than they need. A monthly order export, or a reports-only custom role, is safer.

Whatever the role, each person gets their own account. Shared logins make it impossible to remove one person without changing the password for everyone, and activity logs can't tell you who did what.

How to change a user's role

  1. Go to Users > All Users and click the person's name.
  2. Choose the new role from the Role dropdown.
  3. Click Update User, then ask them to log out and back in.

To change several people at once, tick them on the Users list, pick a role from Change role to and click Change. WordPress won't let you remove your own ability to manage users from this screen, which stops you locking yourself out by accident.

Also check Settings > General. "New User Default Role" should be Subscriber, or Customer on a store, and never Administrator. Untick "Anyone can register" unless members or customers genuinely need to sign up. Some attacks switch both settings so that anyone can register as an administrator, so an unexpected change here is a red flag. If bots are already creating accounts, see stopping spam user registrations.

Developers with SSH access can review and change roles with WP-CLI:

wp user list --fields=ID,user_login,user_email,roles
wp user set-role 12 editor
wp role list
wp cap list editor

Custom roles: when the defaults don't fit

Sometimes no default role fits: an Editor who shouldn't delete published pages, or someone who only needs WooCommerce reports. Plugins such as User Role Editor or Members let you create roles with exactly the capabilities you choose. A few cautions:

  • Clone a role, don't edit the default. Copy Editor to a new "Content Manager" role and change the copy, so the standard roles still behave as everyone expects.
  • Changes outlive the plugin. Roles and capabilities are stored in the database, so deactivating the plugin doesn't undo what you changed.
  • Test as that user. A plugin such as User Switching lets an administrator view the dashboard as another user, so you can confirm they see what they need and nothing more.
  • Keep a note of each custom role and why it exists, for whoever looks after the site next.

Roles are only half of access control

A sensible role limits the damage a stolen password can do, but the account still needs protecting:

  • Two-factor authentication on every Administrator, Editor and Shop Manager account; see how to secure your WordPress login.
  • Remove or downgrade accounts the same day someone leaves or a project ends. For a full clean-up of old accounts, follow the WordPress user accounts audit.
  • An activity log that records logins, role changes and new users; see WordPress activity logs.
  • Treat any administrator nobody recognises as a sign the site may be compromised. Note its details before deleting it, because the registration date can show when the break-in happened.

FAQs

What is the difference between an Editor and an Author in WordPress?

An Author can write, publish and edit only their own posts, and can't touch pages. An Editor can publish and edit every post and page on the site, including other people's, and moderate comments. Give Author to writers and Editor to whoever manages the site's content.

Which WordPress role should I give my web developer?

Administrator, on a separate account in their own name, for as long as the work lasts. Remove or downgrade it when the job ends, and never hand over your own login.

Can an Editor install plugins or change themes?

No. Plugins, themes, settings and user management are Administrator-only on a standard site. Editors can, however, add scripts and embed codes to content on a single site, so protect their accounts as carefully as admin accounts.

What can a Shop Manager do in WooCommerce?

Manage products, orders, coupons, reports and WooCommerce settings, and edit customer accounts. They can't install plugins, change the theme or manage other staff accounts, which makes the role right for store staff.

How many administrators should a WordPress site have?

As few as possible. For most small businesses that's the owner plus the developer or maintenance provider currently looking after the site. Every extra admin account is another password that could leak.

Why can't a user see a menu they need?

Their role probably lacks the capability for that screen, or a plugin has hidden the menu. Check the role first. If an Administrator is missing menus or sees "Sorry, you are not allowed to access this page", see fixing missing dashboard menus.
