Search Console Page Indexing Errors: What Each Status Means and What to Do

Search Console page indexing errors explained: what crawled or discovered not indexed, duplicate, noindex, 404 and 5xx mean, and which ones to fix.

Key takeaways
  • Not every "Why pages aren't indexed" reason is a problem: first decide whether each URL should be in Google at all.
  • Crawled or discovered but not indexed are usually quality, duplication or internal linking issues, not technical errors you can switch off.
  • Fix real errors (5xx, 404s in the sitemap, accidental noindex or robots blocks) first, then validate the fix and check the report monthly.

Search Console page indexing errors are the reasons Google lists under Indexing > Pages for URLs it has not added to its index. Many are normal: redirects, pages you set to noindex, duplicates that point to a canonical version. The ones that need action are server errors (5xx), 404s on pages that should exist, accidental noindex or robots.txt blocks on important pages, and important pages stuck in "Crawled" or "Discovered – currently not indexed". Sort each reason by "should this page be indexed?" before fixing anything.

Below, each status in plain words, with what to do about it on a typical WordPress or Shopify business site.

First, ask: should this page be indexed?

The Pages report shows every URL Google knows about, including ones you never meant to be found: tag archives, attachment pages, cart and account pages, filtered shop URLs, old URLs from a previous site. A high "not indexed" count is not a problem in itself.

Change the filter at the top from "All known pages" to "All submitted pages". That shows only URLs from your sitemap, the ones you said matter. Problems in that view deserve attention first. If the sitemap itself lists junk, tidy it using XML sitemaps explained.

Crawled – currently not indexed

Google fetched the page and decided not to include it, at least for now. There is no switch for this; it is Google's judgement about value. Common causes on business sites:

  • Thin pages: short location or service pages that repeat each other with the city name swapped.
  • Near-duplicates: product variants, tag archives or blog posts that cover the same topic as another page.
  • Weak internal links: pages only reachable from the sitemap or deep in pagination.
  • New site, low trust: new domains often see this for many pages at first.

What helps: make the page clearly more useful than similar ones, merge overlapping pages, link to it from relevant, already-indexed pages, and then request indexing once. Duplicate content explained helps decide what to merge.

Discovered – currently not indexed

Google knows the URL, usually from the sitemap or a link, but has not crawled it yet. On small sites this often clears with time. If it persists:

  • Add internal links from pages Google already crawls often, such as the homepage, service pages and popular posts.
  • Check server speed. Google crawls less on slow or erroring sites.
  • Make sure the sitemap is not flooded with low-value URLs that compete for crawling.

Duplicate and canonical statuses

A canonical tag tells Google which version of similar pages is the main one. "Alternate page with proper canonical tag" means it is working. The two duplicate statuses mean Google is choosing for you, or disagreeing with you.

  • Make sure every page has a self-referencing canonical, which SEO plugins such as Yoast and Rank Math add by default.
  • Use one URL format everywhere: https, www or not, trailing slash or not.
  • When Google picks a different canonical, compare the two pages. If they are nearly identical, Google sees them as one page; make them distinct or merge them.

Canonical tags explained goes into the details.

Excluded on purpose, or by accident

Noindex and robots.txt blocks are correct for cart, checkout, account, thank-you and internal search pages. They are a serious problem on service pages or the whole site. Classic accidents:

  • WordPress's "Discourage search engines from indexing this site" left ticked after launch (Settings > Reading).
  • A staging robots.txt or noindex setting copied to the live site.
  • An SEO plugin set to noindex a whole post type or category.

Note that robots.txt blocks crawling, not indexing, and Google cannot see a noindex tag on a blocked page. To remove a page from Google, allow crawling and use noindex. See robots.txt explained and "Indexed, though blocked by robots.txt".

Errors that need fixing quickly

  • Server errors (5xx): if Google keeps hitting errors, it slows crawling and may drop pages. Check hosting resource limits and error logs for the dates shown.
  • 404s on real pages: often broken permalinks after a migration; fixing WordPress 404 pages and permalinks covers the usual fix. For pages that moved, add a 301 redirect; see 301 vs 302 redirects.
  • Redirect errors: loops and long chains, often from HTTPS or www rules fighting a plugin.
  • Soft 404s: out-of-stock product pages with nothing on them, empty category pages, or search results pages.

404s for URLs that never should have existed, such as spam URLs or old junk, can be left alone. Google drops them over time.

Common patterns on WordPress and Shopify sites

Some indexing reasons show up again and again on particular platforms. Knowing them saves time when you open the report:

  • WordPress attachment pages: each uploaded image can get its own thin page. Yoast and Rank Math can redirect these to the file or parent post; once they do, they move to "Page with redirect", which is fine.
  • WordPress tag and author archives: often thin or duplicated. Either make them useful or set them to noindex and remove them from the sitemap.
  • WooCommerce filters and sorting: URLs with parameters such as orderby or filter values create many near-identical pages. They usually appear as duplicates or "Crawled – currently not indexed".
  • Shopify collection product URLs: Shopify can link products through collection paths as well as the main product path. The canonical points to the main product URL, so "Alternate page with proper canonical tag" is expected.
  • Shopify filter and search pages: often blocked by Shopify's default robots.txt, so "Blocked by robots.txt" for them is normal.
  • Old URLs after a redesign or migration: 404s for pages that moved without a redirect. These are worth fixing if the old pages had links or traffic.

When the numbers in one of these groups grow quickly, it is usually a settings change rather than a Google problem.

To find which of your own pages still link to those missing URLs, and to decide between updating the link, a redirect or a 410, see how to find and fix broken links on WordPress.

Fixing, validating and checking monthly

  1. Click a reason in the report to see example URLs.
  2. Use URL Inspection on one example to see Google's last crawl, the canonical it chose and whether the live page can be indexed.
  3. Fix the cause across the site, not just the example.
  4. Click Validate fix. Validation can take days or weeks, and it re-checks every URL in that group.
  5. Request indexing only for a few important pages; it does not speed up whole groups.

Once a month, check the submitted-pages view and the trend of indexed pages. A sudden drop usually means an accidental noindex, a robots change or server errors after an update.

FAQs

How do I fix Search Console page indexing errors?

Filter to submitted pages, decide which URLs should be indexed, fix real errors such as 5xx, 404s on live pages and accidental noindex, improve thin or duplicate pages, then click Validate fix.

Is "Crawled – currently not indexed" an error?

Not a technical error. It means Google chose not to index the page yet, usually for quality, duplication or weak internal links. Improving the page and linking to it is the fix.

Should I worry about a high number of not indexed pages?

Only if they are pages you want in Google. Redirects, noindexed pages, duplicates with canonicals and junk URLs are expected to be in the not indexed list.

How long does Validate fix take?

It varies with the number of URLs and how often Google crawls your site, from days to several weeks. You get an email when it finishes.

Does requesting indexing guarantee a page gets indexed?

No. It asks Google to crawl the page soon. Google still decides whether to index it.
