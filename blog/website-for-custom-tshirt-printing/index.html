Websites for Custom T-Shirt Printing and Merchandise Businesses

What a custom T-shirt printing website needs: print-method pages, design uploads, minimum orders, bulk quote forms, proof approval and honest delivery times.

Key takeaways
  • Explain each print method, such as screen printing, DTF and embroidery, so customers can choose the right one.
  • State minimum order quantities clearly and use a bulk quote form that asks for sizes, print positions and deadlines.
  • Make written proof approval a fixed step before printing, and give delivery timelines you can reliably meet.

People ordering custom T-shirts range from someone wanting one birthday shirt to an HR team ordering embroidered polos for a whole office. They all want to know the same few things: can you print my design on the product I want, how many do I have to order, what will it look like, and will it arrive in time? A website that answers those clearly turns browsing into orders and quote requests, and cuts down the back-and-forth on WhatsApp.

This guide is for T-shirt and merchandise printers. For cartons, labels and commercial printing, see websites for printing and packaging companies.

Who orders, and what each buyer needs

BuyerTypical orderWhat they ask
IndividualsOne or a few shirts for gifts or occasionsCan I order just one? How long will it take?
College clubs and festsBatch T-shirts and hoodiesCan you work from our sizes list and deliver before the event?
CompaniesLogo polos, joining kits and event merchandiseWill the logo look right? Can we get a GST invoice?
Sports teamsJerseys with names and numbersHow are names handled? Will the print last?
Brands and creatorsMerchandise to sellQuality, consistency and repeat orders

Give each a clear path from the homepage: "Order online" for small quantities and "Get a bulk quote" for larger ones.

Product pages for garments and merchandise

Give each product its own page: round-neck and polo T-shirts, hoodies, sports jerseys, caps, and any mugs, bags or other items you genuinely offer. On each page include:

  • Fabric, GSM (fabric weight) and fit, with real photos in good light
  • Available colours, ideally photographed rather than shown as flat swatches alone
  • A size chart with actual garment measurements, not just S, M and L
  • Which print methods work on that product, and where on it you can print
  • Care instructions so prints last

If you sell online, set sizes and colours up as proper variations so stock and pricing stay accurate; see WooCommerce product variations.

Explain your print methods

Most customers don't know the difference between screen printing and DTF, and they shouldn't have to. A page for each method, plus a simple comparison, helps them choose and shows you know your craft:

MethodGenerally suitsWorth explaining
Screen printingLarger runs of one design with a few solid coloursEach colour needs its own screen, so setup makes very small runs uneconomical
DTF (direct-to-film)Full-colour designs and small quantities on many fabric typesHow the print feels on the fabric and how to wash it
EmbroideryLogos on polos, caps and jacketsLogos are digitised into a stitch file, and very fine detail or tiny text may need simplifying
SublimationAll-over prints, such as sports jerseysNeeds polyester or high-polyester fabric in light colours, not cotton

Only list methods you do in-house or reliably outsource, and say which is which if it affects timelines.

Design uploads and online mock-ups

Let customers upload artwork on the order or quote form, and say which files you accept. Vector files such as AI, EPS, SVG or PDF are ideal for logos, and high-resolution PNG files with transparent backgrounds work for most other designs. Explain what happens if a file isn't print-ready, such as whether you can redraw a logo and whether that costs extra.

An online designer, where customers add text and images to a product preview, can work well for individual orders. There are product customiser plugins for WooCommerce as well as standalone design tools; test how they behave on phones, since that's where many customers will use them. Make clear that on-screen previews are indicative and that colours can look different on fabric.

State that customers must own or have permission to use the artwork they upload, and that you may decline designs using other brands' logos or characters.

Minimum orders and bulk quote forms

Unclear minimum order quantities waste everyone's time. State them per product and method, for example whether you accept single pieces with DTF but need a minimum for screen printing or embroidery. If the price per piece drops as quantity rises, say so, even if you don't publish exact figures.

For corporate and bulk orders, a quote form should ask for:

  • Product, colour and print method, with a "not sure, please advise" option
  • Total quantity and the size breakdown, with the option to upload a spreadsheet
  • Print positions, such as front, back and sleeve, and the number of colours
  • Names and numbers for team jerseys
  • Required delivery date and delivery location
  • Artwork upload, and whether a GST invoice is needed

Send enquiries to email and WhatsApp so someone replies quickly; see request-for-quote forms for how to follow up.

Proof approval before printing

Many reprint disputes come from a design nobody checked properly. Make proof approval a clear step in your process, and explain it on the website:

  1. You send a digital proof showing the design on the garment, with placement, print size and colours
  2. The customer checks spelling, names, numbers and the size breakdown
  3. They approve in writing, by email or WhatsApp, and production starts only then
  4. For large orders, you offer a physical sample first, if you can

Say what happens if changes are requested after approval, and keep a record of every approved proof.

Delivery timelines, stated honestly

Deadlines drive this business: the fest is on Friday and the offsite starts on Monday. Be honest about timelines so customers can plan:

  • Explain that production time starts after proof approval and payment, not from the first enquiry
  • Give typical production times by method and quantity, as ranges you reliably meet
  • Offer rush orders only if you can genuinely deliver them, and mention any extra charge
  • State shipping options and typical courier times, with tracking
  • Flag busy periods, such as college fest season and year-end corporate orders, and suggest ordering early

A next-day promise you miss once can cost you more in reviews than an honest timeline ever loses.

Trust, local SEO and speed

  • A gallery of real orders, with clients' permission before showing their logos
  • Reviews on Google and your own site, and a complete Google Business Profile
  • Pages for searches like "custom T-shirt printing in Pune", "corporate polo T-shirts with logo" or "embroidered caps"
  • Optimised product images, since galleries and mock-up tools can slow pages down on mobile data
