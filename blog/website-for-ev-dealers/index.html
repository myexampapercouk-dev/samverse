Websites for Electric Vehicle Dealers and Showrooms: Model Pages, Test Rides, Charging, Subsidies and Service

Plan an EV dealer website: model pages that explain range honestly, test ride booking, on-road price enquiries, charging and subsidy pages, and service booking.

Key takeaways
  • First-time EV buyers need explanations more than brochures: give every model a page that explains range, charging time and battery warranty in plain words, with the test basis stated.
  • Never publish on-road prices or subsidy amounts you cannot keep current; use an on-road price form, link subsidy information to official sources and date every figure you show.
  • Turn the site into a sales and service tool: test ride booking, a charging guide, a running cost calculator with visible assumptions, and service and battery health bookings.

An electric vehicle dealer's website has to do something a petrol showroom's site rarely does: teach. Many visitors are buying their first EV, so before they book a test ride they want to understand range, charging at home, battery life, warranty, running costs and subsidies. The sites that win those buyers give every model a page that explains these in plain words, take on-road price and test ride requests on WhatsApp, keep subsidy information linked to official sources, and make service and battery checks easy to book after the sale.

This guide is for EV showrooms selling electric scooters, motorcycles, cars, three-wheelers or a mix, whether single-brand authorised dealers or multi-brand stores. Model pages that explain, not just list

Pasting the brochure's specification list is not enough for a first-time EV buyer. For each model and variant, include:

  • Range, explained: the certified figure with its test basis noted, and a short line on what affects real range, such as speed, load, riding mode, terrain, traffic and temperature
  • Battery: capacity, chemistry if the manufacturer states it, and whether the battery is fixed or removable
  • Charging: home charging time and fast charging support, as published by the manufacturer, and whether a charger is included or sold separately
  • Warranty: battery, motor and vehicle warranties listed separately, with conditions linked rather than paraphrased
  • Variants and colours, with your own showroom photos alongside official images where you are allowed to use them
  • Buttons: "Get on-road price", "Book a test ride" and WhatsApp

Organise models by how people use them, not only by name: daily commuting, family riding, delivery work or cargo. Sahni Power Solutions, a generator rental company I built a website for, groups its fleet into capacity bands, each with a page on typical uses and the questions that size raises. An EV range can be organised the same way, so a delivery rider and a college student each land on the models that suit them.

Low-speed scooters need precise wording

Some low-speed electric scooters are sold as not needing registration or a driving licence. If you sell them, describe each model's category exactly as the manufacturer's approval states, and check the current rules with your RTO, because buyers, and parents buying for teenagers, rely on what you write.

On-road prices and subsidies, handled carefully

EV prices move more than most: manufacturer revisions, changes to central and state incentives, registration and road tax rules that differ between states, insurance and accessories all affect what a buyer finally pays. Publishing a figure you cannot keep current leads to arguments at the counter.

  • Use a "Get on-road price" form asking for the model, variant, city or PIN code and when they plan to buy, and reply with an itemised quote on WhatsApp
  • If you show any price, label it clearly as ex-showroom, add a last-updated date and a note that it may change
  • On the subsidies page, explain in general terms that central and state schemes, and road tax or registration concessions in some states, may apply, then link to the official sources and say who to ask

Subsidies change often, so tell buyers to check the current state and central schemes rather than relying on a figure from last year. For solar companies' subsidy pages, I link to the official portal and add a last-checked date to any figure shown; the same approach suits an EV showroom. Test rides and test drives

For many first-time buyers, the test ride is where hesitation about range and power disappears. The booking form should ask for the model, a preferred date and time slot, and whether they want a showroom or home test ride, if you offer one. A home test ride has an extra advantage for EVs: your executive can look at where the vehicle would be parked and charged. Show which models are available to ride, ask riders to bring a valid licence where one is needed, and confirm on WhatsApp with a reminder on the day. A charging guide page

"Where will I charge it?" is often the question that stops a sale. A dedicated charging page answers it once, properly:

  • Home charging: what kind of socket or wall charger each model needs, and why an electrician should check the wiring first
  • Apartments: charging in a basement or society parking usually needs the society's permission and a metered point; suggest buyers ask their society early
  • Removable batteries: how charging indoors works for models that allow it
  • Public charging: the manufacturer's charging network or app, if any, and how to find chargers nearby
  • Charger installation: if you install home chargers, a short enquiry form for it

If charging becomes a business of its own, websites for EV charging stations and charger installers covers station pages and installation enquiries.

Running cost explainers and calculators

A running cost calculator comparing an EV with a petrol vehicle can help buyers, but only if it is honest. Show every assumption on the page: distance per day, electricity tariff, the vehicle's energy use and the petrol price used for comparison, and let visitors change them. Label every result as an estimate, and avoid headline claims such as a fixed saving per month. The calculator can pass the visitor's figures into the enquiry form, so your sales team sees them. Service, battery health and after-sales

EV buyers worry about service more than petrol buyers, because the technology is new to them. A service section should cover:

  • Online service booking with model, registration number, issue and preferred slot
  • Scheduled services as per the manufacturer's schedule, and what an EV service involves
  • Battery health checks and software updates, if your workshop provides them
  • Roadside assistance, as the manufacturer or your dealership provides it, with the number to call
  • Spares, accessories, helmets and chargers, even as an enquiry catalogue

If you also service petrol vehicles or sell used ones, websites for car dealers and workshops covers used vehicle listings and workshop pages.

Commercial EVs and fleet buyers

E-rickshaw operators, delivery companies and businesses buying cargo three-wheelers or electric vans ask different questions: payload, daily range under load, charging during shifts, battery swapping where available, financing for several vehicles and service turnaround. Give them their own page and a fleet enquiry form asking for the use, number of vehicles, routes or daily distance and the city, rather than sending them through the retail test ride flow.

OEM brand guidelines and authorised status

If you are an authorised dealer, your agreement and the manufacturer's brand guidelines usually control the use of the brand name and logo, which official images and specifications you may use, and whether offers must be approved before publishing. Check them before the build. Don't publish a national offer that does not apply at your showroom, and if you are a multi-brand store, never imply you are authorised for a brand you simply stock.

Local search for EV showrooms

  • Complete Google Business Profiles for the showroom and, if separate, the service centre
  • Use searches people actually type in page titles, such as "electric scooter showroom in Hadapsar" or "{brand} EV dealer in {city}"
  • Post new launches and test ride events on your profile, and ask real buyers for reviews after delivery
  • Run launch and festive campaigns on dedicated landing pages, and track calls and WhatsApp clicks

Common mistakes on EV dealer websites

  • Range figures with no test basis, or "real range" claims nobody measured
  • Old subsidy amounts still on the site months after a scheme changed
  • Savings promised as fact rather than shown as an estimate
  • No charging information, so the biggest objection goes unanswered
  • Test rides offered on models that are not in the showroom
  • Brand logos and offers used outside the manufacturer's guidelines

FAQs

Should an EV dealer show prices on the website?

Ex-showroom prices with a last-updated date can help, but on-road prices vary with state rules, incentives and insurance, so most dealers do better with a quick on-road price form answered on WhatsApp.

How should subsidy information be shown?

Explain in general terms which kinds of scheme may apply, link to the official central and state sources, and date any figure you show. Schemes change, so ask buyers to check the current position.

Can buyers book a test ride online?

Yes. A form with model, date, time slot and showroom or home test ride, confirmed on WhatsApp with a reminder, works well. Only list models you actually have available to ride.

Is a running cost calculator worth adding?

It can help hesitant buyers, as long as the assumptions are visible and editable and every result is labelled an estimate. Figures should come from you and the manufacturer, not guesses.

Can authorised dealers have their own website?

Usually, within the manufacturer's brand guidelines. Check your dealership agreement for rules on the domain name, logo use, official content and offers before you build.
