One-Page vs Multi-Page Website: When a Single Page Works, When You Need More, and What Each Means for SEO, Cost and Growth

One-page vs multi-page website: when a single page works for events, one service or campaigns, when you need more pages for SEO, and a decision table.

Key takeaways
  • A one-page website suits one offer, one audience and one action, such as an event, a single service, a launch or a campaign, or a quick start on a small budget.
  • Choose a multi-page website when you offer several services, serve several areas or audiences, or want to be found on Google for more than your name, because each page can target its own search.
  • You can start with one page and grow: plan sections that can become their own pages later, and keep the homepage URL and section links working when you do.

A one-page website works when you have one offer, one audience and one action: an event, a single service, a product launch, an ad campaign, or a new business that needs something credible online quickly on a small budget. A multi-page website is the better choice when you offer several services, serve several areas or types of customer, or want to be found on Google for more than your business name, because each page can target its own search. Many businesses start with one page and grow into more, and that works well if the site is planned for it.

When I plan a site through WordPress website development, every main service gets its own page, because each service is a separate search. A single page still has its place, though. For a campaign that needs one message and one goal, with conversion tracking set up, landing page design is the right tool.

A one-page website isn't quite the same as a landing page. A one-page site is your whole website on one scrolling page, usually with a menu that jumps to each section. A landing page is built for one campaign, usually with no menu at all. Landing page vs website covers that difference in depth.

When a one-page website works well

  • Events: a conference, exhibition, wedding, workshop or festival, where visitors need the date, place, programme and a way to register.
  • A single service or product: one clear offer to one type of customer, such as a home tutor in one subject or a single product brand.
  • A launch or campaign: a new product, a pre-launch sign-up or a seasonal offer with a fixed end date.
  • A new business: something credible online while the full site is planned, with your services, proof and contact details.
  • A personal profile: a consultant, speaker or freelancer who mainly needs people who already know their name to find them.
  • A very small budget, where one well-made page beats five thin ones.

A one-page site works well when visitors arrive already knowing your name or your offer, from a referral, a visiting card, social media or an ad, rather than from a Google search for something specific.

When you need a multi-page website

  • Several services. A clinic with five treatments, an installer offering sales, repairs and maintenance contracts, or a manufacturer with several product families.
  • Several areas or branches, where each branch or service area deserves its own genuinely useful page.
  • Search beyond your name. If you want to appear when people search for a service plus your city, each service needs a page built around that search.
  • Detail buyers need: specifications, menus, fee structures, treatment explanations, case studies or downloadable brochures.
  • Different audiences, such as retail customers and trade buyers, or patients and referring doctors, who need different information.
  • Trust and content: an About page, team profiles, case studies and a blog all need room.

SEO: why one page struggles to rank for many searches

Google ranks pages, not whole websites. A page has one title, one main heading and one main topic, so it can match a handful of closely related searches well. A one-page site for an AC company that covers repair, installation, annual maintenance contracts and fridge repair has to squeeze four different searches into one title and one page, and usually ranks for none of them as well as a competitor with a page for each.

  • Sections aren't separate pages. Links such as yourdomain.com/#services jump to a section of the same page; Google generally treats them as one URL.
  • Depth is limited. Each service gets a paragraph or two on a one-page site, while a dedicated page can answer the questions people actually ask, with FAQs and examples.
  • Fewer ways in. Every extra useful page is another page that can appear in search results and another page other sites can link to.

A one-page site can still rank for your business name and one core service, especially for a local business with a strong Google Business Profile. Area pages need care: pages that only swap the city name are thin, and local landing pages done right explains how to make them genuinely useful.

User experience and speed

Scrolling feels natural on a phone, which is one reason one-page sites can work well on mobile. The risk is length: as more sections, photos and videos are added, the page grows heavy and slow, and visitors lose their place. Keep a one-page site focused, use a sticky call or WhatsApp button, and give the jump menu clear labels.

Multi-page sites avoid the length problem, because each page loads only what it needs, but they depend on clear navigation. How to structure your website navigation covers menus that help visitors and search engines.

Tracking: what each tells you

On a one-page site, every visit is to the same URL, so page reports in Google Analytics say very little about what interested people. Track clicks on the jump menu, scroll depth, and form submissions, calls and WhatsApp clicks as key events, so you can see which sections lead to enquiries. On a multi-page site, page-level data shows which services people look at and which pages lead to enquiries. GA4 events explained shows how to set up both.

Cost and upkeep

A one-page site is usually cheaper because there's one layout to design and less content to write. As a guide, the WordPress cost guide puts a professionally built landing page at ₹5,000 to ₹15,000, a small business site of five to eight pages at ₹15,000 to ₹40,000, and a larger business or B2B site at ₹35,000 to ₹80,000. Domain, hosting and upkeep are much the same either way. The bigger difference is your time: every extra page needs content, photos and someone to keep it current. Start with one page, grow later

A one-page site doesn't have to be a dead end. If you plan for growth:

  1. Build on a platform you can grow on, such as WordPress, rather than a tool that only makes single pages.
  2. Write each section as a future page: one section per service, each with its own heading, so it can later become a page of its own.
  3. When you add pages, move each service's content to its own page and expand it, then keep a short summary on the homepage that links to it.
  4. Keep the homepage address unchanged, and keep the old section anchors, so links people saved or shared still land in the right place.
  5. Add the new pages to the menu and sitemap, and link to them from the homepage summary.

What a good one-page website includes

  • A clear headline saying what you do and for whom, with a call to action visible without scrolling.
  • Services or the offer, in short sections with their own headings.
  • Proof: real photos, testimonials used with permission, client logos or project examples.
  • A short About section and a FAQ answering common questions.
  • Contact details, a form, a map if you have a location, and hours.
  • A sticky WhatsApp or call button on mobile, designed with mobile-first design in mind.
  • A separate privacy policy page, which even a one-page site usually needs if it collects enquiries.

FAQs

Is a one-page website bad for SEO?

Not bad, but limited. It can rank for your business name and one main topic. If you want to be found for several services or areas, separate pages give each one a much better chance.

How many pages should a small business website have?

Enough for one page per main service plus Home, About and Contact. For many small businesses that means five to eight pages, but it depends on what you sell, not on a target number.

Can I turn a one-page website into a multi-page one later?

Yes. Move each section's content to its own page, expand it, keep a summary on the homepage that links to each new page, and keep the homepage address the same.

Is a one-page website the same as a landing page?

No. A one-page website is your whole site on one page, with a menu to its sections. A landing page is built for one campaign, usually without a menu, and is often kept out of Google if it exists only for ads.

Is a one-page website cheaper?

Usually, because there's one layout to design and less content to prepare. The savings shrink if the page has to do a lot, and a single page built to a high standard can cost more than a quick multi-page site built from a template.
