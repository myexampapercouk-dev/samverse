Websites for Catering Services: Event Pages, Menu Packages, Per-Plate Pricing, Tasting Sessions and Enquiry Forms That Win Wedding and Corporate Orders

Plan a catering website that wins event orders: menu packages, per-plate pricing shown or on request, tastings, event galleries and a smart enquiry form.

Key takeaways
  • Design the enquiry form first: event type, date, guest count, venue, service style, cuisine and dietary needs, and a budget range give you enough to quote without a long phone call.
  • Show menu packages people can compare, and decide openly whether per-plate prices are published, shown as a starting price or given on request, with what the price includes.
  • Back the food with proof: tastings for larger bookings, a gallery of past events labelled by type and scale, hygiene practices you actually follow and your FSSAI details.

A catering website has one job above all others: to turn "we have a function sometime in March" into an enquiry you can quote on. That takes event pages that show you have done this kind of function before, menu packages with a clear idea of cost per plate, a tasting option for big bookings, and a form that asks for the guest count, date, venue and cuisine before you pick up the phone.

Caterers fit naturally with the food businesses I build for under websites for restaurants and food brands: menus as real web pages rather than PDFs, fast photo galleries, WhatsApp and Google Maps. For wedding-season ads or a corporate meals campaign, a separate landing page with conversion tracking can run alongside the main site.

Work backwards from the enquiry form

Most catering enquiries arrive missing the one detail you need, and the first reply becomes a list of questions. Decide what you need to quote, then build the form around it:

FieldWhy you need itHow to ask it
Event typeSets the menu style, timing and staffingA dropdown: wedding, reception, sangeet or mehendi, birthday, corporate, religious function, other
Date and timeAvailability, and whether it is lunch, dinner or hi-teaA date picker, plus "date not fixed yet"
Guest countThe biggest driver of the quoteRanges you define, not a free number
VenueTravel, kitchen setup and venue rulesVenue name or area, plus "venue not booked yet"
Service styleStaff, crockery and equipmentBuffet, plated, live counters, packed meal boxes
Food preferencesMenu and kitchen planningVeg, non-veg, Jain, no onion and garlic, cuisines wanted
Budget per plateWhich package to suggestRanges you set, with "not sure" as an option
ExtrasA complete quote, first timeTick boxes for crockery, service staff, tables, counters and decor

Eight fields on one screen can feel long on a phone. Splitting them into two or three short steps, starting with the easy ones, usually keeps more people going; multi-step forms shows how. Send each enquiry to email and WhatsApp at the same time, because the caterer who replies first with a sensible menu often wins the booking.

One page for each kind of event

A wedding planner and an office administrator are looking for different things, even if they end up ordering the same paneer. Separate pages let each one see work like theirs:

  • Weddings and receptions: large buffets, live counters, multiple meals across several functions, and coordination with the venue and decorator
  • Sangeet, mehendi and haldi: lighter menus, chaat and snack counters, often at home or a lawn
  • Birthdays and house parties: smaller numbers, home kitchens with limited space, kids' menus
  • Corporate events and conferences: fixed timings, packed or plated options, GST invoices and a single contact
  • Religious functions and poojas: satvik and Jain menus, no onion and garlic, and kitchens that respect those rules if you genuinely run them that way

Each page can carry a sample menu, two or three photos from that type of event, the service styles you offer for it and an enquiry button that preselects the event type.

Menu packages people can compare

Customers find it hard to compare caterers because every menu is different. Packages make comparison easier, and comparison is where a well-organised caterer looks good. For each package, state:

  • The number of welcome drinks, starters, main course dishes, breads, rice items and desserts
  • Which live counters are included, if any
  • The type of crockery and cutlery, from disposable to china
  • Service staff included, and for how many hours
  • The minimum number of guests

Live counters and add-ons

Live counters are often what guests remember, and they change the cost and the space you need at the venue. List each one you run, such as dosa, chaat, pasta, tandoor or a jalebi counter, with a photo, the space and power it needs, and whether it carries an extra charge per plate or a flat fee. Do the same for add-ons like welcome drinks served by staff, a paan counter or late-night snacks for a sangeet. Customers can then build a package up instead of asking for everything and being surprised by the quote.

Alongside the packages, offer a "build your own menu" route: a list of dishes by course, with a note that the per-plate price is confirmed in the quote. Put menus on proper web pages so they load quickly and Google can read them; putting a menu online compares PDFs, images and web pages.

Per-plate pricing: shown, a starting price, or on request?

Many caterers worry that showing prices invites bargaining or scares people off. Not showing them has a cost too: people who cannot judge your range often move on to someone who shows one. There are three honest options:

  1. Show the price per plate for set packages with a fixed menu. This suits office lunches, packed meals and smaller parties.
  2. Show a starting price for packages that vary with guest numbers and dishes, and say what moves it up or down.
  3. Price on request for weddings and large custom events, with a sentence explaining why and how quickly you send a quote.

Whichever you choose, say what the price includes: tax, transport, staff, crockery, counters, setup and cleanup. Most disputes in catering come from what someone assumed was included.

Tasting sessions for larger bookings

For a wedding or a large corporate event, a tasting often decides the booking. Put the details on a page of its own:

  • Who it is for, such as events above a guest count you set
  • Whether it is charged, and whether the charge is adjusted against the booking
  • How many people can attend, and which dishes are included
  • Where it happens and how to book a slot

A short booking form with preferred dates, or a link to a scheduling tool, saves a round of calls. Confirm the tasting on WhatsApp with the address and time.

The kitchen behind the food

People booking food for hundreds of guests want to know it will be cooked and served safely. Show what you actually do:

  • FSSAI registration or licence number, in the footer and on enquiry pages; food businesses are generally expected to display it, and an FSSAI consultant can tell you what applies to a caterer at the time you launch
  • Your kitchen, photographed as it is, with staff in uniform
  • How food travels and stays hot, such as insulated containers and chafing dishes
  • Separate preparation for veg and non-veg, or for Jain food, only if you really keep them separate
  • Allergens, and how guests can ask about ingredients

Avoid words such as "best", "five-star" or "100% hygienic". Specific, checkable details do more.

A gallery of past events that answers "can they handle ours?"

The gallery is your proof of scale. Organise it by event type, and give each set of photos a caption with the type of event, the kind of venue, the service style and, if you can state it accurately, the rough number of guests. Include setup shots: the buffet line before guests arrive, live counters in action, the team at work. Photos from private weddings need the family's permission, and venues sometimes have their own rules about photography.

Caterers often work with the same venues, decorators and planners. A short list of venues you regularly serve helps couples who have already booked one, and partners send referrals both ways. Websites for wedding venues and banquet halls and websites for event and wedding planners show what those partners publish, and suppliers of tents, furniture and sound face a similar job in websites for event rental businesses.

Corporate meals and repeat orders

Office lunches, meeting meals and daily staff meals are smaller orders that repeat, and they smooth out the gap between wedding seasons. A corporate page should cover the minimum order, how far ahead to order, delivery timing, packed or buffet service, menu rotation for daily meals, GST invoicing and monthly billing. A trial week is an easy way in. If daily meal plans become a large part of your business, websites for tiffin and meal subscriptions covers plans and renewals.

FAQs

Should my catering website show per-plate prices?

Show them for fixed packages and smaller orders, a starting price for packages that vary, and keep large custom weddings on request. In every case, say what the price includes.

What should a catering enquiry form ask?

Event type, date, guest count range, venue or area, service style, food preferences and a budget range per plate, plus a WhatsApp number. That is usually enough to send a first quote.

Can customers book a tasting session online?

Yes. A short form or scheduling tool can offer tasting slots, and the confirmation can include the address, time and whether the tasting charge is adjusted against the booking.

Should a caterer display the FSSAI number online?

It is generally expected for food businesses that take orders, including online. Put it in the footer and on the enquiry pages, and confirm the present requirements for your registration or licence with your consultant.

Can I use photos from weddings I have catered?

Yes, with the permission of the family and, where it applies, the venue. Focus on the food, counters and setup rather than guests' faces.
