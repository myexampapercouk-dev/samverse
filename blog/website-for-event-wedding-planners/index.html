Websites for Event and Wedding Planners: Event-by-Event Portfolios With Vendor Credits, Destination Pages, Your Process, Budget Talk and Enquiry Forms

Build a planner website that wins enquiries: event portfolios with vendor credits, destination pages, a clear process and a form asking date and guests.

Key takeaways
  • Instagram shows your taste, but the website is where couples and companies check your process, read full event stories and send a proper enquiry.
  • Organise the portfolio by event, each with a short story, the scale and credited vendors, rather than one gallery of hundreds of mixed photos.
  • You can guide budgets without a price list by explaining what drives cost and asking for a budget range in the enquiry form.

An event or wedding planner's website has to answer three questions for someone who has already seen your Instagram: have you handled an event like mine, how would working with you actually go, and how do I start? A portfolio arranged by event, a page that explains your process and a short enquiry form asking for date, city and guest count will do more for bookings than a cinematic homepage video.

The build itself is a standard business site, covered under WordPress website development, with extra care for galleries and speed. During the wedding season or ahead of corporate budget cycles, a focused landing page for a specific offer, such as destination weddings in one region or corporate offsites, gives ad clicks a clearer path than the homepage.

Instagram or a website? Both, with different jobs

Many planners get most of their first contacts through Instagram and wonder whether a site is worth it. The two do different work:

TaskInstagramYour website
Showing taste and recent workStrong, frequent and visualCurated, with the best events only
Explaining your process and servicesHard to find in a feed or highlightsA page for each, easy to read and share
Being found on GoogleRarely, beyond your nameFor searches like "destination wedding planner in Udaipur"
Collecting complete enquiriesDMs that need several rounds of questionsA form that captures date, city, guests and budget at once
OwnershipRented space, subject to the platform's rulesYours, on hosting in your name

Link the website in your bio, point story highlights at specific pages, and use the site to show the full story of events that only get a few frames on Instagram. Turning Instagram followers into website enquiries covers the linking in more detail.

Say which events you plan

"Event management" covers everything from a 40-guest engagement dinner to a three-day product launch. Visitors want to see their kind of event named. A typical menu might include:

  • Weddings: full weddings, intimate weddings, destination weddings, and individual functions such as sangeet, mehendi or reception
  • Social events: engagements, milestone birthdays, anniversaries, baby showers
  • Corporate events: conferences, dealer meets, offsites, award nights, product launches
  • Public events: festivals, concerts and exhibitions, if you genuinely run them

Give a page to each type you actively want more of, and leave out types you would rather not take. If you sell tickets to public events, the site needs a different setup; websites for event organisers selling tickets covers it.

Service levels

Spell out what you mean by full planning, partial planning and on-the-day coordination. Couples often assume "coordination" includes vendor booking, then feel short-changed. A simple list of what each level includes, and what it does not, prevents that conversation later.

A portfolio arranged by event, with vendor credits

One giant gallery of mixed photos makes it hard to judge anything. A page per event lets a visitor see the whole arc: the venue, the decor, the guest experience and the scale. Each event story can follow the same short structure:

  1. The brief: type of event, city or destination, number of guests, number of days, in a sentence or two.
  2. The challenge: monsoon dates, a venue change, guests flying in from several countries, a tight build window.
  3. What you did: planning, design direction, logistics, hospitality.
  4. The photos: twenty to forty well-chosen images, not three hundred.
  5. The team: credited vendors with their roles.

Crediting the photographer, decorator, caterer, makeup artist and venue is fair to them, and it helps you: vendors tend to share and link back to pages that credit them, and a reader planning their own event gets a useful list. Ask the photographer's permission to use their images and credit them as they prefer. Ask your clients before publishing their event, and agree whether names, faces of guests or children should appear. Some families prefer a private wedding, and a story without names still works.

Planners who work closely with venues and decorators may find related ideas in websites for wedding venues and websites for wedding decorators.

Destination weddings need their own pages

Destination work is often the most valuable, and the most searched by place. Build a page for each destination where you have actually planned events, with your own photos from there:

  • The kind of venues available: palaces, beach resorts, hill properties, heritage hotels
  • Best and difficult seasons for that place, in general terms
  • How guests usually travel there, and how you handle airport pickups and room blocks
  • Local permissions or venue rules that can affect music, fireworks or timings, with a note that these vary and are checked for each event
  • A link to the events you have done there

Avoid publishing destination pages for places you have never worked. A page full of stock photos of a location you have no experience in is easy to spot and hard to defend in a client meeting.

Show your process as a timeline

Couples, and their parents, want to know what happens and when. A timeline page sets expectations and makes you look organised before the first meeting. Adjust the stages to your own way of working:

StageWhat happensWhat the client decides
First callDates, guest count, cities, style and rough budget discussedWhether to go ahead with a proposal
ProposalScope, service level, fee basis and timeline sentSign and pay the booking amount
Venue and key vendorsShortlists, site visits, bookingsVenue, photographer, decor direction
Design and guest planningMood boards, function themes, guest list, travel and roomsFinal designs and guest numbers
Final weeksRun sheets, vendor confirmations, rehearsalsLast changes, payments due
Event daysOn-site team runs every functionNothing, ideally
AfterwardsVendor settlements, returns, photo delivery follow-upFeedback and permission to feature the event

Corporate clients often care about different checkpoints, such as purchase orders, invoices and approvals, so a short corporate version of the process page is worth having.

Talking about budget without a price list

Event costs vary too widely for a published price list to mean much, but hiding the topic completely brings in enquiries you cannot serve. A middle path works well:

  • Explain what drives cost: guest count, number of functions, venue type, season, decor scale, travel
  • Say how your fee works, for example a fixed planning fee or a percentage, without giving figures if you prefer not to
  • Include a budget range field in the enquiry form, with broad bands you choose
  • Mention the kinds of events you take, such as "weddings of 150 guests and above", if that is how you work

This lets visitors self-select, and gives you a realistic picture before the first call.

A planner's form should collect what you would ask in the first five minutes of a phone call, and nothing more.

Add a WhatsApp button beside the form for people who would rather message, with a pre-filled line that mentions the page they were on. Reply quickly; couples often contact several planners on the same evening.

A photo-heavy site that still loads fast

Planner websites are among the heaviest I see, because every event has hundreds of full-resolution photos. A few habits keep them fast on a phone:

  • Export images at the size they are shown, then compress them; image optimisation for WordPress explains how
  • Use modern formats such as WebP, served automatically
  • Show thumbnails in grids, and open the full image only when tapped
  • Load images further down the page only as the visitor scrolls, which is called lazy loading
  • Host films on YouTube or Vimeo and embed them with a light preview, instead of autoplaying video on the homepage

Photographers face the same problem; websites for photographers has more gallery ideas.

Search terms worth a page

Searches tend to combine the event type and the place: "wedding planner in Jaipur", "destination wedding planner Goa", "corporate event management company in Pune". Give each genuine combination a real page, with your own events and details, and link it from the relevant portfolio stories. Planning guides, such as a wedding planning checklist by month or how to plan guest travel for a destination wedding, attract couples early, long before they are ready to enquire.

FAQs

How many events should my portfolio show?

Quality matters more than number. Six to twelve well-told events, covering the types you want more of, usually work better than dozens of thin galleries.

Can I use photos taken by the photographer we worked with?

Only with their permission, and with a credit in the form they prefer. Many photographers are happy to agree in exchange for a credit and a link.

Should I publish my planning fees?

It is your choice. Many planners explain what drives cost and how their fee works, then share figures after the first call, and use a budget range field to check fit.

Is a website worth it if most of my leads come from Instagram?

Yes, because the website gives those leads somewhere to read your process, see full event stories and send a complete enquiry, and it brings in search traffic that Instagram does not.

Can I add destination pages for places I have not worked in yet?

I would wait until you have real events and photos from there. A page without genuine experience behind it rarely persuades anyone.
