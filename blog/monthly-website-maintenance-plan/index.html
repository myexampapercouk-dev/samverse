Monthly Website Maintenance Plans: What a Good WordPress Plan Should Include, and What Is Usually Extra

What a monthly website maintenance plan should include: tested updates, off-site backups, security, uptime and form checks, content time and a monthly report.

Key takeaways
  • A good monthly plan covers updates applied after a backup and tested afterwards, off-site backups, security scans, uptime monitoring, speed, form and link checks, small content changes and a report.
  • The difference between plans is in how the work is done: whether updates are tested, backups are stored off-site and restorable, and someone checks forms and checkout.
  • New features, redesigns, large content projects, hosting fees and cleaning an already-hacked site are usually quoted separately.

A good monthly website maintenance plan includes WordPress, theme and plugin updates applied after a backup and tested afterwards; regular off-site backups; security scans and monitoring; uptime monitoring; speed checks; form, checkout and broken link checks; a set amount of small content changes; and a short monthly report. New features, redesigns, large content projects and hosting fees are usually extra.

Most plans list similar items. What separates a useful plan from a box-ticking one is how each item is done, so this post explains what "done properly" looks like for each, from the buyer's side. If you'd rather do the work yourself, the WordPress maintenance checklist is the hands-on version, and my own WordPress maintenance plans are built around the same list.

Updates, done properly

Updates are the centre of any plan, and the riskiest part. Done properly, a backup is taken first, important plugins such as WooCommerce, page builders and payment gateways are updated one at a time, and key pages, forms and, on stores, checkout are checked afterwards. When an update breaks something, the fix is to roll back that one update or resolve the conflict, not to restore the whole site and lose recent orders. How to update WordPress safely walks through the process in detail.

Ask how often updates happen (weekly or fortnightly is typical, with urgent security fixes sooner) and what is tested afterwards. "We keep everything updated" without a word about testing usually means auto-updates.

Backups, and restores

  • Off-site storage. At least one copy outside your hosting account, so a server failure or suspended account can't take out the site and its backups together.
  • Frequency to match the site. Daily is common; stores may need more.
  • Retention of several weeks, because problems are often noticed late.
  • Restores included, not charged as an emergency extra.
  • Your copy on request, and the latest backup handed over if you leave.

The backup and restore guide explains the 3-2-1 rule and how restores work.

Security monitoring

Most hacked WordPress sites were running outdated plugins or themes, so regular updates are the biggest security measure in any plan. On top of that, expect regular malware scans, login protection against password-guessing, a monthly review of administrator accounts, and attention to anything the scans flag. Cleaning up a site that was already hacked before the plan started is usually a separate job.

Uptime and speed checks

Uptime monitoring checks the site every few minutes from outside and raises an alert when it fails. A useful plan also watches SSL certificate expiry and uses a keyword check, so an error page that still "loads" is noticed; see uptime monitoring explained.

Speed checks compare key pages with last month. A sudden drop usually follows a new plugin, a large image or a third-party script, and catching it early is far cheaper than fixing a site that has slowly become sluggish over a year.

Form, checkout and broken link checks

This is the check that protects enquiries, and the one most often skipped. A contact form can show its thank-you message while every email silently fails, and owners often find out from a customer weeks later. A proper check submits each form and confirms the email arrives. Where a form routes enquiries by type, such as the service-type dropdown on the Vansh Group contact form I built, the enquiry should arrive with the right service attached. Interactive features, calculators and self-assessments need the same attention, because they break more quietly than a layout does.

On a WooCommerce store, add a test order through the payment flow, using test mode or cash on delivery, and a check that order emails and invoices go out. Broken links and new 404 errors are picked up from Search Console or a crawl and fixed with the right link or a redirect.

Small content changes

Most plans include small changes each month: updating text, swapping images, changing prices, adding a team member or posting a notice. Before signing up, find out:

  • What counts as a small change, and whether it's measured by request or by time
  • What happens to an allowance you don't use
  • How to send requests, and how quickly they're usually handled

Anything that adds new functionality or layout is normally a change request, quoted separately. Bug or change request? explains where that line usually sits.

The monthly report

A short report is how you know the work is happening. It should list:

  • Updates applied, and anything held back with the reason
  • Backup status, and where backups are stored
  • Uptime for the month, with any outages and their causes
  • Security scan results and anything fixed
  • Speed figures for key pages compared with last month
  • Content changes made
  • Recommendations, such as an abandoned plugin to replace or an upcoming renewal

What is usually not included

  • New pages, new features and redesigns
  • Large content projects, such as uploading a product catalogue or rewriting service pages
  • A full speed overhaul for a site that's slow to begin with
  • Hosting, domain and premium licence fees, which are paid to the providers, ideally in your name
  • SEO campaigns, content writing and ad management

Plans that include regular content publishing, SEO and conversion work are closer to website management; website maintenance vs management explains the difference.

What the first month should look like

A good plan doesn't start by clicking "update all". The first month is a health check: access and a full backup, an inventory of every plugin and theme with its version and licence status, a look at PHP version, Site Health, scheduled tasks, email delivery and admin access, agreed fixes for anything urgent, then uptime monitoring and baseline speed figures. A site that has gone years without updates is brought up to date in stages rather than all at once.

Signs your current plan isn't working

  • No monthly report, or the same generic report every month
  • A growing list of pending updates in the dashboard
  • You learn about outages or broken forms from customers
  • Nobody can tell you where backups are stored, or send you a copy
  • The plan locks you into a long contract with no easy way out

FAQs

What does a website maintenance plan include?

Tested updates, off-site backups, security scans, uptime monitoring, speed checks, form and broken link checks, a set amount of small content changes and a monthly report. Stores add test orders and order email checks.

Is hosting included in a maintenance plan?

Usually not. Hosting is normally paid to the hosting company, ideally in your name, and the maintenance plan covers looking after the site. Some providers bundle both, so check which is which.

What happens if an update breaks my site?

On a properly run plan, a backup is taken before updates, so the faulty update can be rolled back or the conflict fixed quickly. Ask any provider how they handle this before you sign up.

Can I cancel a monthly maintenance plan?

Month-to-month plans can usually be cancelled with notice. Make sure your domain, hosting and WordPress accounts stay in your name, and that you can take the latest backup with you.

Do I need a plan if my website rarely changes?

Plugins and WordPress still need updating, backups still need checking and forms still need testing, even if your content never changes. A small site can be maintained by its owner with a checklist, or covered by a basic plan.
