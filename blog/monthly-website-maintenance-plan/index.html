Monthly Website Maintenance Plans: What a Good WordPress Plan Should Include, and What Is Usually Extra

What a monthly website maintenance plan should include: tested updates, off-site backups, security, uptime and form checks, content time and a clear report.

Key takeaways
  • A monthly maintenance plan is worth paying for only when updates are tested, backups are off-site and restorable, and someone checks forms and checkout afterwards.
  • Ask exactly how many minutes or tasks of content changes are included, and what counts as a new feature, before you sign up.
  • The monthly report should show what changed and what was checked, so you can tell whether the plan is doing its job.

A monthly website maintenance plan should include tested plugin, theme and core updates, daily off-site backups with a proven restore, security monitoring, uptime checks, a monthly test of your forms and checkout, a small amount of content-change time and a plain report. If a plan only says "updates and backups" with no detail, you are likely paying for a button click, not maintenance.

This guide breaks down each part of a good WordPress maintenance plan, what is normally charged separately, how plans differ by type of site, and how to tell whether your current plan is working.

Updates, done properly

Updates are the core of any plan and the part most likely to be done badly. Good practice is to take a fresh backup, read the changelog for major releases, update on a staging copy for large or risky changes (a WooCommerce major version, a page builder update, a PHP version change), then check the home page, a few templates, forms and checkout on the live site. Premium plugins need valid licences or they stop updating, so the plan should say who holds and renews them. How to update WordPress safely explains the full process.

Backups, and restores

A backup stored only on the same server is not a backup; if the server fails or the site is hacked, it can go too. Ask for daily backups kept off-site, a retention period (for example, the last 30 days), and a test restore at least occasionally. For WooCommerce stores with orders arriving all day, more frequent database backups are sensible. The WordPress backup and restore guide covers what to ask for.

Security monitoring

Security in a plan should mean more than an installed plugin. Expect malware scanning, login protection (limited attempts, two-factor authentication for admins), removal of unused plugins and admin accounts, file change alerts and someone who actually reads the alerts. Check whether malware cleanup is included if something gets through, or charged as a separate job.

Uptime and speed checks

Uptime monitoring checks your site every few minutes and alerts when it goes down. The useful question is who receives the alert and what they do next: contact the host, roll back an update, or fix an expired domain or SSL. Uptime monitoring explained covers the options. A monthly speed check on mobile catches slow-downs caused by new plugins, large images or tracking scripts before they hurt enquiries.

Form, checkout and broken link checks

The most expensive silent failure is a contact form that stops sending emails, or a checkout that fails on one payment method. A good plan includes a real test submission of every important form and, for shops, a test order through each payment option (including UPI and COD if you offer them) every month and after updates. A broken link scan and a quick look at Search Console errors fit in the same visit.

Small content changes

Most plans include some time for edits: changing text, swapping images, adding a team member, updating prices. The plan should state how much (a number of minutes or small tasks per month), whether unused time rolls over, and the turnaround. New pages, new features and design changes are usually quoted separately. Bug or change request? explains where the line normally sits.

The monthly report

A short report keeps everyone honest. It should list what was updated (with versions), backups confirmed, security events, uptime, form and checkout test results, content changes done and anything that needs your decision, such as a plugin that is no longer maintained. If a report is just an automated PDF of graphs with no notes, ask what was actually checked by a person.

Response times and emergencies

Ask two separate questions: how quickly routine requests are handled (a text change, a new image) and how quickly an emergency is handled (site down, checkout broken, hacked). A plan that answers routine requests within a couple of working days can be perfectly fine, but an emergency on a store during a festival sale needs a much faster response. Check whether emergencies on weekends and holidays are covered, how you report one (email, WhatsApp, phone), and whether out-of-hours work costs extra. Write the answers into the agreement rather than relying on a sales conversation.

Who owns what

Maintenance should never make you dependent on one person. Your hosting, domain, Google Analytics, Search Console and premium licences should be registered to your business, with the provider given access rather than ownership. Keep a simple list of every login and who holds it. If the provider changes, you can hand over the site in a day instead of a month.

What is usually not included

  • Hosting and domain renewals, unless the plan explicitly bundles them.
  • Premium plugin and theme licences.
  • New pages, new features, redesigns and integrations.
  • Major version upgrades that need code changes, such as an old theme that breaks on a newer PHP version.
  • Full malware cleanup after a hack, in some plans.
  • SEO work, content writing and ad management.

None of these exclusions is unfair, but they should be written down. The difference between maintenance and ongoing management is covered in website maintenance vs website management.

How plans differ by type of site

Brochure sites with a few pages and a contact form need reliable updates, backups and form checks; content time matters more than heavy monitoring. WooCommerce stores need more frequent backups, checkout testing on every update, payment gateway and shipping plugin attention, and faster response when something breaks during a sale. Membership, LMS and booking sites need user-facing flows (login, booking, payment) tested after updates. Sites with heavy page builders need careful builder updates because a single release can change layouts across the site.

What the first month should look like

A new maintenance provider should begin with an audit: list every plugin and theme, flag abandoned or duplicate ones, check PHP version and hosting health, set up backups and monitoring, confirm admin access and licence ownership, and record a baseline speed test. Expect a short list of recommendations at the end of the month. If the first month passes with no questions about your site, that is a warning sign. I took a similar audit-first approach when looking after the Vansh Group site.

Questions to ask before you sign

  1. How often are updates run, and what do you check afterwards?
  2. Where are backups stored, how long are they kept and when was a restore last tested?
  3. Who receives uptime and security alerts, and what is the response time?
  4. How much content-change time is included, and what counts as new work?
  5. Is malware cleanup included if the site is hacked?
  6. Who holds hosting, domain and premium licences?
  7. What does the monthly report contain? Can I see a sample?
  8. What is the notice period, and what do I receive when I leave?

Clear, specific answers to these questions are a better signal of quality than any feature list on a pricing page.

Running maintenance yourself

If you prefer to handle maintenance in-house, keep a written routine: weekly update round with a backup first, a monthly form and checkout test, a monthly look at Search Console for errors, and a quarterly restore test on a staging copy. Use an uptime monitor that alerts your phone. The routine matters more than the tools; most self-maintained sites fail because updates are skipped for months, not because the owner lacked skill.

Maintenance during busy seasons

For stores and booking sites, timing matters. Avoid major updates in the days before a festival sale, an admissions window or a big campaign; run them a week or two earlier on staging and freeze changes during the peak. A good provider will ask about your calendar and plan updates around it, and will be on standby during the busiest days rather than discovering a problem after it has cost orders.

Signs your current plan isn't working

  • You find out the site was down from a customer, not your provider.
  • Enquiries dropped and nobody noticed the form had stopped sending.
  • Plugins show pending updates for weeks.
  • Nobody can tell you where your backups are or when one was last restored.
  • Reports arrive but never mention a problem or a recommendation.

What it costs

Monthly plan pricing depends on the number of plugins and integrations, whether it is a store, how much content time is included, response times and whether hosting is bundled. FAQs

What does a monthly website maintenance plan include?

Tested core, plugin and theme updates, daily off-site backups, security and uptime monitoring, monthly form and checkout tests, a set amount of content-change time and a written report of what was done.

Is hosting included in a maintenance plan?

Sometimes. Many plans exclude hosting and domain renewals; others bundle managed hosting. Check the scope, and make sure the hosting and domain accounts stay in your name either way.

What happens if an update breaks my site?

Under a good plan, the provider restores from the backup taken just before the update or rolls back the plugin, then investigates and fixes the conflict, usually without extra charge.

How many content changes are included in a monthly plan?

It varies by plan. Look for a stated number of minutes or small tasks per month, whether unused time carries over, and a clear rule for what counts as new work.

Can I cancel a monthly maintenance plan?

Most monthly plans can be cancelled with notice. Before you leave, make sure you have admin access, hosting and domain logins, licence details and a recent full backup.

Do I need a plan if my website rarely changes?

Yes, usually. Plugins, WordPress and PHP still change even if your content doesn't, and an unpatched, unmonitored site is the most common target for hacks and silent form failures.
