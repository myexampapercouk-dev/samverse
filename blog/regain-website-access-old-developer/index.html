How to Regain Control of Your Website From a Previous Developer: Domain, Hosting, WordPress, Google Accounts and Email

Developer gone silent? How to regain access to your domain, hosting, WordPress admin, DNS, Google accounts and email, plus a handover checklist.

Key takeaways
  • Start with the domain: whoever controls the registrar account controls DNS, and with it the website, the email and Search Console verification.
  • Accounts in your business name can be recovered from the provider with proof of ownership; accounts in the developer's name need their cooperation, so ask politely and in writing first.
  • Once you are back in, change every password, remove old administrators and keep a record of each login in your name, so the next handover takes minutes.

To get your website back from a developer who has stopped replying, work out who controls each piece the site depends on: the domain registrar, DNS, hosting, the WordPress administrator account, business email and your Google accounts. Anything registered in your name can usually be recovered from the provider with proof of ownership. Anything in the developer's name needs their cooperation, so start with a polite written request, and deal with the domain first, because whoever controls the domain controls everything that hangs off it.

It happens more often than people expect: a freelancer changes careers, an agency closes, or a relationship ends over an unpaid invoice, and every login turns out to sit with someone else. My WordPress maintenance plans take on sites I didn't build, and the first step is a health check that includes who has administrator access and whose name the licences are in. Below is the order I'd work in, what providers typically ask for, and how to stop it happening again.

Before you contact anyone

Gather the contract or quote, paid invoices, project emails or WhatsApp messages, and any logins you were ever sent. If there is an unpaid invoice or a disagreement about scope, settling it is often the quickest route to a full handover. Then send one calm, written request with a list and a date, because a list is easy to act on:

Hi [name], we are taking over the management of our website. Please could you send by [date]: transfer of the domain to our account, a full backup of the site (files and database) or a move to our hosting, an Administrator login in our name, and admin access to Search Console, Analytics and our Business Profile for [business email]. Thank you for your work on the site.

If there's no reply after a reminder, recover what you can directly, in this order.

Step 1: Get the domain back

For .com and most generic domains, ICANN's lookup tool at lookup.icann.org shows the registrar and expiry date; for .in domains, use the .IN registry's or a registrar's WHOIS search. Owner details are usually hidden for privacy, but the registrar is visible.

If the domain is registered in your name

Ask the registrar's support to recover the account. Expect them to want ID matching the registrant name, business documents such as a GST certificate or certificate of incorporation, and sometimes the original invoice; requirements vary, so ask first. Once verified, change the account email to your own and switch on two-factor authentication.

If it is in the developer's name or reseller account

Many developers buy domains through a reseller panel, so the registrar treats the developer as its customer. They need to move it to an account in your name or give you the authorisation code to transfer it; transferring a domain to another registrar covers the codes, locks and timing.

Check the expiry date straight away: a lapsed domain takes the website and email down with it, so a close date with a silent developer is urgent; see what to do when a domain expires. If a developer flatly refuses to hand over a domain that clearly belongs to your business, dispute routes such as the UDRP for generic domains and INDRP for .in exist, but they target bad-faith registrations, involve fees and take time. That is a conversation for a lawyer, with your contract in hand.

Step 2: Take control of DNS without breaking anything

Once the domain is yours, you choose its nameservers. The trap is switching them before copying the existing records: if DNS lives in the developer's Cloudflare or hosting account, that is where the website, email and verification records are.

  1. Use a DNS lookup tool to note the public records: the A record for the root domain, www, MX, the SPF TXT record, DMARC at _dmarc, and any verification TXT records.
  2. Get DKIM values from your email provider's admin panel, and ask about any subdomains in use, such as a shop or booking system.
  3. Recreate every record at the new DNS provider: your registrar, your host or a Cloudflare account in your name.
  4. Change the nameservers, then test the website, send and receive email, and submit a form.

Step 3: Recover the hosting, or get a full copy of the site

To find the host, check which company owns the domain's IP address, or search your inbox and bank statements for renewals; hPanel, cPanel or a host's name in an old email are good clues.

  • Hosting in your name: the host can verify you through the registered email or phone, proof of payment such as an invoice or UPI transaction reference, and ID.
  • Hosting in the developer's account, common with freelancers who host several clients on one plan: ask for a full backup, meaning the files (especially wp-content, which holds uploads, themes and plugins) plus a database export, or a move to hosting in your name.
  • No reply, but you have a WordPress Administrator login: a backup or migration plugin such as UpdraftPlus, Duplicator or All-in-One WP Migration can export the whole site for restoring on new hosting. Free versions have size or feature limits at the time of writing, and some hosts block large exports.

If the hosting is due to renew and nobody is paying for it, a full copy is the most urgent job of all. Moving a site onto hosting you own, without downtime, is what my WordPress migration service covers.

Step 4: Get back into WordPress

  1. "Lost your password?" on the login page, with every email the account might use. The reset goes to the account's email, so it only helps if that address is yours.
  2. Your hosting panel. Some panels, such as Hostinger's hPanel or Softaculous in cPanel, can change the WordPress admin password or log you straight in. Options differ by host.
  3. phpMyAdmin. In the users table (wp_users by default; check $table_prefix in wp-config.php), change the admin's user_email to your address, then use "Lost your password?". Fixing WordPress login problems walks through this and other lockouts.
  4. WP-CLI over SSH, if your host offers it, to list administrators and create one for you:
wp user list --role=administrator --fields=ID,user_login,user_email
wp user create newowner owner@example.com --role=administrator --send-email

Once you have your own Administrator account, demote or delete the developer's. When deleting, attribute their content to your account so no pages disappear, then review everyone else with the WordPress user accounts audit.

Step 5: Search Console, Analytics and Business Profile

Search Console: with control of DNS, you don't need the developer. Add a domain property in your own Google account and verify it with the TXT record Google provides. To remove the old owner, delete their verification token (TXT record, HTML file or meta tag) first, then remove them under Settings, Users and permissions, or they stay verified.

Google Analytics 4: only an existing administrator can grant access; at the time of writing there is no DNS-based way to claim a property. Ask the developer to add your Google account as Administrator. If they never respond, create a new property in your own account and replace the tag, accepting that the old history stays behind; setting up GA4 and Search Console covers the setup.

Google Business Profile: if Google says someone else manages your business, it offers a way to request access. The current owner is notified, and if they don't respond within Google's set period, you may be able to verify it yourself; follow the steps Google currently shows. For Tag Manager, Google Ads and Meta business assets, ask for access added to your own account, never for the developer's login.

Step 6: Business email and premium licences

If your mailboxes sit on the developer's hosting or in their Google Workspace or Zoho account, ask for access or an export while you can. To move, create the new mailboxes with a provider in your name first, then change the MX, SPF and DKIM records, so no mail is lost in between; business email options compared helps you choose. Premium themes and plugins licensed to the developer stop updating when their licence lapses, and licences often can't be transferred, so buying a fresh one in your business name is usually simpler.

Secure everything once you're back in

  1. Change passwords for the registrar, hosting, WordPress, SFTP and the database user (updating wp-config.php for the database password).
  2. Switch on two-factor authentication everywhere it is offered.
  3. Remove old SSH keys, API keys and WordPress application passwords.
  4. Take a full backup stored away from the server, check for administrators or plugins nobody recognises, then run updates.

Make sure it can't happen again

Keep every account above in a password manager the business controls, with the login email, renewal date and who else has access. Developers should get their own user accounts, never your master logins, and every project should end with a written handover. The website handover checklist lists what to receive.

FAQs

Can a developer legally keep my website or domain?

It depends on your contract and whose name the domain and hosting were bought in. If the developer registered the domain in their own name, they are the registrant on record, even if you paid. This isn't legal advice; if the stakes are high, speak to a lawyer with your contract and invoices.

How do I find out who hosts my website?

Look up the domain's IP address with a DNS lookup tool, then check who owns that IP with an IP WHOIS lookup.

How can I get into WordPress without the admin email?

You need hosting access. From there, the host's WordPress tools, phpMyAdmin or WP-CLI can change the admin email or create a new administrator, and you reset the password normally.

Will my website go down while I take back control?

It shouldn't. The risky moments are changing nameservers before copying every DNS record, and letting the domain or hosting lapse while you wait. Copy records first, check renewal dates early and keep a full backup.

Can I keep my Google Analytics history?

Only if an existing administrator adds you. Search Console is easier, because verifying the domain through DNS gives you the data the property already holds.
