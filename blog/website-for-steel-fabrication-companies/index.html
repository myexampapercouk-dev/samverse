Website for Steel Fabrication Companies: Capability Pages, Machinery, Certifications, Projects and RFQs

How to plan a website for a steel fabrication company in India: capability and machinery pages, certifications, case studies and drawing RFQ forms.

Key takeaways
  • Fabricators sell capability, so give each process its own page with honest limits on materials, thickness, size and weight, and say what is outsourced.
  • List only certifications you currently hold, with scope and expiry, and describe your inspection process and the documents you supply.
  • Build an RFQ form that accepts drawings securely and asks for material, quantity, finish, scope and delivery, then acknowledge every submission quickly.

A website for a steel fabrication company should prove capability fast: one page per process with honest limits, a current machinery list, certifications stated precisely, short project case studies and an RFQ form that accepts drawings. Buyers usually arrive with a drawing and a deadline, and they leave if they cannot tell whether your shop can make the job.

Fabrication buyers rarely browse for fun. An EPC contractor, a plant engineer or an OEM's purchase team wants to know one thing: can this shop make it to specification and deliver on time? For general B2B advice, see how manufacturers can get more B2B enquiries; this guide focuses on job-shop and project fabrication.

What fabrication buyers check

  • Processes: the cutting, forming, welding, machining and finishing you do in-house.
  • Limits: the materials, thicknesses, sizes and weights you can handle.
  • Capacity: shop floor area, lifting capacity and how much work you can take on.
  • Quality: welding procedures, inspection, documentation and certifications.
  • Experience: similar jobs for similar industries.
  • Logistics: your location, delivery reach and whether you erect on site.

If a buyer has to phone you just to learn your maximum plate thickness or bed size, they may move on to a shop that publishes it.

Website for steel fabrication company: capability pages

Capability pages are the heart of a fabrication website. Give each main process its own page, with clear and honest limits:

CapabilityWhat to state
CuttingLaser, plasma, oxy-fuel or waterjet; materials, maximum thickness and bed size
FormingPress brake bending, plate rolling and section bending; tonnage, length and thickness limits
WeldingProcesses (MIG/MAG, TIG, SAW, stick), materials such as mild steel, stainless steel and aluminium, and welder qualifications
MachiningTurning, milling, drilling and boring of fabricated parts
FinishingShot blasting, paint systems, powder coating or galvanising, and which you do in-house versus through partners
ErectionSite installation teams, cranes, safety practices and the regions you cover

Only list what you can reliably deliver. If finishing or galvanising is outsourced, say so. Buyers find out eventually, and honesty up front saves arguments later.

Show what you make, and for whom

Many buyers search by the thing they need. Create pages for the main types of work you take on:

  • Structural steel: industrial sheds, pre-engineered buildings, mezzanine floors, platforms, staircases and pipe racks.
  • Process equipment: tanks, hoppers, chutes, ducting, skids and frames.
  • Sheet metal: enclosures, panels, cabinets and machine guards.
  • Architectural metalwork: gates, railings, canopies and facade elements.

Then add industry pages if you serve particular sectors, such as pharma, food processing, power, cement or warehousing, explaining the materials, finishes and documentation those clients usually expect. If you also do glass and aluminium work, the guide to websites for glass and aluminium fabricators covers that side.

A machinery and infrastructure page

A simple, up-to-date machinery list does a lot of selling. Present it as a table with the machine type, the make or model if you are happy to share it, and its key capacity. Add photos and short videos of the shop floor, overhead cranes, material storage and inspection area.

Keep it current: a list that includes machines you have sold, or misses new ones, undermines trust the moment a buyer visits your shop.

Certifications and quality, stated precisely

Certifications often decide whether you get onto a vendor list. List only those you currently hold, with the scope, the certifying body and the expiry date, and offer copies on request. Depending on your work, buyers may ask about:

  • A quality management system such as ISO 9001.
  • Welding quality standards such as ISO 3834, with qualified welding procedures and welders.
  • EN 1090, if you supply structural steel to European markets.
  • Approvals under ASME codes or the Indian Boiler Regulations, if you make pressure vessels, boilers or their components.
  • Vendor approvals with large companies, mentioned only where you are allowed to.

Requirements vary by product and market, so check current rules with the relevant certifying body rather than relying on a general article. Alongside certificates, describe your inspection process: incoming material checks, dimensional inspection, any NDT you arrange, and the documents you supply, such as mill test certificates and inspection reports.

Projects that prove it

For each notable job, write a short case study: the industry, the scope (fabrication only, or supply and erection), materials and grades, approximate size or tonnage, the timeline and any challenge you solved. Add photos from the shop floor, dispatch and site. Name clients only with written permission; otherwise describe them generally, such as "a food processing plant in western India". An RFQ form built for drawings

Your RFQ form is where interest becomes work. Make it fit how buyers actually send enquiries:

  1. File upload for drawings and specifications (PDF, DWG, DXF or STEP), with a sensible size limit and an email alternative for very large files.
  2. Material and grade, quantity, and the required finish or coating.
  3. Scope: fabrication only, supply and erection, or repair and modification.
  4. Inspection and documentation requirements, if any.
  5. Delivery location and required date.

Drawings can be commercially sensitive, so restrict the file types your form accepts and keep uploads out of public folders. Say that drawings are treated confidentially, and that you will sign an NDA if asked, but only if both are true. Route submissions to someone who can estimate, send an automatic acknowledgement, and add a WhatsApp number for quick questions. The guide to RFQ forms for B2B websites goes deeper, and if your form is not bringing in RFQs, see why your contact form isn't getting enquiries.

Downloads: company profile and brochures

Purchase teams often need a company profile PDF for vendor registration. Offer one that matches the website: capabilities, machinery, certifications, key projects and contact details. Keep it reasonably small, name the file sensibly and update the date on the cover when anything changes. A short HTML page with the same content helps the profile get found in search too; see SEO for PDFs and brochures. Some fabricators also add a vendor registration page listing the documents they can provide, such as GST registration and company details, with bank details shared only on request and never posted publicly.

Responding to RFQs fast

A fabrication website brings enquiries of very different quality: a serious RFQ with drawings, a student asking for a project quote, a trader asking for rates on standard sections. Plan how each is handled. Tag RFQs with drawings as priority and route them to the estimating team, reply to vague enquiries with a short request for drawings, quantity and location, and keep a simple tracker of status from received to quoted, won or lost. Over a few months, the tracker shows which capabilities and industries bring real orders, which tells you which pages deserve more photos, case studies and detail.

Update the website when the business changes: a new laser cutter, a larger crane, a new certification or a completed landmark project. Fresh, specific updates keep the site credible for repeat buyers who check it before each new order.

SEO for fabricators

Buyers search by process, product and place: "steel fabrication in [city]", "sheet metal fabrication [industrial area]", "laser cutting service near me", "MS structure fabricator [city]" or "PEB shed manufacturer [state]". Your capability and product pages are what rank for these, so give each a clear title, specific content and photos of real work.

  • Keep your Google Business Profile complete, with shop and project photos.
  • List your company on relevant B2B directories, each linking back to your website.
  • Write practical articles, such as choosing between mild steel and stainless steel for a particular use.
  • Host machinery videos on YouTube and compress photos and PDFs, so pages load quickly for buyers checking your site from a plant or site office.

FAQs

What should a website for a steel fabrication company include?

Capability pages per process with limits, product and industry pages, a machinery list, certifications with scope and expiry, project case studies, a company profile download and an RFQ form that accepts drawings.

Should a fabricator publish its machine capacities?

Yes. Maximum thickness, bed size, bending length and lifting capacity help buyers decide quickly and reduce unsuitable enquiries.

How do I accept drawings safely through a website form?

Limit file types and sizes, store uploads outside public folders, email them only to estimators and keep backups secure.

Can I name the companies I have fabricated for?

Only with written permission. Otherwise describe them by industry and region.

Is WordPress suitable for an industrial fabrication website?

Yes. It handles capability pages, case studies, downloads and RFQ forms well, and your team can update projects and machinery lists easily.
