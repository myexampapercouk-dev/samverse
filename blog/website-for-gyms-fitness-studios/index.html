Websites for Gyms, Fitness Studios and Yoga Studios: Live Timetables, Membership Plans, a Trial Pass, Trainer Profiles and Honest Results

Plan a gym or studio website around a timetable members trust, clear plans, a trial pass, trainer profiles with real certifications and no body promises.

Key takeaways
  • A gym website is mostly a timetable, a set of plans and a trial offer, so build it so staff can update each one in minutes.
  • Trainer profiles should name each certification and the body that issued it, and facilities should be shown in real photos at real times of day.
  • Avoid body-transformation guarantees; describe programmes, coaching and support instead, and use member stories only with consent and context.

Someone choosing a gym or fitness studio wants to know whether your classes fit their day, what it costs to join and what it feels like to walk in. A site that shows an accurate timetable, lists plans clearly (or explains how to get a quote), offers a simple trial pass and introduces real trainers in a real space answers those questions before they visit.

The site itself is a straightforward business build, covered under WordPress website development. The work that matters is in keeping three moving parts accurate and making the trial pass easy to claim. For January resolutions, summer batches or corporate offers, a dedicated landing page per campaign keeps ad clicks focused on one offer.

Plan the site around how often things change

Most gym websites go stale because nobody planned who updates what. Sorting content by how often it changes tells you how to build each part.

ContentChangesHow to build itWho updates it
Class timetable, cancellations, substitutesWeekly or dailyAn editable schedule, or an embed from your booking softwareFront desk or studio manager
Offers and trial passesMonthly or seasonallyA banner and a campaign page that can be switched on and offOwner or marketing
Membership plans and feesA few times a yearOne plans page, referenced everywhere elseOwner
Trainer profilesWhen staff join or leaveA profile template with fixed fieldsManager
Facilities, location, hoursRarelyStandard pagesSet up at launch

If a member turns up for a 7 am class the website listed and finds it was cancelled weeks ago, you have lost more trust than a missing page ever would.

A timetable people can actually use

Too many gyms post the timetable as a photo of a printed sheet. It cannot be read on a phone, searched or updated quickly. A proper timetable:

  • Is real text in a table or schedule view, readable on a small screen
  • Can be filtered by day, class type, trainer and level
  • Shows duration, intensity or level, and whether booking is required
  • Links each class name to a short page explaining what it involves and who it suits
  • Shows cancellations and substitutions clearly for the current week
  • Marks women-only batches, senior-friendly sessions or kids' classes where you run them

If you already use gym management software with class booking, check whether it offers a schedule widget that can be embedded, so the timetable is entered once. Features and embedding options vary between providers, so confirm with yours what is possible before the site is planned around it.

Membership plans: list them or quote them?

Both approaches are common, and the right one depends on how you sell.

ApproachWorks well whenWatch out for
Full plan list with pricesPlans are simple and fees are not negotiatedUpdating every page when fees change; keep prices in one place
Plans listed, price on requestYou run frequent offers or tailor plansVisitors assuming you are expensive; reply fast to every request
Starting prices onlyYou want to filter without committing to every figureSay clearly what the starting price includes

Whichever you choose, describe what each plan includes: gym floor access, number of classes, personal training sessions, locker, steam or pool use, freeze options and joining fees. Should you show prices on your website? goes through the trade-offs.

Online payment for memberships

Members renewing a plan, buying a class pack or paying for a workshop can pay online through an Indian gateway with UPI and cards, with a receipt emailed automatically; accepting online payments on WordPress covers the setup. If your gym software already handles billing and renewals, link to its payment page instead of building a second system.

The trial pass is the main conversion

For most gyms and studios, the realistic goal of a first visit to the website is a trial session, not a sale. Make the trial easy to understand and quick to claim:

  1. State exactly what the trial includes: one class, a day pass, a week, or an assessment with a trainer.
  2. Say whether it is free or paid, and whether it is limited to first-time visitors or local residents.
  3. Ask only for name, phone, preferred day and time, and the class or area of interest.
  4. Confirm by WhatsApp with the address, what to bring and where to park.
  5. Follow up after the visit with plan options, once, politely.

Put the trial button in the header, at the end of every class page and on the timetable. A visitor who is reading about a spin class at 9 pm should be able to claim a trial for that class without hunting for the contact page.

Trainer profiles with certifications stated precisely

"Certified trainer" means little on its own. A good profile gives:

  • Name and a recent, natural photo
  • Each certification by name, with the issuing organisation, as it appears on the certificate
  • Specialties: strength, mobility, pre- and post-natal fitness, sports conditioning, yoga styles
  • The classes they take and the days they are usually in
  • Whether they offer personal training, and how to book it

Only list certifications the trainer currently holds, and update profiles when someone leaves. If a trainer offers nutrition guidance, describe their actual qualification; diet advice for medical conditions is a different field, and websites for nutritionists covers how qualified professionals present it.

A facilities tour in real photos

People want to see the floor, the equipment, the changing rooms and the studio space before they commit. A few practical points:

  • Photograph at a normal time of day, not only the empty floor at 5 am
  • Show changing rooms, showers and lockers, because these are what many visitors worry about
  • List equipment by type rather than brand, and keep the list honest
  • Mention air conditioning, ventilation, parking, water and accessibility
  • Get permission from any members who appear, or shoot from angles where they cannot be identified

A short walk-through video, embedded with a light preview, often says more than twenty photos.

Online and hybrid classes

Many studios now run some live online classes or sell recorded programmes. If you do, keep them clearly separate from the in-person offer:

  • A page explaining the format, the platform used, equipment needed and the schedule in IST
  • Separate plans or class packs for online members
  • A members' area for recordings, if you sell them; membership websites on WordPress explains the options
  • Safety notes, since an instructor cannot correct form in person

Member apps and gym software

Larger gyms often run a member app or management system for check-ins, bookings and renewals. The website should work alongside it rather than compete with it. Typical links are a "Book a class" button that opens the app's booking page, app store links on the members' page, and a timetable widget if the provider offers one. What can be integrated depends on the provider and your plan with them, so I would check that with the vendor before promising members anything on the site.

Results, honestly presented

Body-transformation promises are tempting in fitness marketing, and they are risky. "Lose 10 kg in 30 days" sets an expectation nobody can guarantee, can attract complaints, and may fall foul of advertising codes on misleading claims and the ad policies of platforms you run campaigns on. Safer ground:

  • Describe the programme: frequency, coaching, tracking and support
  • Share member stories that focus on consistency, strength, energy or confidence, with consent
  • If you show before-and-after photos, get written consent, avoid edited images, state the time period honestly and note that results vary
  • Never show minors in transformation content

Programmes for people with injuries or health conditions should say when a doctor's clearance is needed.

Yoga studios and personal trainers

Yoga studios need more explanation of styles and levels and less of the equipment; websites for yoga studios and teachers covers that. Trainers working one to one, at home or online, need a personal brand site rather than a facilities site; see websites for personal trainers.

Being found nearby

Gym searches are almost always local: "gym near me", "CrossFit in {area}", "women's gym in {city}". A complete Google Business Profile with accurate hours, real photos and a link to the trial page, plus a page for each class type with the locality named naturally, does most of the work. With several branches, every location needs a page carrying that branch's timetable, plans and photos.

Salons and spas share many of these needs; see websites for salons and spas.

FAQs

Should my gym website show membership prices?

Either can work. Listing prices filters visitors and saves calls; showing plans with price on request suits gyms with frequent offers. Describe what each plan includes in both cases.

Can the website handle class bookings?

Yes, through a booking plugin, or by embedding the booking widget from gym software you already use, if your provider offers one.

Can I post member before-and-after photos?

Only with written consent, without editing, with an honest time period and a note that results vary. Many gyms prefer stories about strength and consistency instead.

How should I show trainer qualifications?

Name each certification and the organisation that issued it, exactly as on the certificate, and keep profiles up to date when trainers join or leave.

Who should update the timetable?

Whoever already manages the schedule at the front desk. A good build gives them a simple editing screen, so changes take minutes and need no developer.
