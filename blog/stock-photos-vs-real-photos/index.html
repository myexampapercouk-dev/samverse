Stock Photos vs Real Photos: Which Should Your Business Website Use?

Where stock photos are fine on a business website, where real photos build more trust, what stock licences allow, and how to get usable photos of your own.

Key takeaways
  • Use real photos wherever a visitor could think "that's them" or "that's what I'll get", such as team, premises, work and products.
  • Stock images are fine for abstract banners, blog posts and hard-to-photograph concepts, but always check the licence terms first.
  • A phone, daylight, a shot list and one organised day can give most businesses usable photos, as long as they are resized and compressed.

Almost every new website runs into the same question: use polished stock images now, or wait for photos of the real business? Stock photos are quick and look professional, but visitors have seen them many times before. Here's how to decide where each belongs, what to check in a stock licence, and how to make your own photos good enough to use.

Why real photos usually win

People choosing a clinic, contractor, restaurant or consultant want to know who they'll deal with and what they'll get. A real photo answers that question. A stock photo only decorates the page.

  • They prove you exist. Your actual shopfront, team and work show there's a real business behind the website.
  • They set honest expectations. A customer who visits your showroom or clinic should recognise it from your website.
  • They're unique. The same image of a handshake or a smiling receptionist appears on countless other sites, sometimes including your competitors'.
  • They match your other listings. Photos on your Google Business Profile are meant to show your actual business, and a website that looks consistent with them feels more believable.

Real photos of you and your team are also one of the simplest ways to make an About page build trust.

When stock photos are perfectly fine

Stock isn't forbidden. It works well when the image illustrates an idea rather than claiming to show your business:

  • Background images behind a headline, such as textures, skylines or landscapes
  • Blog post images that illustrate a topic, like a calculator on an article about tax deadlines
  • Concepts that are hard to photograph, such as "data security" or "exporting worldwide"
  • Destination images for a travel business, as long as they're licensed and not passed off as your own trip photos
  • Temporary placeholders at launch, with a clear plan and date to replace them

Where stock photos hurt

Stock becomes a problem when it pretends to be you. Avoid it for:

  • Team and "meet the doctor" sections: models in suits or scrubs suggest the real people are hiding
  • Your premises: a glossy office that isn't yours misleads visitors who later walk in
  • Portfolios, projects and before-and-after results: for interior designers, builders, clinics and salons, showing work that isn't yours is simply misleading
  • Testimonials: a stock face next to a review makes a genuine review look fake
  • Food and products you sell: customers expect the dish or product they receive to look like the photo

A useful test: if a customer could reasonably think "that's them" or "that's what I'll get", the photo should be real.

Stock photo licensing: free doesn't mean unrestricted

Every stock image comes with a licence, and it's worth reading before you publish. Things to check:

  • Royalty-free is not the same as free. It usually means you pay once (or download from a free site) and can reuse the image within the licence terms.
  • Editorial-only images, such as news events, recognisable brands or public figures, generally can't be used to promote a business.
  • Sensitive uses like health conditions, debt or personal problems are often restricted when a recognisable person is shown, because it implies something about the model.
  • Logos and trademarks: most licences don't allow stock images to form part of your logo.
  • Attribution: some free images require a credit, so check each site's current terms.

Never take images from Google Images, Pinterest or another business's website. "Found online" is not a licence, and it can lead to copyright notices or takedown requests. Keep a simple record of where each image came from and its licence, stored alongside the file. AI-generated images raise the same trust questions as stock and come with the tool's own terms, so don't use them to depict your "team" or "office". If you're unsure whether a use is allowed, ask the stock site or a lawyer.

Making real photos work on a small budget

You don't need a studio. Most businesses can get a solid set of photos with a recent phone and one well-organised day.

  1. Write a shot list from your website pages: homepage banner, team, each service, premises and work samples.
  2. Clean up first: tidy counters, remove clutter, switch on the lights and ask the team to wear their usual uniform.
  3. Use daylight: shoot near windows or outdoors in soft light, and avoid harsh midday sun.
  4. Shoot wide and in landscape for banners, leaving space for text.
  5. Capture people doing things: a technician at work or a chef plating food beats a stiff line-up.
  6. Get consent from anyone recognisable, especially customers, patients and children.

Our guide to preparing photos for your website covers phone photography, editing and file preparation in more detail. For hotels, restaurants, real estate, interiors and product catalogues, where visuals drive the decision, a professional photographer for the key images is often worth it.

Mixing stock and real photos

Most sites end up using both. A simple rule of thumb:

SectionBest choice
Homepage bannerReal photo of your work, place or team; stock only if it's abstract
About and teamAlways real
Service pagesReal photos of the service being delivered; stock for supporting concepts
Portfolio and case studiesAlways real
TestimonialsA real photo with permission, or no photo at all
Blog postsStock images or simple graphics are fine

Keep the style consistent. If your own photos are warm and natural, choose stock images with similar lighting and colours, and avoid heavily posed shots that clash with the real ones.

Don't let photos slow the site down

Stock or real, oversized images are one of the most common reasons business websites load slowly, especially on mobile data. Stock downloads are often huge, so never upload the original file straight from the stock site. Resize images to the size they're actually displayed, compress them, use WebP where possible, and give each one a descriptive file name and alt text. See image optimization for WordPress for the details.

Original photos have one more advantage: they give search engines something unique to show in image results, which a stock image published on many other sites rarely does.
