Websites for Medical Equipment Suppliers and Distributors: Catalogues Hospitals Can Evaluate, Quotations and Tenders, Registrations and After-Sales Service

Plan a medical equipment supplier website: a catalogue hospitals can evaluate, spec sheets, RFQ and tender enquiries, registrations, AMC and dealer pages.

Key takeaways
  • Write for everyone who signs off a purchase: the biomedical engineer wants specifications, the purchase team wants documents and a quote form, and the clinician wants to know how it fits their work.
  • Publish only the registrations, licences and certificates you actually hold, with numbers, validity and the products they cover, and check current medical device rules first.
  • Give after-sales service its own pages, with AMC and CMC terms, engineer locations and spares support, because service often decides the order.

A hospital rarely buys equipment because of a website, but it often decides which suppliers to ask for a quotation after looking at one. The supplier that makes the shortlist is the one whose site lets a biomedical engineer check specifications, a purchase officer download documents and send an RFQ, and a doctor see that installation, training and service are properly handled. Your website's job is to make that evaluation quick.

For me this is a B2B catalogue project of the kind described on my page about websites for manufacturers and B2B suppliers: product categories with specification tables, brochure and datasheet downloads, a quote request on every product, certifications, and enquiries that reach the right person fast. Your buyers include clinics and individual doctors as well as hospitals, and a habit from my websites for doctors and clinics carries straight across: describe medical use factually, and never promise outcomes.

Who signs off a hospital purchase

A single order can pass through several hands, and each person reads your website for something different:

PersonWhat they checkWhat helps them
Clinician or head of departmentWhether the equipment suits their cases and workflowClear application notes, accessories, consumables and a demo request
Biomedical engineerTechnical specifications, power and site requirements, service supportFull spec tables, installation needs and service network details
Purchase or procurement officerCompany details, documents and the quotation processCompany profile, certificate copies, an RFQ form, GST and contact details
Owner of a smaller clinicRunning cost over the equipment's life, space neededConsumables and AMC information, warranty terms, a quick call-back
Dealer or sub-dealerWhether to stock or represent your rangeA dealer page with territories and the support you give

A site written for only one of these, usually the supplier's own owner, leaves the rest to phone you. Each row deserves an obvious route on the site.

Product pages a biomedical engineer can use

Many supplier catalogues show a photo, a model name and three bullet points copied from a brochure. Engineers need more than that to put you on a comparison sheet. Taking a patient monitor as the example, a useful product page covers:

  1. What it is for: the settings it suits, such as ICU, ward, operation theatre or ambulance, in factual terms
  2. Specifications in a table: parameters measured, display, alarms, battery backup, connectivity, dimensions and weight, as the manufacturer publishes them
  3. Configuration: what comes in the box and which modules are optional, so quotes compare like with like
  4. Consumables: what the unit uses, whether compatible alternatives work, and whether you stock them
  5. Installation needs: power, space, environment and any network or gas connections
  6. Warranty and service: what is covered, who services it and where your engineers are based
  7. Downloads: brochure, datasheet and any document the manufacturer allows you to share
  8. A quote request button that carries the model name into the form

Group the catalogue the way hospitals budget and search: by department (critical care, operation theatre, radiology, laboratory, physiotherapy, home care) and then by product type. Models that differ only in size or channel count can share one family page with a comparison table. Write your own descriptions rather than pasting the manufacturer's text, which already appears on dozens of dealer sites; building an industrial product catalogue covers spec tables and categories in more depth.

Brochures and datasheets that stay current

Downloads are often the most-used part of a supplier site, and the most neglected. A few rules keep an outdated brochure out of a tender file:

  • Name files clearly, with brand, model and date, so a purchase officer can tell versions apart
  • Remove a brochure when the model is discontinued, and redirect its page to the replacement
  • Share a manufacturer's documents only with their permission, and keep your own company profile separate
  • Keep the specifications on the page as text, not only inside a PDF, so they can be found in search and read on a phone

Quotations, tenders and rate contracts

Private hospitals and clinics usually ask for a quotation. Government hospitals and many trusts buy through tenders or rate contracts, and some public buyers use government procurement portals such as GeM. The website supports each route differently.

The quotation request

Keep the form short but specific: product or model, quantity, facility type and city, department, whether installation and training are needed, and the buyer's name, organisation and phone number. Let buyers attach a specification sheet they want matched. On the Vansh Group site I built for an electronics manufacturer, the enquiry form opens with a service-type dropdown so each enquiry arrives already sorted; for a medical supplier, a department or product category field does the same job. Request-for-quote forms for B2B websites covers fields and follow-up.

Tenders

Never publish bid prices on the site. A short page for government and institutional buyers can instead list the documents you can supply for a tender, such as company registration, GST details, product registrations, certificates and manufacturers' authorisation letters, and give a named contact for tender queries. If you are registered on public procurement portals, say which.

Registrations, licences and certificates: publish what you hold

In India, medical devices are regulated by the Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation under the Medical Devices Rules, and licensing has been extended to more classes of device in recent years. What applies to you depends on each device's risk class and on whether you manufacture, import or distribute it. The rules and their deadlines have changed more than once, so confirm the current position with the manufacturer or a regulatory adviser before writing anything about it.

On the website itself, the safe approach is simple:

  • List each licence, registration or certificate you hold, with its number, the issuing body and its validity
  • Say which products a certificate covers; a quality management certificate for your company is not a certificate for every product you sell
  • Call yourself an authorised distributor only for brands that have appointed you, and keep the letters ready to share
  • Avoid phrases such as "fully approved" or "government certified" that suggest more than you hold
  • Keep any medical claim within what the manufacturer's approved literature says

Service, AMC and CMC: the page that wins the next order

Equipment that sits idle waiting for an engineer costs a hospital money and its patients time, so many buyers weigh service as heavily as price. Give after-sales support its own section rather than a line in the footer, and explain your contract options in your own terms. The table shows one common way to frame them; your definitions are what count:

ItemAnnual maintenance contract (AMC)Comprehensive maintenance contract (CMC)
Preventive maintenance visitsIncluded, as scheduledIncluded, as scheduled
Breakdown visitsState whether includedUsually included
Spare partsOften charged separatelyOften included, with listed exclusions
Consumables and batteriesState your termsState whether excluded
Calibration and service reportsState what the hospital receivesState what the hospital receives

Beside the table, say where your service engineers are based, which cities or districts you cover, how breakdown calls are logged (phone, WhatsApp or a form), what training staff get at installation, and how long you support spares for older models. Dealers, sub-dealers and territories

Many suppliers sell partly through a network. A dealer page should explain which territories are open, what you provide (training, demo units, marketing material, service backup), what you expect (a showroom, a trained engineer, minimum stock) and how to apply. If you list dealers for buyers to contact, keep the list current, with each dealer's district and phone number; a list with dead numbers sends hospitals straight to a competitor. Hospitals planning a new wing look for suppliers with local support, and websites for hospitals shows what those buyers are building on their own side.

Reaching clinics, labs and smaller buyers as well as hospitals

Large hospitals buy through committees and tenders, but many suppliers also sell to nursing homes, diagnostic labs, dental and physiotherapy clinics, and home-care customers. These buyers search differently and need a slightly different path through the site.

Pages by buyer type

Short landing pages for each segment, such as equipment for diagnostic labs or for new clinics, let you group the relevant products, mention the usual questions (space, power supply, training, consumables) and link to the right catalogue categories. A clinic owner setting up a new practice often wants a checklist more than a single product page.

Consumables and repeat orders

Many suppliers earn steadily from consumables, spares and accessories. Make these easy to find from each equipment page and offer a simple repeat-order or quotation request for them, so customers do not switch to whichever supplier answers the phone first.

Rental and refurbished equipment

If you rent equipment or sell refurbished units, give them their own clearly labelled section. State what testing and servicing a refurbished unit goes through and what support comes with it, without making claims you cannot document.

City and region pages that are genuinely useful

If you have service engineers or stock in specific cities, say so on pages for those cities, with the areas covered and the typical service arrangement. Avoid creating near-identical pages for places you do not actually serve.

FAQs

Should a medical equipment supplier show prices on the website?

For capital equipment, usually not, because configuration, accessories, installation and service terms all change the price. For consumables and small devices, a listed price or a quick price request on each item can save calls.

Can we use the manufacturer's photos and brochures?

Only with their permission, which authorised distributors normally have. Write your own product descriptions even when you use their images, and replace documents when new versions come out.

Should every model have its own page?

Models for different uses or departments should. Models that differ only by size or channel count can share a family page with a comparison table.

Can buyers attach specifications to a quote request?

Yes. The form can take PDFs and spreadsheets, checked for file type and size, and the notification email can carry a secure download link, which keeps large files out of your inbox.

How should we show our medical device registrations?

List each one you hold with its number, issuing body, validity and the products it covers, and check the current rules before claiming anything for products you only distribute.

Should a medical equipment supplier show prices on its website?

Usually not for capital equipment, because price depends on configuration, accessories, installation and service terms. Consumables and smaller items can carry prices or price ranges, with a quotation request for bulk and institutional orders.
