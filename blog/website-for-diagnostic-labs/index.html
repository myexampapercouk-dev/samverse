Websites for Diagnostic Labs and Pathology Centres: Test Catalogue and Preparation, Home Sample Collection, Report Download, Packages, Centres and Doctor Referrals

What a diagnostic lab website needs: a searchable test catalogue with preparation notes, home collection booking, report access, packages and centre pages.

Key takeaways
  • The test page is the core of a lab website: what the test is for, sample type, preparation, reporting time and price if you publish it.
  • Home collection booking works best when it shows the areas and time slots you can really serve, and asks only for what the phlebotomist needs.
  • Report download usually means linking or embedding your lab software's patient portal, so check what your vendor supports before planning the page.

A diagnostic lab website should let someone find a test, understand how to prepare for it, book a home collection or walk-in, and later get the report, all from a phone and without calling. Most of that depends on one thing done well: a test catalogue where each test page answers the practical questions clearly.

The build follows the same principles as the websites I build for doctors and clinics: accurate medical content that comes from you, booking that is easy on mobile, and local SEO so people nearby find you. A lab adds a large catalogue, collection logistics and report access on top.

The test page is the unit of the whole site

People search for individual tests far more often than for a lab's name: "HbA1c test near me", "thyroid profile price", "vitamin D test fasting required". Each test, or each closely related group, deserves a page that a patient can read in a minute. A useful structure:

FieldWhat to writeWhy it matters
Test name and other namesThe formal name plus common names patients usePatients search with the everyday name
What it measuresTwo or three plain sentences, reviewed by your pathologistBuilds understanding without giving a diagnosis
Sample typeBlood, urine, stool, swab, and the volume if relevantPrepares the patient for what will happen
PreparationFasting, timing, medicines to mention, what to avoid beforehandWrong preparation means repeat visits
Reporting timeA typical range, stated honestlySets expectations and reduces calls
Price and home collectionThe price if you publish it, and whether home collection is availableThe two questions that decide the booking
Included in packagesLinks to packages that contain this testOften better value for the patient

A lab with hundreds of tests does not need hundreds of hand-written pages on day one. Start with the most requested forty or fifty, and list the rest in a searchable catalogue with name, sample type and price. Expand the popular ones as you see which pages people visit.

Search that understands how patients type

Patients misspell test names and use abbreviations: "TSH", "thyroid", "T3 T4", "LFT", "liver test". The catalogue search should match synonyms and short forms, not just exact titles. Most of this can be done by adding alternative names to each test record so the search picks them up.

Preparation instructions, written carefully

Preparation notes are the most useful content on a lab site, and the most important to get right. The wording should come from your pathologist or lab head, not from a general article. A few points that help:

  • State the fasting rule in hours, and say what "fasting" allows, such as plain water
  • Mention timing where it matters, such as samples that should be taken in the morning
  • Ask patients to tell the phlebotomist about medicines they take, rather than telling them to stop any medicine
  • Give separate notes for children, pregnant women or older patients where preparation differs
  • Add a printable or shareable version, because families often forward these to the person being tested

Never let the site suggest stopping a prescribed medicine before a test. That decision belongs with the treating doctor.

Home sample collection booking

Home collection is often the main reason people choose one lab over another. The booking flow should be quick, and honest about what you can serve:

  1. The patient enters a pin code or chooses a locality, and the site confirms whether you collect there.
  2. They choose the tests or a package, or simply describe what the doctor has asked for.
  3. They pick a date and a time slot from the slots you actually run, with early morning slots marked for fasting tests.
  4. They give name, age, gender, phone and address. That is usually enough for the collection visit.
  5. A confirmation on screen and by SMS or WhatsApp states the slot, the preparation needed and how payment works.

Publish your collection areas as a list or map, and the home collection charge if there is one. If a slot fills up, it should disappear from the form rather than being accepted and cancelled later. Online appointment booking for websites compares the tools that can run slot-based booking.

Avoid asking patients to upload a doctor's prescription through an open form unless you have a secure, access-controlled place for it. Prescriptions and test requests are sensitive health data. The phlebotomist can see the prescription at the visit.

Report download and the patient portal

Patients expect to get reports online, and most labs already have laboratory software that generates them. The website's job is usually to link to that system's patient portal, or embed its login, rather than store reports itself. Before planning this page, ask your software vendor:

  • Whether they provide a patient login page or a report link sent by SMS
  • Whether the portal can be placed on a subdomain of your own website
  • How patients are verified, such as a registered mobile number with a one-time code
  • Whether doctors who referred the patient can have their own access

Storing reports on the WordPress site itself is rarely a good idea. Keep the website for information and booking, and let the lab system handle the medical records it was built for.

Health packages that say exactly what they include

Package pages convert well, but vague names do not help anyone. For each package, list every test included with the number of parameters, the preparation needed, who the package is meant for, and the price. Avoid wording that suggests a package can rule out disease or replace a doctor's advice. Packages built for a particular group, such as older adults or women, should still read as a list of tests, not a promise.

Accreditation, stated precisely

NABL accreditation and similar quality marks matter to patients and doctors, but only claim what you hold. Accreditation is often granted for a defined scope of tests at a specific laboratory, not for every test or every collection centre in a network. If you are accredited:

  • Name the accrediting body and the accredited laboratory location
  • Link to or describe the scope, so patients can see which tests it covers
  • Use the logo only as the accrediting body's current rules allow
  • Keep the certificate details up to date when it is renewed

Check the current rules for using accreditation marks with the body that issued yours. Registration under your state's clinical establishment rules, where they apply, is a separate matter to confirm with your adviser.

Collection centres, one page each

Labs with several collection centres should give each its own page: address, landmark, map, timings including Sundays, phone and WhatsApp, and which services run there, such as ECG or X-ray. Match each page to that centre's Google Business Profile. Websites for multi-location businesses explains the structure, and local pages without doorway pages covers how to keep centre pages genuinely different from each other.

A section for referring doctors

Many lab tests start with a doctor's recommendation. A short section for doctors can cover:

  • The full test menu as a downloadable list with sample types and reporting times
  • How sample pick-up from clinics works, and which areas you cover
  • How doctors receive reports for their patients
  • A contact for your pathologist, for questions about results

Keep this informational. Any arrangement with doctors should follow medical ethics rules, so take advice before offering anything that could look like an incentive for referrals.

Speed, structure and search

A catalogue of hundreds of pages needs clean structure to be found. Use one URL per test, consistent titles that include the test name and city, internal links between tests and packages, and schema markup that describes the business correctly. FAQs

Should a lab publish test prices on its website?

Many patients compare prices before booking, so published prices help. If prices vary by centre or change often, show a starting price and keep the list easy for your team to update.

Can patients download reports from the lab website?

Usually through your lab software's patient portal, linked or embedded on the site. Check with your vendor what it supports before planning the page.

Who should write the test preparation instructions?

Your pathologist or lab head. I can structure and format them for mobile reading, but the medical content has to come from your team.

Can we show NABL accreditation on every page?

Only in the way the accrediting body's rules allow, and only for the scope and location it covers. State it precisely rather than as a general badge.

How should home collection areas be shown?

As a pin code checker or a list of localities, with the collection charge and available time slots, so patients know before booking whether you serve them.

If your lab's website lists tests but does not help people book, prepare or find their reports, it can be rebuilt around those tasks. The website for doctors and clinics page sets out what a healthcare build includes.
