Websites for Furniture Showrooms and Manufacturers: Made-to-Order and Ready Stock, Dimensions and Materials, Measuring Guides, Delivery and Assembly, Showroom Visits and Custom Quotes

Plan a furniture website: ready stock and made-to-order products, dimensions and materials, measuring guides, delivery, showroom visits and fast images.

Key takeaways
  • Sell ready-stock pieces with prices and a cart, and handle made-to-order work through a quote flow that asks for sizes, materials and room photos.
  • Every product page needs exact dimensions, materials, finishes and care, plus a measuring guide so buyers know the piece will fit through the door and into the room.
  • Furniture sites are image-heavy, so resize and compress photos and lazy-load galleries, or the pages that should sell will load too slowly on a phone.

Someone buying a sofa, bed or dining table online is asking four questions: will it fit, will it last, how will it reach my home, and what will it really cost? A furniture website that answers those on every product page, sells ready-stock pieces directly and turns custom work into well-qualified quote requests will do better than a pretty gallery with a phone number.

For most furniture businesses that means a WooCommerce store for products with fixed prices, Indian payment gateways, COD rules where you allow them, shipping rules and order management, combined with enquiry forms for made-to-order work. Because furniture sites carry so many large photos, speed needs attention from the start; the WordPress speed optimization work I do often begins with exactly this kind of image-heavy store.

Ready stock, made to order, or both

How you make and sell furniture decides how the site works. Most businesses fit one of three patterns:

Ready stockMade to orderHybrid
Product pagesFixed price, stock status, add to cartStarting price or "price on request", options listedStandard sizes in the cart, custom sizes by quote
Main actionBuy online or reserve for pick-upRequest a quote or book a showroom visitBoth, clearly separated on each page
Delivery promiseA dispatch window from stockA making time quoted per orderDifferent windows for each, stated on the page
PaymentFull payment or COD rules at checkoutAdvance on order confirmation, balance before deliveryBoth, depending on the item

Whichever you choose, say it clearly near the price. A customer who adds a made-to-order bed to the cart expecting delivery next week will be a cancelled order and an unhappy review.

"Will it fit?" Dimensions, materials and a measuring guide

Most furniture returns and disputes start with size. Every product page should carry a full specification:

  • Overall width, depth and height, and seat height for chairs and sofas, in both centimetres and inches
  • For beds, the mattress size it takes and the overall footprint, which is larger than the mattress
  • For storage, internal dimensions of drawers and shelves
  • The smallest dimension when packed or dismantled, which decides whether it goes through a door or up a staircase
  • Weight, and whether the piece arrives assembled or flat-packed

A measuring guide on its own page, linked from every product, helps buyers check their space before ordering: measure the room and the walls, the doors and lift, the turn of the staircase, and allow walking space around the piece. A downloadable sheet or a simple diagram is more useful than a fancy room planner, which takes real work to build and maintain. If you want something interactive, start with a few room templates showing standard furniture sizes, and see how buyers use them.

Materials and finishes, described honestly

"Solid wood" means different things to different sellers. Name the actual material of each part: sheesham, teak, mango wood, engineered wood with veneer, MDF with laminate, metal frames. Say what the finish is and how it should be cared for. Show close-up photos of the wood grain, fabric weave and joinery. Fabric and finish swatches should be photographed in daylight, with a note that screens show colours differently. Precise wording here prevents more disputes than any returns policy.

Product options without a hundred duplicate pages

A sofa in three sizes and twelve fabrics is one product, not thirty-six. Use product variations for size, fabric and finish, with each variation able to carry its own price and photos. Customers then pick their options on one page and see the price change. WooCommerce product variations explains how to set this up, including how to stop the option list becoming overwhelming.

"How will it reach me?" Delivery and assembly

Delivery is where furniture differs most from other online shopping. Publish:

  • The cities or pin codes you deliver to, and whether delivery outside them is possible through a freight partner
  • Delivery charges, or whether delivery is included, by zone
  • Dispatch times for ready stock and making times for custom orders, as ranges
  • Whether assembly is included, and whether the team carries items up stairs where there is no lift
  • How damage on arrival is handled: inspect before signing, photographs, and the replacement or repair process
  • Return and cancellation terms, especially for custom pieces, which usually cannot be returned

A pin code checker on product pages lets buyers see delivery availability before they fall in love with a piece; a WooCommerce pin code checker shows how.

Showroom visits

For higher-value furniture, many buyers want to sit on the sofa before paying. Treat the showroom visit as a conversion in its own right. A showroom page should give the address, hours, parking and photographs of the floor, and a short form to book a visit with a preferred date and the pieces the customer wants to see. Mark on product pages which items are on display at which showroom. If you have several showrooms, give each its own page with a map and its own Google Business Profile.

The custom quote flow

Made-to-order enquiries are valuable but slow to qualify by phone. A quote form that asks the right questions lets your team reply with a sensible estimate:

  1. What the customer wants made: the type of piece, or a link to a product to adapt
  2. Approximate dimensions, or a note that they need a site visit to measure
  3. Preferred material and finish, with "not sure" as an option
  4. Room photos and any reference images
  5. City, timeline and approximate budget
  6. Name, phone and preferred contact method

The Studio Agama Interiors website, built for an interior design studio that also offers custom furniture, uses a similar approach: its enquiry form asks for the type of project, its stage, the budget and the time frame, so each request arrives with enough context for a proper first call. If most of your custom work is carpentry on site rather than factory-made pieces, websites for carpenters is the closer guide.

Photos that sell without slowing the site

Furniture is sold by photographs, and furniture sites are often slow for the same reason. Every product may have eight to fifteen images, plus room settings and close-ups. To keep pages quick on mobile data:

  • Resize images to the largest size they are displayed at before uploading, not the camera's full resolution
  • Compress every file, and let the site deliver WebP versions to browsers that support them
  • Lazy-load gallery images below the first one, so the page appears quickly
  • Avoid autoplay sliders and heavy 360-degree viewers on category pages
  • Keep category pages to a sensible number of products per page

Image optimisation for WordPress explains the process in detail. Use styled room photos to inspire, but always include plain shots on a neutral background so the buyer can see the piece itself.

Trade and bulk buyers

Interior designers, architects, hotels, offices and builders buy differently from homeowners. They want specification sheets, trade pricing, bulk lead times and someone who understands a project schedule. A short trade page with its own enquiry form, asking for the company, the project, quantities and the delivery date, keeps these enquiries away from the retail cart. Downloadable catalogues with dimensions and material options help them present your pieces to their own clients.

Related products and categories

Buyers furnishing a room often want matching pieces. Link sets together, such as a bed with its side tables and dresser, and organise categories by room as well as by type. If you also sell mattresses, soft furnishings or rent furniture, those deserve their own sections; websites for mattress and sleep brands and websites for home decor stores cover those ranges.

Selling fitted kitchens and wardrobes rather than loose furniture? See websites for modular kitchen and wardrobe companies.

Cafés, hotels and offices also need existing seating re-covered between refits; if that is your trade, see websites for upholstery and sofa repair services.

FAQs

Should a furniture business sell online or just show a catalogue?

Sell ready-stock pieces with fixed prices online, and use quote requests for custom work. Many businesses do both on the same site, with each product clearly marked.

How do we stop buyers ordering furniture that does not fit?

Give full dimensions and packed sizes on every product, link to a measuring guide, and mention door, lift and staircase checks on the product page.

Can customers choose fabric and size on one product page?

Yes. Product variations let buyers choose size, fabric and finish on a single page, with the price and photos updating to match.

Why is our furniture website slow?

Usually oversized images, heavy sliders and too many plugins. Resizing and compressing photos, lazy-loading galleries and removing unused scripts often make a large difference.

Can we take an advance online for custom furniture?

Yes. A quote can be sent with a payment link for the advance, with the balance taken before delivery, and the terms stated on the quote and the website.
