Online Donations for NGOs and Trusts: Donation Pages, 80G Receipts, FCRA, Transparency and Volunteers

NGO donation websites explained: cause pages, payment gateways, 80G receipts and FCRA with your CA, transparency pages, volunteer forms and impact stories.

Key takeaways
  • Donors give when the website answers their doubts: who you are legally, what their money will do, whether a tax benefit applies and what happened after they gave.
  • Mention 80G, 12A, FCRA or CSR registration only if the organisation genuinely holds it, collect PAN only from donors who want 80G, and confirm receipts and foreign donation rules with your CA.
  • Transparency pages, simple volunteer forms and impact stories shared with written consent build the trust that brings repeat and monthly giving.

An NGO website raises money when it answers a donor's doubts before they reach the payment button: is this organisation real and properly registered, what exactly will my money do, do I get a tax benefit, is paying online safe, and will I hear what happened? Get those answers right, add a short donation form backed by a payment gateway, and handle 80G and foreign donations exactly as your chartered accountant advises, and online giving becomes a steady source of support rather than an occasional bank transfer.

I build websites for temples, trusts and NGOs with online donation pages, receipts sent by email, events, history and impact pages, galleries and volunteer sign-up forms, in Hindi or other languages where needed. This guide is about the donor's side of an NGO website and the rules that shape it.

For the hands-on setup, from gateway documents to UPI QR codes and receipt contents, see how to set up online donations on a temple, trust or NGO website. Nothing here is tax or legal advice: confirm the details with your CA.

Donation pages that explain the cause

A bare form titled "Donate" asks for trust it hasn't earned. Give each programme or appeal its own page, with the form on it:

  • The need, in a paragraph: who you help, where, and why it matters now.
  • What you actually do, with photos from your own work.
  • Suggested amounts tied to real things, such as a month of school supplies or a medical camp, using costs your accounts support. If you can't stand behind a figure, leave it out.
  • A "where it's needed most" option, so donors can give without restricting the money to one programme.
  • Progress towards a goal, only with real figures that someone updates.
  • A short FAQ: receipts, 80G, monthly giving and who to contact.

If donors choose a purpose, your accounts need to honour it, so agree with your CA how restricted donations are recorded before you offer a long list of purposes.

Payment options and gateways

Most NGOs offer three routes: a donation form connected to a payment gateway as the main route, a UPI QR code for people at events or your office, and bank details for large donors and companies. Gateways check your registration documents, a current account held by the organisation and your website's policy pages before activating, and their fees, settlement times and support for monthly giving differ. Compare them on what matters to you rather than on brand; choosing a payment gateway in India lists the questions to ask.

Monthly giving is worth offering once one-time donations work. Where your gateway supports card mandates or UPI AutoPay, a monthly option on the form, with an easy way to cancel, gives you predictable support.

80G: what the website should say and do

Section 80G lets eligible donors claim a tax deduction for donations to organisations that hold a valid 80G registration. The website's job is to state your status accurately and collect what is needed, not to promise a tax outcome. The Income-tax Act, 2025, which replaced the 1961 Act from April 2026, renumbers many provisions and forms, so the section and form names your CA uses may differ from the familiar ones:

  • Only if you hold it. Show the 80G registration number and its validity period. Under the current rules, approvals are generally granted for a limited period and must be renewed, so update the page when yours is renewed. Without 80G, tell donors plainly that their gift won't qualify for the deduction.
  • PAN only when needed. A tick box such as "I want an 80G receipt" reveals PAN and address fields, so other donors are not asked for them.
  • Acknowledgement, then certificate. The email sent straight after payment is an acknowledgement. At the time of writing, donors claim the deduction with a certificate, Form 10BE, that is generated only after the organisation has filed its annual statement of donations with the tax department, so explain this in the acknowledgement along with the timing your CA confirms.
  • Cash. Cash donations above a limit set in the Income Tax Act are not eligible for the deduction, which is one more reason to encourage online payment.
  • The rate of deduction depends on how the organisation is registered, so don't state a figure your CA hasn't confirmed.

Your 12A registration, which concerns the organisation's own tax exemption, can sit alongside 80G on the registration page.

Foreign donations and FCRA

Under the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, an NGO generally needs FCRA registration or prior permission to accept contributions from foreign sources, and those contributions must be received in its designated FCRA bank account. What counts as a foreign source, including how donations from Indian citizens living abroad are treated, has specific rules, so take professional advice. For the website, that means:

  • Without FCRA: switch off international cards, and add a declaration on the form that the donor is an Indian citizen.
  • With FCRA: route foreign donations separately so they reach the designated account, and show your FCRA registration number.
  • Disclosure: FCRA-registered organisations have reporting and disclosure duties for foreign contributions, which can include publishing details on their website. Confirm the current requirements and who will keep that page updated.

Transparency pages for the sceptical donor

Many donors, and most companies considering CSR funding, look for a page that sets out:

  • The legal identity: registration certificate details, PAN, and 12A, 80G, FCRA and CSR-1 numbers, each only where held
  • Governance: the names of trustees or board members, and key staff
  • Annual reports and audited financial statements as downloadable files, year by year
  • How funds are split between programmes and administration, only as your accounts show it
  • Your privacy policy (see privacy policies for Indian websites), a refund policy for mistaken donations and, if you work with children, your safeguarding approach

Volunteer and in-kind forms

Many supporters can give time or goods rather than money. A volunteer form should ask only what you need: name, phone, city, availability, skills and areas of interest, with a consent line and a link to your privacy notice, in line with India's DPDP Act; DPDP basics for small business websites covers what notice and consent involve. Say who will get in touch, and if volunteers will work with children, explain your screening process. For in-kind donations, list what you accept and what you can't use, with drop-off times, so you don't receive goods you have to turn away.

Impact stories, with consent and dignity

Stories persuade more than statistics, but they involve real people who often have little power to object. Before publishing:

  • Get written consent from the person, and a parent or guardian for children, explaining where the story and photos will appear, and remove them promptly if consent is withdrawn
  • Avoid full names with exact locations for vulnerable people; change names where needed and say so
  • Choose photos that show people with dignity, not images designed to provoke pity
  • Use impact figures only from your own records

After a donation, a thank-you page and an immediate acknowledgement start the relationship, and an occasional email newsletter with programme updates, sent only to donors who agreed to receive it, is what turns first-time donors into regular ones.

FAQs

Can our NGO website issue 80G certificates automatically?

The website can send an instant acknowledgement with the transaction details. The deduction certificate itself (Form 10BE at the time of writing) comes later, once the organisation has filed its statement of donations, and only an organisation with valid 80G registration can issue it. Your CA will confirm the process and timing.

Can we accept donations from abroad on our website?

Generally only with FCRA registration or prior permission, and into the designated FCRA bank account. Without it, switch off international cards and add an Indian-citizenship declaration to the form. Get professional advice before accepting any money from abroad.

Which payment gateway is best for an NGO?

The one that will onboard your type of organisation, supports UPI and the payment methods your donors use, offers monthly giving if you want it, settles funds on terms that suit you and has fees you are comfortable with. Check current terms with each provider.

Do we need to publish our accounts on the website?

It is strongly advisable. Annual reports and audited statements are what serious donors and CSR teams look for, and some registrations bring their own disclosure duties, so confirm those with your CA.

Can donors set up monthly donations?

Yes, where your gateway supports recurring payments through card mandates or UPI AutoPay. Make cancelling easy and send an acknowledgement for every payment.
