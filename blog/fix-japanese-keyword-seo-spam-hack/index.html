Japanese Keyword Hack and SEO Spam on WordPress: How to Find the Cloaked Pages, Clean Your Site and Clear Google Results

Fix the Japanese keyword hack on WordPress: confirm cloaked spam in Search Console, remove hacker-added owners, delete the generator and clear spam URLs.

Key takeaways
  • The Japanese keyword hack creates cloaked spam pages that only Google sees, so confirm it with site: searches and the URL Inspection live test rather than by visiting your site.
  • Remove any Search Console owner or sitemap the hacker added, including their verification token, then delete the spam generator and every backdoor at the same time.
  • Let spam URLs return 410 or 404, never redirect or block them, use the Removals tool for brand searches, and expect a large hack to take weeks or months to clear.

The Japanese keyword hack fills your domain with auto-generated pages in Japanese, usually selling counterfeit or discounted brand goods, and hides them so only Google sees them. To fix it, confirm the spam in Search Console, remove any owner or sitemap the hacker added, delete the code that generates the pages along with its backdoors, let the spam URLs return 410 or 404, and then request a review and use the Removals tool for the worst URLs. The same steps work for pharma, gambling and other SEO spam hacks.

Your homepage can look perfectly normal while thousands of these pages sit in Google's index. If you would rather hand it over, my WordPress malware removal service covers SEO spam hacks: generated pages, cloaking code in theme files and unknown owners added in Search Console, plus the review request afterwards.

This guide covers the SEO spam itself: confirming it, finding the generator and clearing the spam out of Google. For the rest of the cleanup, follow how to remove malware from WordPress step by step.

What the Japanese keyword hack is, and its relatives

All SEO spam hacks have the same aim: borrow your domain's trust in Google to rank pages the attacker profits from. They differ in what the spam looks like and where it is stored.

TypeWhat you see in GoogleWhat is usually on the server
Japanese keyword hackPages with Japanese titles and snippets, often brand names with words for "cheap" or "sale"A PHP script that builds pages on request plus rewrite rules, or folders of generated HTML files
Pharma hackMedicine names in your titles or descriptions, or spam pages selling pillsCloaking code in theme files, injected database content
Gambling or "slot" spamCasino and betting pages, often in Indonesian or ThaiGenerated pages or spam posts, sometimes on a forgotten subdomain
Gibberish hackPages stuffed with random keywords, sometimes redirecting to scam sitesMany static HTML files dropped into one folder
Hidden link injectionNothing obvious; spam links appear only in the version of your real pages that Google seesInjected code in theme files or post content that only shows to bots

Why you can't see the spam pages

Most SEO spam is cloaked. The code checks who is asking: Googlebot gets the spam page, while you get your normal site, a 404 or a redirect to your homepage. Some versions check the user agent, some check for Google's IP addresses, and some target only visitors arriving from a Google search. Logged-in administrators are usually skipped, which is why the hack can run for months unnoticed.

Step 1: Confirm it with site: searches

Search Google for site:yourdomain.com and scroll through several pages of results. Then narrow it down:

  • site:yourdomain.com japan or site:yourdomain.com 激安 (a Japanese word for "very cheap" that often appears in this spam)
  • site:yourdomain.com casino, viagra or slot for the other variants
  • site:yourdomain.com inurl:html if your real pages don't end in .html
  • site:yourdomain.com -site:www.yourdomain.com to show subdomains you may have forgotten

Note a few spam URLs and look for a pattern: a shared folder, a query string such as a short code followed by numbers, or a file extension your site doesn't use. You will need that pattern later. Result counts for site: searches are rough estimates, so don't read much into the number.

Step 2: Check Search Console

Search Console is where the hack becomes clear, and where attackers leave footprints. If the site isn't verified yet, verify it as a Domain property so subdomains are included.

Security issues and Manual actions

Under Security & Manual Actions, the Security issues report may list "Hacked: URL injection" or "Hacked: content injection" with sample URLs. Check Manual actions too, because spam on your domain can also lead to a separate manual action that needs its own reconsideration request.

Users and permissions: look for a hacker-added owner

Attackers often verify themselves as owners of your property so they can submit their spam sitemaps and watch how the pages perform. Open Settings, then Users and permissions, and check every name. Search your inbox for Google's new owner notification emails as well.

  • A delegated user can be removed straight away from the same screen.
  • A verified owner keeps access for as long as their verification token exists. Find it first: an HTML file in your site's root named google followed by a code, a google-site-verification meta tag in your theme or header settings, or a TXT record in your DNS. Delete the token, then remove the owner under Manage property owners.

If you remove the owner but leave the token, they can simply verify again.

Sitemaps

Open the Sitemaps report and remove any sitemap you didn't submit. Then delete the sitemap file itself from the server, because removing it from Search Console doesn't stop Google fetching a file it already knows about. Also open your robots.txt: some attackers add a Sitemap line pointing to their spam sitemap.

Pages and Performance

A sharp rise in indexed pages in the Pages report, or Japanese queries and unfamiliar URLs in the Performance report, shows roughly when the hack started and how big it is. Note the date: it helps you judge whether any backup is clean.

Step 3: Prove the cloaking with URL Inspection

To see what Google sees, paste one of the spam URLs into the inspection bar at the top of Search Console and click Test live URL. When the test finishes, open View tested page and look at the HTML and the screenshot.

  • The live test shows Japanese content, but the same URL in your browser shows a 404 or your homepage: the spam is cloaked and still active on the server.
  • The live test returns a 404 or 410: the generator is gone for that URL, and Google just needs to recrawl it.
  • Your real pages: test your homepage and a key service page too. Spam links or a Japanese title in the tested HTML mean your genuine pages are infected as well.

Faking Googlebot with a browser extension doesn't always work, because some cloaking checks Google's real IP addresses. The live test comes from Google itself.

Step 4: Find and remove the spam generator

The generator is rarely a WordPress page you can delete from the dashboard. Look in these places:

  • .htaccess in the site root: rewrite rules that send unknown or spam-pattern URLs to a PHP file other than index.php
  • Unfamiliar PHP files and folders in the root, wp-content, wp-includes or uploads, often beside a large keyword or template file
  • The top of index.php, wp-config.php and the theme's functions.php: code that reads the user agent or referrer
  • Folders full of .html files with random names
  • The database: spam posts, or spam stored in options and loaded by a small injected file
  • Other sites and subdomains in the same hosting account

With SSH access, these commands find recently changed PHP files, files that mention Googlebot (cloaking code usually does, though so do genuine SEO plugins) and core or plugin files that don't match the official copies:

find . -type f -name "*.php" -mtime -30 -not -path "./wp-content/cache/*"
grep -ril "googlebot" --include="*.php" .
wp core verify-checksums
wp plugin verify-checksums --all

Deleting the generator stops new spam pages, but attackers almost always leave other ways back in. Remove them at the same time using how to find and remove WordPress backdoors, change every password and close the entry point. Pirated software is a common source of this kind of hack; see the risks of nulled themes and plugins.

Step 5: Remove the spam URLs from Google

Once the generator is gone, Google has to recrawl each spam URL and see that it no longer exists. You can't delete URLs from the index directly, but you can make the signal clear.

OptionWhat it doesUse it?
410 GoneTells Google the page was removed on purposeYes, for URL patterns that are clearly spam
404 Not FoundTells Google the page doesn't exist; WordPress returns this by default once the generator is goneYes, fine for everything else
Removals toolHides URLs from results for about six months, at the time of writingYes, for spam showing on brand searches
Block in robots.txtStops Google crawling the URLs, so it can't see they've goneNo
Redirect to the homepageGoogle may treat it as a soft 404 and keep the spam URLs around longerNo

410 or 404?

Both work. A 410 is a slightly clearer signal that a page is gone for good, but in practice the difference is small. If the spam URLs share a folder, a rule that returns 410 for them is tidy. On Apache or LiteSpeed hosting, this .htaccess rule, placed above the WordPress block, does it:

RewriteEngine On
RewriteRule ^spam-folder/ - [G,L]

Replace spam-folder with the real pattern, and only use a rule like this when every URL it matches is spam. Test a few genuine pages afterwards. On Nginx your host can add the equivalent, and redirect plugins such as Redirection can also return a 410 for a pattern.

The Removals tool

Use Removals in Search Console for the spam that appears when people search your business name. "Remove all URLs with this prefix" helps when the spam sits in one folder. It only hides results temporarily, so use it on URLs that already return 404 or 410, never as a substitute for cleaning.

Sitemaps

Resubmit your real sitemap so Google recrawls your genuine pages. A temporary sitemap listing the removed spam URLs can prompt Google to revisit them sooner; it is optional, so delete it once they've dropped out.

Step 6: Request a review and watch for a return

If the Security issues report lists a problem, request a review once the whole site is clean. The process and what to write are covered in removing "This site may be hacked" from Google. Google's guidance has said reviews for spam hacks can take longer than other security reviews, sometimes weeks.

Spam URLs drop out gradually as Google recrawls them, so a large hack can take weeks or months to clear completely. For the next few weeks, repeat your site: searches, watch spam URLs move to Not found (404) in the Pages report, recheck Users and permissions and Sitemaps, and look for newly modified files on the server. New spam means something was missed. Once it has stopped, recovering rankings after a hack covers getting your real pages back to where they were.

FAQs

What is the Japanese keyword hack?

An SEO spam hack that creates large numbers of Japanese pages on your domain, usually advertising counterfeit or discounted goods. The attacker uses your site's reputation in Google to rank them and sends searchers on to their own shops.

Why can't I see the Japanese pages when I visit my site?

Because they are cloaked: the code shows spam to Googlebot and something harmless to everyone else. Search Console's URL Inspection live test shows you what Google sees.

How long until the Japanese spam disappears from Google?

It depends on how many spam URLs were indexed and how often Google recrawls them. A small infection can clear within weeks; a large one can take months. The Removals tool hides the worst ones on brand searches in the meantime.

Will the Japanese keyword hack hurt my rankings?

It can: spam pages compete with your real ones, a "This site may be hacked" label reduces clicks, and injected content drags genuine pages down. Rankings usually recover once the site is clean and recrawled, though nobody can promise how quickly.

The spam came back after I deleted it. Why?

Usually a backdoor, an owner still verified in Search Console, a cron job that reinstalls the files, or another infected site in the same hosting account. Remove all of them at once, then change every password.
