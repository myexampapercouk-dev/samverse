Website or Hosting Account Sending Spam Emails? How to Find the Source, Stop It and Get Off Blacklists

Website or hosting account sending spam? Find the source in mail logs, remove mailer scripts, secure leaked SMTP logins and get your IP off blacklists.

Key takeaways
  • Spam from a hosting account usually comes from a malicious mailer script, a stolen mailbox or SMTP password, or an abused form, and the mail logs and message headers tell you which.
  • Ask your host to pause outgoing mail, keep a sample of the spam, fix the cause and change every mailbox password before you request delisting from Spamhaus or any other blacklist.
  • Send website email through authenticated SMTP with SPF, DKIM and DMARC in place, so abuse is easier to spot and spoofed mail is easier for receivers to reject.

If your host says your account is sending spam, or you're getting bounce messages for emails you never sent, the cause is usually one of three things: a mailer script uploaded to the website after a hack, a mailbox or SMTP password that has been stolen and used to send through your server, or a form or plugin being abused to email strangers. Ask your host to pause outgoing mail, use the mail logs and message headers to find which one it is, fix it, and only then deal with blacklists.

Mailer scripts usually sit in PHP files in uploads or old plugin folders, and finding them is part of a proper cleanup. If you would rather hand it over, my WordPress malware removal service finds and removes them along with the backdoor that put them there.

If your host has already suspended the whole account, start with what to do when hosting is suspended for malware. This guide covers the email side: tracing the source, stopping it and repairing your sending reputation.

Is it really your server?

Before you start digging, make sure the spam is actually leaving your hosting account. Spammers can put your address in the From line without touching your server at all, and the bounces then come back to you.

What you noticeLikely causeHow to tell
Host warning about outgoing spam, or a sending limit reachedSomething in the account is sendingThe host's logs show the script or mailbox
Bounces for emails you never sent, but no host warningOften spoofing: someone forging your address elsewhereThe bounced message's headers show a server that isn't yours
Your genuine emails rejected with a blocklist messageYour server's IP or your domain is listedThe rejection names the list, often Spamhaus or similar
Contacts receive odd emails from your real mailboxThat mailbox's password is compromisedSent items, login history or authenticated sending in the logs
Hundreds of contact form or registration emailsBots abusing a formForm entries and plugin logs

If it turns out to be spoofing, nothing on your server needs cleaning. What helps is SPF, DKIM and a DMARC policy that tells receivers to reject mail that fails them; see SPF, DKIM and DMARC explained.

Step 1: Stop the sending, but keep a sample

Ask your host to suspend outgoing mail for the account or domain while you investigate. On shared hosting only the host can do this; on a VPS with WHM you can use the mail queue manager yourself. Many hosts also cap how many emails a domain can send per hour, and hitting that cap is often what triggered the warning. Messages over the limit are usually deferred or rejected, so the queue fills up.

Before the queue is cleared, save two or three of the spam messages with their full headers, or ask the host for them. They are the quickest route to the source, and deleting everything first throws that evidence away.

Step 2: Find the source

Read the headers

Open a spam message or a bounce, view the original or full headers, and look for these lines, which servers running cPanel and PHP commonly add:

  • X-PHP-Originating-Script: names the PHP file that sent the email. A file in uploads, or one with a random name, is your mailer script.
  • X-Get-Message-Sender-Via or X-Authenticated-Sender: shows whether the mail came from a script running as your hosting user or from a mailbox that logged in with its password.
  • Received lines: read from the bottom up to see which server the message really started from. If none of them is yours, it is spoofing.

Check the mail logs

On shared hosting, ask your host to look for you. With root access on a cPanel server, the Exim log shows which folder sent mail through PHP, and which mailboxes logged in to send:

grep "cwd=" /var/log/exim_mainlog | grep -o "cwd=[^ ]*" | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head
grep -o "A=dovecot_[a-z]*:[^ ]*" /var/log/exim_mainlog | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head

The first command counts messages by the folder they were sent from, so a burst from a folder inside wp-content stands out. The second counts messages by authenticated mailbox, so one account sending far more than usual points to a stolen password.

Look for mailer scripts

If the evidence points to a script, check uploads and any random-named folders for PHP files, look at files changed around the time the spam started, and check hosting cron jobs, because some mailers run on a schedule. A mailer is usually dropped through a backdoor, so finding one means the site is compromised; work through finding and removing WordPress backdoors as well as deleting the script.

Check for leaked SMTP or mailbox passwords

If spam is going out through a real mailbox, its password has leaked. Common routes are a phishing email imitating your webmail login, a weak or reused password, an infected computer, or a hacked website: SMTP plugins often store the mailbox password in the database, where anyone who gets into the site can read it. Some SMTP plugins let you keep it in wp-config.php instead, which is better, though a full compromise can still reach it. API keys for transactional email services can leak the same way.

Check forms and plugins

Forms that send a "copy to yourself" to whatever address the visitor types, or that let the visitor choose the recipient, can be turned into spam relays. Registration forms abused by bots send a welcome email for every fake account. Turn off copy-to-sender options, add bot protection and update the plugin; see how to stop contact form spam.

Step 3: Fix the cause

  • Mailer script: delete it, then clean the site fully and close the hole the attacker used, or another one will appear.
  • Compromised mailbox: change its password from a clean device, then change every other mailbox password on the domain. Check for forwarders, filters and auto-replies the attacker may have added, and turn on two-factor login for webmail if your provider offers it.
  • Leaked SMTP or API credentials: rotate the password or key, update the website's settings and revoke the old key in the email service's dashboard.
  • Abused form: fix or replace the form, add bot protection and remove options that send mail to visitor-supplied addresses.

Then ask your host to clear the queue and restore outgoing mail, and watch the sending volume for a few days.

Step 4: Get off blacklists

Check your server's IP address and your domain on a multi-list lookup tool such as MXToolbox, then on the lists that matter most for delivery. Each list has its own process, and all of them expect the problem to be fixed before you ask.

List or providerWhat it coversHow removal usually works
SpamhausIP lists and a domain list, used by many mail serversLook up the IP or domain on Spamhaus's site and follow the removal steps shown for that listing
BarracudaIP reputation used by Barracuda filtersA removal request form on its lookup page
SpamCopIPs reported by recipientsListings expire on their own once reports stop
Microsoft (Outlook, Hotmail)Its own sender reputationMicrosoft's delisting portal for senders
GmailIts own domain and IP reputationNo delisting form; reputation recovers as clean mail flows, and Google Postmaster Tools shows how Gmail sees your domain

On shared hosting, the sending IP belongs to your host and is shared with other customers, so IP delisting is the host's job; tell them the cause is fixed. A domain listing is yours to handle. Don't request removal while spam is still going out: you'll be relisted quickly, and some lists make repeat removals harder.

Step 5: Send website email properly from now on

  • Send WordPress emails through an authenticated SMTP account or a transactional email service rather than PHP's basic mail function; see fixing WordPress email with SMTP.
  • Publish SPF, DKIM and DMARC records, and read the DMARC reports for mail sent in your name from places you don't recognise.
  • If all your website email goes through SMTP, ask your host whether PHP's mail function can be disabled for your account, so a dropped mailer script has nothing to use.
  • Use strong, unique mailbox passwords, keep plugins updated and keep an eye on sending volume.

FAQs

Why is my website sending spam emails?

Usually because a malicious script was uploaded after a hack, a mailbox or SMTP password was stolen, or a form is being abused. The message headers and your host's mail logs show which.

How do I check if my domain or IP is blacklisted?

Use a multi-list lookup such as MXToolbox for both your server's IP and your domain, then check Spamhaus directly. A bounce message often names the list that rejected you.

How long does it take to get off a blacklist?

It depends on the list. Some remove a listing soon after a request once the problem is fixed, and some expire automatically when spam stops. Reputation with big providers such as Gmail improves gradually as clean mail flows.

Someone is sending emails from my address, but my site is clean. What's happening?

That is spoofing: another server is forging your From address. You can't stop the sending itself, but SPF, DKIM and a DMARC policy of quarantine or reject tell receivers to bin it.

Will changing my email password stop the spam?

Only if a stolen mailbox password is the cause. If a script on the website is sending it, the spam continues until the script and its backdoor are removed.
