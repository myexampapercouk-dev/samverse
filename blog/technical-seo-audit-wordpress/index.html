WordPress Technical SEO Audit: A Practical Checklist (With WooCommerce Checks)

A practical WordPress technical SEO audit checklist: indexing, sitemaps, robots.txt, canonicals, redirects, Core Web Vitals, schema and WooCommerce checks.

Key takeaways
  • Start with what Google can see: the Search Console Pages report, URL Inspection, the WordPress visibility setting, robots.txt and a clean XML sitemap.
  • Then make sure every page has one clean version: HTTPS, single-step redirects, correct canonicals, no indexable duplicates and no broken internal links.
  • Prioritise by impact: fix indexing blockers and site-wide template issues first, then key-page speed, structured data, internal links and tidy-up work.

Great content can't rank if search engines struggle to crawl, index or understand your site. A technical SEO audit finds those hidden problems: pages Google can't reach, duplicate versions competing with each other, broken redirects, slow templates and settings that quietly tell Google to stay away. This checklist is written for WordPress and WooCommerce sites, in the order I'd work through it. Run it once a year, after a redesign or migration, or whenever traffic drops unexpectedly (see what to check when traffic drops).

Technical SEO is about whether search engines can find, crawl and index your pages properly; on-page SEO is about what each page says. Both matter, and the on-page SEO checklist covers the second part.

Tools for a WordPress technical audit

  • Google Search Console: the most important tool, because it shows what Google itself has crawled and indexed. If it isn't set up yet, see setting up Search Console
  • PageSpeed Insights: speed and Core Web Vitals for individual URLs, with real-visitor data where Google has enough of it
  • A website crawler: a desktop or online tool that follows your links the way a search engine does and lists every URL with its status code, title, canonical and click depth
  • Your SEO plugin settings: many technical issues come down to a single setting
  • A real phone and "view source": to check what visitors and search engines actually receive

New to the reports? See Search Console reports explained.

A simple way to start: compare three lists

Before working through the checklist, gather three lists of URLs: what your crawler finds by following links, what your XML sitemap contains, and what Search Console reports as indexed. Comparing them shows a lot quickly:

  • In the sitemap but not found by the crawler: likely orphan pages with no internal links
  • Found by the crawler but not in the sitemap: either pages missing from the sitemap, or junk URLs (parameters, archives) that shouldn't exist
  • In the sitemap but not indexed: pages Google has chosen to skip, which need a closer look

1. Crawling and indexing

Start here, because nothing else matters if important pages aren't in Google's index.

  • Search Console > Pages: how many pages are indexed vs not indexed, and the reason given for each group
  • URL Inspection on your key pages: is each one indexed, and which canonical has Google chosen?
  • Settings > Reading: "Discourage search engines from indexing this site" must be unticked on the live site
  • No accidental noindex on important pages, or on whole post types in your SEO plugin
  • Thin pages (empty tag archives, internal search results, author archives on single-author sites) deliberately kept out of the index where needed

Not every "not indexed" status is a problem; redirects and deliberately noindexed pages belong there. See page indexing errors explained.

2. XML sitemaps and robots.txt

  • One sitemap setup, not two: WordPress has a built-in sitemap at /wp-sitemap.xml, and SEO plugins usually replace it with their own
  • Only indexable, canonical URLs that load normally: no redirects, 404s or noindexed pages
  • No unwanted content types listed, such as page-builder templates, attachment pages or plugin-created post types
  • The sitemap submitted in Search Console, with no errors in the Sitemaps report
  • robots.txt not blocking important content, CSS or JavaScript; check the robots.txt report in Search Console settings
  • robots.txt not used to hide pages from the index: blocking crawling doesn't remove an indexed page, and it stops Google seeing a noindex tag

Background reading: XML sitemaps and robots.txt explained.

3. One version of every page: HTTPS and canonicals

  • HTTPS everywhere, with a valid certificate and no mixed content warnings (see fixing mixed content)
  • http to https, and www to non-www (or the reverse), redirecting in one step to your preferred version
  • Consistent trailing slashes, so /page and /page/ don't both load as separate pages
  • A canonical tag on every page pointing to its own preferred URL, not to the homepage or a redirected address
  • Paginated archives (page 2, page 3) canonicalised to themselves, not all to page 1
  • Staging or temporary domains blocked from indexing, ideally with a password

More detail: canonical tags explained.

4. Redirects, 404s and broken links

  • No broken internal links pointing to 404 pages; your crawler will list them
  • Old URLs redirected (301) to their closest new equivalent
  • No redirect chains or loops; update internal links to point straight to the final URL
  • No "soft 404s": near-empty or "not found" pages that still return a normal status
  • Redirects managed in one place, either a plugin or the server, not scattered across both
  • A helpful custom 404 page (see 404 pages that help)

See 301 vs 302 redirects for which to use when.

5. Core Web Vitals and speed

  • Search Console > Core Web Vitals: which groups of URLs are flagged for LCP (loading), INP (responsiveness) or CLS (layout shift)
  • PageSpeed Insights on one URL of each template: homepage, service page, blog post, category and product page
  • Real-visitor data given priority over the lab score, which is a diagnostic rather than a ranking
  • The usual WordPress causes checked: oversized images, no page caching, slow hosting, heavy page builders and third-party scripts

Fixing a template fixes every page that uses it. See Core Web Vitals explained.

6. Mobile experience

Google mainly uses the mobile version of a page for indexing and ranking, and it has retired its standalone mobile-friendly test and mobile usability report, so check on real phones and with Lighthouse in PageSpeed Insights.

  • The same content and internal links on mobile as on desktop
  • No horizontal scrolling, tiny text or tap targets that are too close together
  • Pop-ups and chat widgets not covering the main content

7. Structured data

  • Organization or LocalBusiness, BreadcrumbList, Article and Product markup where relevant
  • Only one plugin or theme outputting schema, so markup isn't duplicated or conflicting
  • Markup that matches what's visible on the page, such as prices, ratings and business details
  • No errors in Search Console enhancement reports, and key templates checked in the Rich Results Test

Google has reduced some rich results, such as FAQ results, for most sites, so accurate markup describes your content but doesn't guarantee extra features. See schema markup explained and how to test structured data.

8. Internal links and site structure

  • No orphan pages that nothing links to (see fixing orphan pages)
  • Important pages reachable within a few clicks from the homepage
  • Service and category pages linked from the menu, the homepage and relevant articles
  • Normal HTML links, not buttons that only work with JavaScript

See internal linking explained.

9. Duplicate and thin pages

  • Unique title tags and meta descriptions on important pages; a crawler will list duplicates
  • No near-duplicate pages targeting the same search
  • Tag, category, date and author archives either improved or kept out of the index
  • Attachment pages redirected to the file or parent post (newer WordPress installs turn them off; older sites may still have them)
  • Filter, sort and tracking parameters not creating indexable copies of the same page

More detail: duplicate content and category and tag pages.

10. WordPress and SEO plugin settings

  • Only one SEO plugin active; two can output duplicate titles, canonicals and sitemaps
  • Readable permalinks, and no slug changes without redirects (see SEO-friendly URLs)
  • Title templates that produce sensible titles for posts, pages, products and archives
  • Index and noindex settings checked for each post type and taxonomy
  • Hreflang set up correctly if you run more than one language version

11. Security and manual actions

  • No security issues or manual actions reported in Search Console
  • No unfamiliar spam pages showing in a site: search, which can be a sign of a hack
  • WordPress, plugins and themes up to date

Extra checks for a WooCommerce technical SEO audit

  • Filters and sorting: colour, size, price and sort parameters not creating thousands of indexable URLs
  • Add-to-cart links: URLs with add-to-cart parameters not being crawled and indexed
  • Cart, checkout and My Account: kept out of the index and sitemap
  • Out-of-stock and discontinued products: temporarily unavailable products kept live and marked out of stock; discontinued ones redirected to the closest alternative or category
  • Product structured data: price, availability and reviews accurate, with no errors in Search Console's product reports
  • Category pages: useful introductory text, and not dozens of near-empty categories
  • Product descriptions: not copied word for word from the manufacturer on every product

See WooCommerce SEO and product filters and SEO.

How to prioritise the fixes

An audit usually produces a long list. Sort it by impact and effort, and work in this order:

PriorityWhat it covers
1. BlockersImportant pages noindexed, blocked or missing from the index; site-wide HTTPS, redirect or canonical errors; hacked content
2. Site-wide template issuesProblems that affect many pages at once: duplicate titles, wrong canonicals, slow post or product templates
3. Key-page fixesBroken links, redirect chains and Core Web Vitals issues on pages that bring enquiries or sales
4. ImprovementsStructured data, internal links, thin archives and general tidy-up

Within each group, start with the pages that matter most to the business. Record what you changed and when, use "Validate fix" in Search Console where it's offered, and recheck after a few weeks, because Google needs time to recrawl.

FAQs

How often should I run a technical SEO audit on WordPress?

A full audit once a year suits most business sites, with a quick monthly look at Search Console. Run one straight away after a redesign, migration, domain change or unexplained traffic drop.

Can I do a WordPress SEO audit myself?

Yes, for the basics. Search Console, PageSpeed Insights and a crawler will show most issues, and the one-hour DIY website audit is a good starting point. Fixing redirects, templates, canonicals and speed safely is where developer help is usually worth it.

What is the best tool for a technical SEO audit?

Search Console comes first because it shows Google's own view of your site. Add PageSpeed Insights for speed and a crawler for broken links, redirects and duplicate titles. No single tool covers everything.

Will a technical audit improve my rankings straight away?

Not on its own. Fixing blockers can help once Google recrawls, but most fixes remove obstacles rather than add rankings. Content, relevance and links still decide where you rank.

How long does a technical SEO audit take?

It depends on the size of the site. A small business site can be reviewed much faster than a large WooCommerce store with thousands of products and filter URLs.
