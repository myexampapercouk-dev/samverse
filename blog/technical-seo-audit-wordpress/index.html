WordPress SEO Audit: A Practical Step-by-Step Checklist (With WooCommerce Checks)

How to do a WordPress SEO audit step by step: indexing, crawl errors, speed, on-page SEO, schema and WooCommerce checks, plus what to fix first.

Key takeaways
  • A WordPress SEO audit checks whether Google can crawl and index the right pages, whether they load fast, and whether each page is clearly targeted and linked.
  • Start with Search Console and a site crawl: indexing errors, noindex mistakes, redirects, broken links and duplicate pages usually matter more than small on-page tweaks.
  • Prioritise fixes by impact: blockers first, then templates that affect many pages, then individual page improvements.

A WordPress SEO audit is a structured check of whether Google can crawl, index and understand your site, and whether your pages are fast, well targeted and well linked. To do one, start with Google Search Console and a site crawl to find indexing and technical problems, then check WordPress settings, speed, on-page SEO, structured data and (for stores) WooCommerce-specific issues, and fix them in order of impact.

Short answer: check indexing, crawlability, speed, on-page basics, structured data and content quality, in that order. Blockers such as a site-wide noindex, broken redirects or pages excluded from the index come first; title tweaks come last.

What you need before you start

  • Access to Google Search Console and Google Analytics.
  • An admin login to WordPress and your hosting panel.
  • A site crawler (several have free versions that cover small sites).
  • A spreadsheet to log each issue, the pages affected, impact and who will fix it.
  • A recent backup, in case any fix needs undoing.

Step 1: Check indexing in Search Console

Open the Pages report. It shows how many pages are indexed and why others are not. Pay attention to:

  • Excluded by noindex: are any important pages in this list?
  • Crawled, currently not indexed and Discovered, currently not indexed: usually thin, duplicate or poorly linked pages.
  • Not found (404) and Soft 404: broken URLs that may need redirects.
  • Duplicate without user-selected canonical: pages Google sees as copies.
  • Blocked by robots.txt: check nothing important is blocked.

Our guide to Search Console page indexing errors explains each status. Also run a site:yourdomain.com search to spot spam pages, staging URLs or tag archives that should not be in Google.

Step 2: Check WordPress settings that block SEO

  • Search engine visibility: in Settings, Reading, make sure "Discourage search engines from indexing this site" is unticked. It is often left on after launch.
  • Permalinks: use a readable structure such as post name, not ?p=123.
  • SEO plugin settings: check which post types and taxonomies are set to noindex, and that only one SEO plugin is active.
  • Attachment pages: media attachment pages should redirect to the file or parent post, not be indexed as thin pages.
  • Tag and category archives: decide which are useful and noindex or prune the rest (see category and tag pages for SEO).

Step 3: Crawl the site

Run a crawler from your homepage and review:

  • Status codes: 4xx and 5xx errors, plus internal links pointing to redirects. Fix links at source. See finding and fixing broken links.
  • Redirect chains and loops: point every old URL straight to the final one. See 301 vs 302 redirects.
  • Canonicals: each indexable page should have a self-referencing canonical, and none should point to redirected or noindexed URLs.
  • Orphan pages: pages with no internal links. Compare the crawl with your sitemap (see fixing orphan pages).
  • Crawl depth: important pages should be reachable within three clicks of the homepage.
  • Duplicate titles and descriptions: often from paginated archives or product variations.

Step 4: Check robots.txt and the XML sitemap

Open yourdomain.com/robots.txt. It should not block CSS, JavaScript or important folders, and it should point to your sitemap. Then open the sitemap and check:

  • It only lists indexable pages that return 200.
  • No noindexed, redirected or thin archive pages are included.
  • It is submitted in Search Console with no fetch errors.

See robots.txt explained and XML sitemaps explained.

Step 5: Check speed and Core Web Vitals

In Search Console, open the Core Web Vitals report, which uses real visitor data. Then test key templates (homepage, a service page, a post, a product) in PageSpeed Insights. Common WordPress causes of poor scores are heavy page builders, too many plugins, unoptimised images, render-blocking CSS and JavaScript, third-party scripts and slow hosting. Our Core Web Vitals guide explains LCP, INP and CLS, and speed test tools explained helps you read the results.

Step 6: Check on-page SEO

  • Each important page targets one main topic and search intent.
  • Titles are unique, around 60 characters or fewer, and include the main keyword naturally (see writing meta titles and descriptions).
  • One clear H1 per page, with logical H2 and H3 headings.
  • Images have descriptive file names and alt text.
  • Internal links use descriptive anchors and connect related pages.
  • No two pages compete for the same query (keyword cannibalisation). Search Console's Performance report, filtered by query, shows when several URLs swap places.

Step 7: Check structured data

Test the main templates in Google's Rich Results Test and review Search Console's enhancement reports. Look for errors, duplicate schema from theme and plugins, and markup that does not match the page. Our guide to testing structured data covers this in detail.

Step 8: WooCommerce SEO audit checks

Stores have extra issues because of products, variations and filters:

  • Filter and sort URLs: parameters like ?orderby= or filter combinations can create thousands of near-duplicate URLs. Make sure they are canonicalised or not crawlable.
  • Out-of-stock and deleted products: keep useful out-of-stock pages live; redirect discontinued products to the closest alternative.
  • Thin product descriptions: manufacturer copy duplicated across many stores.
  • Product schema: price, availability and reviews valid for each product.
  • Cart, checkout and account pages: should be noindexed.
  • Category pages: need some unique introductory content and good internal links.

The WooCommerce SEO guide goes deeper.

Step 9: Check content quality and security

List pages with impressions but no clicks, pages with no impressions at all, and posts that cover the same topic. Merge, improve or remove weak pages. Also confirm the site runs on HTTPS everywhere, has no mixed content warnings and has no hacked spam pages, because security problems can wipe out rankings quickly.

Step 10: Check mobile usability and local signals

Google indexes the mobile version of your site, so open key pages on a real phone, not just a resized desktop browser. Check that menus work, text is readable without zooming, buttons are not too close together, and that nothing important is hidden on mobile that appears on desktop. Pop-ups that cover the content on arrival can also hurt how Google and visitors judge the page.

If you serve customers in particular cities, check the local basics too: your business name, address and phone number match your Google Business Profile, each location or service area has a genuinely useful page, and LocalBusiness schema matches what is on the page. Our local SEO guide for Indian small businesses covers these checks.

Finally, check analytics is tracking correctly. An audit that compares traffic before and after fixes is only useful if the numbers are reliable, so confirm Search Console and Analytics both cover the same domain version (with or without www, and HTTPS).

How to prioritise what you find

PriorityExamples
1. BlockersSite-wide noindex, robots.txt blocking, hacked pages, important pages returning errors
2. Template issuesMissing canonicals, duplicate schema, slow templates, indexable filter URLs
3. Structural issuesOrphan pages, deep pages, redirect chains, weak internal linking
4. Page-level improvementsTitles, descriptions, headings, content depth

Re-run the crawl and check Search Console after fixes. Technical improvements often show within weeks, while content changes can take longer.

FAQs

How do I do a WordPress SEO audit?

Check indexing in Search Console, crawl the site for errors and redirects, review WordPress and SEO plugin settings, test speed, review on-page SEO and structured data, then prioritise fixes by impact.

What is the best WordPress SEO audit tool?

There is no single tool. Google Search Console, a site crawler, PageSpeed Insights and the Rich Results Test together cover most of what an audit needs, and the first and last are free.

How often should I audit my WordPress site's SEO?

A full audit once or twice a year is enough for most business sites, plus a quick check after a redesign, migration, theme change or big traffic drop.

Can an SEO plugin audit my WordPress site for me?

SEO plugins check individual pages and settings, which is useful, but they do not crawl the whole site, assess indexing in Google or judge content overlap, so they do not replace a full audit.

What is the difference between a technical SEO audit and an SEO audit?

A technical SEO audit focuses on crawling, indexing, speed, redirects and structured data. A full SEO audit also covers content quality, keyword targeting and backlinks.

What should a WooCommerce SEO audit include?

Everything in a WordPress audit plus filter and sort URLs, product and category content, out-of-stock handling, product schema and noindexing cart, checkout and account pages.
