Websites for Specialist Doctors: Credentials, Procedures and Second Opinions

What specialist doctors need on a website: verifiable credentials, condition and procedure pages, second opinions, hospital affiliations and careful claims.

Sameer Gupta 1 October 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways
  • Show specific, verifiable credentials such as degrees, registration number and fellowships instead of vague "best doctor" claims.
  • Give each major condition and procedure its own plain-language page, reviewed and dated by the doctor.
  • Add a second opinion page and a clear list of the clinics and hospitals where you consult and operate.

Patients rarely find a specialist by chance. A family doctor has suggested seeing an orthopaedic surgeon, a check-up has flagged something for a cardiologist, or a pregnancy needs a gynaecologist the family can trust. By the time they reach your website, they are comparing you with two or three other names, and often a son or daughter in another city is doing the research for a parent.

This guide covers what a specialist's website needs beyond the basics. For timings, maps, contact buttons and the other essentials every practice needs, start with the clinic website checklist.

What patients check before choosing a specialist

  • Is this doctor experienced in my specific problem, not just the broad speciality?
  • Do they perform the procedure I've been told I may need?
  • Which hospital would I be admitted to, and does it accept my insurance?
  • Can I get a second opinion before deciding on surgery?
  • When and where can I see the doctor, and how do I book?

Your site should answer each of these within a click or two of the homepage.

Credentials that are specific and verifiable

Specialists have years of training behind them, and patients want to see it laid out clearly:

  • Degrees in full, such as MS (Orthopaedics), DM (Cardiology), MD or MS (Obstetrics and Gynaecology) or DNB, with the institution
  • Medical council registration number, so patients and referring doctors can verify it
  • Fellowships and special training, for example in joint replacement, interventional cardiology or high-risk pregnancy, with where they were completed
  • Current and past positions, such as consultant or head of department
  • Areas of focus within the speciality, such as sports injuries, heart rhythm problems or infertility

Leave out vague labels like "best surgeon in the city" and awards nobody can check. Precise facts are more convincing, and health is the area where Google's guidelines put the most weight on expertise and trust; see E-E-A-T explained.

Conditions and procedures pages

A single "Services" page listing twenty items won't help patients or rank in search. Give the main conditions you treat and the procedures you perform their own pages:

SpecialityCondition pagesProcedure pages
OrthopaedicsKnee arthritis, ligament tears, back painKnee replacement, arthroscopy, fracture fixation
CardiologyChest pain, high blood pressure, heart failureEchocardiography, TMT, angiography, angioplasty
GynaecologyPCOS, fibroids, high-risk pregnancyLaparoscopic surgery, hysteroscopy, antenatal care

Each page should explain, in plain language, what the condition or procedure is, common symptoms or reasons it is recommended, how it is diagnosed, the treatment options (including non-surgical ones where they exist), what recovery generally involves, and the questions patients usually ask. Make clear that every patient is different and a consultation is needed for personal advice. Show the doctor as author or reviewer, with the date the page was last reviewed.

A clear second opinion page

Many patients look for a specialist because they want a second opinion before major surgery or a long course of treatment. A dedicated page makes this easy and signals confidence:

  • What a second opinion includes, and what can't be judged without examining the patient
  • What to bring or send: previous reports, scans, imaging CDs, discharge summaries and current medicines
  • Whether it is available in person, by video consultation or both, and the fee
  • A secure way to share reports, rather than asking patients to send scans to a personal WhatsApp number

For video consultations, follow the current telemedicine guidelines. Keep the tone respectful towards the patient's current doctor: the aim is to help them decide, not to win them over by criticising someone else.

Hospital affiliations and where you consult

Many specialists see patients at a private clinic in the morning and at one or two hospitals later in the day, and operate somewhere else again. Patients get confused quickly. A "Where I consult" page should list each location with:

  • Days and OPD timings
  • How to book there: your clinic's number or the hospital's appointment desk
  • Address, map link and parking notes
  • Which hospitals you admit patients to and operate at

State affiliations factually and keep them current; remove a hospital as soon as you stop consulting there, and use hospital logos only with permission. For cashless insurance questions, point patients to the hospital's insurance desk, since the hospital handles it. To see how hospitals present their own departments and doctors, read websites for hospitals.

Responsible claims and patient stories

Careful wording protects both patients and your reputation:

  • No guaranteed outcomes, "painless" promises or success rates you can't support from your own records
  • No "best" or "number one" labels, and no comparisons with other doctors
  • Patient stories and testimonials only with written consent, without identifying details the patient hasn't agreed to share
  • No scans, X-rays or before-and-after images that could identify a patient without consent
  • A clear note about urgent symptoms, especially on cardiology pages: anyone with chest pain right now should go to the nearest emergency department, not fill in a form

India's medical council rules on professional conduct have long restricted how doctors may promote themselves, and the regulations have been under revision in recent years. Check the current National Medical Commission guidance and your state medical council before publishing, and ask a lawyer if you're unsure.

Booking and local search

  • Appointment requests by location, so the patient picks the clinic or hospital first, then a day
  • Call and WhatsApp buttons for patients and families who prefer to talk
  • A Google Business Profile with accurate timings for each location, matching the website
  • Pages that match real searches, such as "knee replacement surgeon in Pune" or "cardiologist in Indore", built on genuinely useful content
  • Schema markup for the doctor, the practice and each location
  • Fast, readable pages, since many visitors are older, or reading on a parent's behalf on a phone
