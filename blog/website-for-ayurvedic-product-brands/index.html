Websites for Ayurvedic and Herbal Product Brands: Ingredients, Claims and Repeat Orders

How Ayurvedic and herbal D2C brands can plan a website: ingredient and usage pages, careful health claims, genuine certifications, reviews and subscriptions.

Key takeaways
  • Confirm whether each product is treated as an Ayurvedic medicine, a cosmetic or a food before writing claims, as the rules differ for each.
  • Give full ingredient, usage and caution details, avoid cure or treatment claims, and have claims reviewed by a regulatory consultant.
  • Show only certifications you genuinely hold, with issuer, scope and validity, and grow repeat orders with fair subscriptions and honest reviews.

Ayurvedic and herbal brands selling direct online, from hair oils and herbal teas to churnas and skincare, have a particular challenge. Shoppers are curious and often loyal, but they are wary of exaggerated claims and want to know exactly what is in a product, how to use it and who makes it. Your website has to inform and sell without promising more than you are allowed to say or can honestly back up. If you run a treatment centre or retreat rather than a product brand, see websites for Ayurveda and wellness centres; for food products sold direct, see websites for organic and D2C food brands.

Know which category each product falls into

"Ayurvedic" covers products that can be regulated quite differently in India. Depending on formulation and licence, a product may be treated as an Ayurvedic medicine, a cosmetic or a food or supplement, and each category has its own rules on licences, labelling and advertising. Ayurvedic medicines, for example, generally need a manufacturing licence from the relevant state AYUSH licensing authority. The category depends on the product and its licence, not on how you would like to market it, so confirm it with a regulatory consultant before writing product pages.

Treat this as a starting point for a conversation with your adviser, not a ruling. The same herb, or even a similar-looking hair oil, can fall into different categories.

Product pages: ingredients, usage and who it's for

Ayurvedic shoppers read labels. Give them everything the pack says, with more context:

  • Full ingredient list with common and botanical names, for example ashwagandha (Withania somnifera), the part of the plant used and the quantities shown on the label
  • How to use it: amount, time of day, what to take or apply it with and for how long, exactly as on the label
  • Who should check first: pregnant or breastfeeding women, parents buying for children, people taking other medicines and anyone with allergies
  • Clear photos of the front and back of the pack, so label details are readable
  • Manufacturer details: who made it and where, including a third-party unit if one makes it for you
  • Pack size, shelf life and storage, and where to find batch and expiry details

Tell your sourcing story honestly too: where herbs come from, how products are made and what quality checks you run, without implying more than you can show.

Careful wording about health claims

This is where Ayurvedic websites most often get into trouble. In general terms, Ayurvedic medicines and cosmetics come under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act and its rules, foods under food safety regulations, and advertising under consumer protection law, along with a separate law restricting advertisements for remedies for certain diseases. ASCI also publishes guidelines on claims. Advertising of Ayurvedic and herbal products has drawn close attention in recent years and interpretations can change, so have a regulatory consultant or lawyer review your claims. Some habits suit every brand:

  • Don't say a product cures, treats or prevents a disease, and keep disease names out of product titles and ads
  • Avoid "no side effects", "100% safe" and "natural, so it can't harm you"
  • Don't promise guaranteed, permanent or quick results
  • Describe traditional use cautiously, only where it is accurate and fits your product's category and licence
  • Don't say "doctor recommended" or "clinically proven" unless you have the evidence and are allowed to say it
  • Have health articles written or reviewed by a qualified Ayurvedic practitioner, named only if they genuinely did the work

Herbal skincare raises similar questions; see websites for beauty and cosmetics brands for more on cosmetic claims and influencer disclosures.

Certifications, only where genuinely held

Certificates build trust only when they are real and clearly explained. Examples might include GMP certification for the manufacturing unit, organic certification for specific ingredients or third-party lab reports. For each one you actually hold, show:

  • What it is and who issued it
  • What it covers: the unit, an ingredient or a specific product
  • Its validity, with a copy available to view or on request

Be careful with loose phrases. "AYUSH approved", "government certified" or "certified organic" can mislead if what you hold is a manufacturing licence, or if only one ingredient is certified. Describe exactly what you hold, product by product, and remove badges when a certificate lapses.

Reviews that help without becoming claims

  • Ask for reviews a few weeks after delivery, once customers have actually used the product
  • Publish critical reviews too, and reply politely
  • Never write, buy or reward fake reviews
  • Agree a moderation policy for reviews claiming a product cured an illness; featuring them can turn a customer's words into your claim, so ask your adviser how to handle them
  • Get permission before using a review or customer photo in ads, and ask influencers to label paid partnerships

Subscriptions, bundles and repeat orders

Many Ayurvedic products are used daily, which suits repeat buying:

  • Subscriptions for oils, teas and other daily-use products, timed to how long a pack lasts, with easy pause, skip and cancel; see selling subscriptions with WooCommerce for how recurring payments work in India
  • Refill reminders by email or WhatsApp, sent only with consent
  • Bundles and gift boxes for festivals and corporate gifting, presented as routines or gift sets rather than treatment courses for a condition
  • Trial sizes so new customers can try a product before committing
  • Payments through an Indian gateway with UPI and cards, and COD with sensible limits if your customers expect it

Store setup, SEO and selling abroad

WooCommerce gives you full control over product pages, ingredient content and SEO. Build pages around how people search for ingredients and product types, such as "bhringraj hair oil" or "what is triphala", rather than disease-led pages promising a remedy. Add product schema so price, availability and ratings can appear in Google, and keep the site fast despite rich photography. If you plan to ship abroad, check each destination country's rules for herbal products first, as they can differ a lot from India's.
