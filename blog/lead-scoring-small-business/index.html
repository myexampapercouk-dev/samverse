Simple Lead Scoring for Small Businesses: Know Which Enquiries to Call First

Simple lead scoring for small businesses: enquiry details that signal serious buyers, scoring in a spreadsheet or CRM, and prioritising follow-up fairly.

Sameer Gupta 1 October 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways
  • Score each enquiry on a few fit and intent signals, such as a clear requirement, timeline, location and budget.
  • A simple Google Sheet with 0 to 2 points per signal and three follow-up bands is enough to start.
  • Use scores to prioritise, never to ignore: every lead gets a reply, and small leads can grow into big ones.

When a few enquiries arrive each week, you can call everyone straight away. When dozens arrive from your website, ads, WhatsApp and phone calls, you need a quick, consistent way to decide who to call first and how much effort each one deserves. That's all lead scoring is: giving each enquiry a few points based on what it tells you. You don't need expensive software. A spreadsheet and some honest thinking about your past customers are enough to start.

What lead scoring is, and what it isn't

A lead score adds up points for details that, in your experience, tend to come with people who go on to buy. A higher score means a faster, more personal follow-up.

Signals usually fall into two groups:

  • Fit: is this the kind of customer you serve? Location, service needed, size of job, type of business.
  • Intent: how ready are they? A clear timeline, a specific request, asking for a visit or a call.

Lead scoring is a way to prioritise, not a filter for ignoring people. It's also a rule of thumb, not a prediction. You'll refine it as you learn which signals really matter for your business.

Enquiry details that signal a serious buyer

Start by looking back at your last few months of enquiries, both the ones that became customers and the ones that didn't. Common signals include:

SignalWhy it can matter
A specific requirement"Quote for a 3 kW rooftop system" shows more thought than "send price"
A timelineNeeding something this month usually means a decision is close
Location in your service areaYou can actually serve them without extra cost or delay
Budget range that fitsExpectations match what you offer
The page or form usedA detailed quote form or a pricing page suggests more intent than a general contact form
Referral or past customerTrust already exists
Asked for a call, visit or demoThey want the next step, not just information

Your own history matters more than any generic list. If most of your best clients come by referral, weight referrals heavily. If out-of-area enquiries almost never convert, give them fewer points.

Build a simple scoring sheet

Keep the first version deliberately simple:

  1. Choose four to six signals that clearly separate good leads from poor fits
  2. Give each signal 0, 1 or 2 points
  3. Add a total column
  4. Set three bands, such as hot, warm and nurture, each with a follow-up rule

Here's an illustrative example for an interior design studio. Adapt the signals and points to your own business.

SignalPoints
Clear requirement (rooms, property type)2 if detailed, 1 if partial, 0 if vague
Possession or start date2 within a month, 1 within three months, 0 if unsure
Inside your service area2 yes, 0 no
Budget range2 if it fits, 1 if "not sure", 0 if far below
Referral or repeat client2 yes, 0 no

With these numbers, you might treat 7 or more as hot, 4 to 6 as warm and 3 or less as nurture. The exact cut-offs matter less than applying them consistently.

Scoring in a spreadsheet or a CRM

A Google Sheet is often the easiest place to start. Website form entries can flow into a sheet automatically; see connecting WordPress forms to a CRM or Google Sheets. Add a column for each signal and a total using simple formulas such as IF and SUM, or score each row by hand when it arrives.

  • Standardise the answers. Buttons and ranges in your form (such as "within a month") are much easier to score than free text; see multi-step forms for lead qualification
  • Record the source. Hidden form fields can capture the page or campaign an enquiry came from
  • Log calls and WhatsApp too. Add them to the same sheet and score them when you note the requirement
  • Move to a CRM when the sheet gets crowded. Many CRMs offer custom fields, tags or built-in scoring; check current plans, and get a manual system working before you automate it

Prioritising follow-up

A score is only useful if it changes what you do next. Agree a simple rule for each band:

  • Hot: call as soon as you can during working hours, ideally by the owner or senior person, with a personalised reply
  • Warm: reply the same day, answer their questions, suggest a clear next step and follow up after a couple of days
  • Nurture: send a helpful reply with useful information, and check in later; add them to a newsletter only with their consent

Every enquiry still gets a reply. Scoring should take seconds and never become a reason to delay; see how to respond to website enquiries fast.

Don't ignore small leads

A low score doesn't mean a worthless lead. Small enquiries matter for several reasons:

  • A small first job can grow into repeat work or a much bigger project
  • Happy small customers refer friends, family and colleagues, and leave reviews
  • A vague enquiry sometimes comes from a busy decision-maker; a short call can reveal a serious requirement
  • A low score may mean your form didn't ask the right question, not that the buyer isn't serious

Offer low-scoring leads a lighter path, such as a standard package, an FAQ page or a self-service booking link, and always reply politely. Keep notes factual and store only what you need to follow up.

Review and adjust every month

Once a month, compare scores with what actually happened. Which hot leads became customers? Did any low scores turn into good jobs? If a signal doesn't predict anything, drop it or lower its points; if a pattern keeps appearing among your best clients, add it. Comparing scores by source also shows which pages and campaigns bring the most promising enquiries, which helps when you measure your website's ROI.
