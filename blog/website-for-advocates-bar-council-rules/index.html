Website for Advocates in India: What the Bar Council Rules Allow, What a Compliant Site Contains, the Entry Disclaimer and Practice Areas Described Factually

How advocates in India can have a website within Bar Council rules: permitted information, the entry disclaimer, factual practice areas and what to avoid.

Key takeaways
  • The Bar Council of India rules forbid advocates from soliciting work or advertising, but permit a website that gives factual information; check the current rules before launch.
  • A compliant site sticks to facts: name, contact details, enrolment, qualifications and areas of practice, with an entry disclaimer and no testimonials, rankings or success claims.
  • Describe practice areas in neutral, informative language, and treat Google reviews, paid ads and SEO phrases like "best lawyer" with the same caution as the website itself.

Yes, an advocate in India can have a website, but it must inform rather than advertise. Under the Bar Council of India rules, as I understand them at the time of writing, advocates may not solicit work or advertise, though a website may set out a limited set of facts about you: who you are, how to reach you, when you enrolled, what you studied and which areas of law you practise in. A well-built site stays within those limits and is still genuinely useful to someone looking for an advocate.

Technically, the build matches the law firm and consultant websites I build: a profile with qualifications, practice area pages, a contact form, office location and an articles section. For advocates, though, the content rules shape every page, and several features that work for consultants, such as testimonials and client logos, are left out entirely. This guide explains what I understand the rules to allow and how that turns into pages. It is not legal advice, and the rules and their interpretation can change, so check the current position with your State Bar Council or a senior colleague before launch.

The rule, in plain terms

The relevant provision sits in the Bar Council of India Rules on standards of professional conduct, commonly referred to as Rule 36. In its original form it prohibited advocates from soliciting work or advertising directly or indirectly, including through circulars, advertisements, touts, personal communications and interviews, and from producing or encouraging newspaper comments or photographs about their cases. It also restricted sign boards and name plates to a reasonable size.

A later amendment added a proviso allowing advocates to furnish certain information on their websites: name, address, telephone numbers and email, enrolment number and date, professional and academic qualifications, and areas of practice. Many advocates also add a declaration that the information is accurate and provided by them. Everything else on the site should be judged against the general ban on solicitation and advertising.

Two practical points follow. First, the site should read like a professional record, not a sales page. Second, the same discipline applies wherever the information appears, including social media and directory listings.

What a compliant site contains, page by page

The entry disclaimer, and how to implement it

Most advocates' and law firms' websites in India show a disclaimer before the visitor enters, with an "I agree" button. The wording usually states that the Bar Council of India does not permit advertisement or solicitation, that the visitor is seeking information on their own initiative, that nothing on the site is legal advice or creates an advocate and client relationship, and that the firm is not liable for action taken on the basis of the content. The wording should come from you or your firm; I can format and place it.

How it is built matters for usability and search:

  • Show it as an overlay on first visit, remembered for that browser, so returning visitors are not asked every time
  • Keep the actual page content in the HTML underneath, so search engines can still read and index it
  • Make it readable on a phone, with a clear button and no tiny print
  • Repeat a short version of the disclaimer in the footer of every page
  • If the overlay uses a cookie or local storage to remember consent, mention it in the privacy policy

Practice areas: describe the work, do not sell it

Practice area pages are where most compliant sites go wrong, because the habit of marketing copy creeps in. The test is simple: would the sentence still make sense in a law school handout? Compare:

Avoid: "Facing a bounced cheque case? Our expert team wins cheque bounce matters quickly. Call now for a free consultation."

Write instead: "Cheque dishonour matters under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act: the notice period, the complaint process before the magistrate, and the options available to the drawer and the payee. The practice handles matters for both complainants and accused."

Each page can explain the area of law, the kinds of matters the practice handles, the forums involved, and general stages of the process. It should not describe outcomes, fees as an incentive, or anything that urges the reader to act. Linking to statutes and official sources adds value without adding promotion.

Things that quietly cross the line

The website is rarely the only channel, and several common marketing habits sit poorly with the rules. Check each with your Bar Council or senior colleagues before using it:

  • Paid search and social ads that target people looking for a lawyer
  • Search phrases such as "best divorce lawyer in Pune" placed in titles and headings to rank, since the wording itself is promotional
  • Google reviews, especially asking clients for them or displaying ratings on the site
  • Paid directory listings that rank lawyers or present them as recommended
  • WhatsApp broadcasts and bulk messages to people who have not asked for information
  • Posts about specific cases or client wins on social media

A Google Business Profile with the office address, hours and phone number can help clients find you, but whether and how to use reviews on it is a decision to take with advice, not by default.

Articles that inform

An articles section is one of the most useful parts of an advocate's site, and one of the safest when written carefully. General explanations, such as how to register a will, what a legal notice is, or what changed in a recent amendment, help readers and show your understanding of an area of law. Keep each article factual and dated, cite the statute or judgment, avoid giving advice on individual situations, and add a short line saying it is general information and not legal advice.

Plain writing, short sections and clear headings make articles easier to read on a phone, which is where most readers will find them.

Enquiries without collecting case papers

A contact form should collect only a name, phone number, email and a short outline of the matter. Ask visitors not to send documents or confidential details through the form, because nothing is agreed until you accept the brief, and conflict checks come first. Explain in the privacy policy how enquiry details are stored and who sees them; privacy policy and cookie basics for Indian websites covers what to include, and a plain guide to the DPDP Act explains consent.

Firm details, accessibility and the rest

Show the firm's name, office addresses, and each advocate's enrolment details consistently on every page where they appear. An about page written in a measured, factual voice also helps; writing an about page that builds trust has pointers, though leave out its testimonial suggestions. Use real photographs of the advocates and the office rather than stock images of gavels and scales, which add nothing. Make text large enough and contrast strong enough for older visitors; website accessibility basics lists the essentials. A modest, clean design suits the professional tone the rules require, and it loads quickly on any phone.

Chartered accountants and other professionals have different rules from advocates, so if your practice works alongside them, see websites for lawyers and chartered accountants for how a combined or referral relationship can be presented.

FAQs

Are advocates in India allowed to have a website?

Yes, within limits. The Bar Council of India rules, as amended, allow a site that lists your identity, contact details, enrolment, degrees and practice areas, while still prohibiting solicitation and advertising.

Can an advocate's website show client testimonials?

Testimonials and ratings are generally treated as promotional, so most compliant sites leave them out. Check the current position with your Bar Council before adding any.

Is the disclaimer pop-up compulsory?

It is widely used rather than a specific requirement I can point to, and it makes clear the visitor is seeking information on their own initiative. Take advice on wording.

Can I publish articles on legal topics?

Yes, as general information. Keep them factual and dated, cite your sources, avoid advice on individual cases and do not use them to promote your services.

Can I use SEO to bring visitors to my website?

Basic technical SEO, accurate titles and useful articles are generally fine. Avoid promotional phrases such as "best" or "top" and take advice before running paid ads.

If your current website reads like an advertisement, it can be rewritten into a factual, well-organised record of your practice that is easier to defend and easier to read.
