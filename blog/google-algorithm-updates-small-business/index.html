Google Algorithm Updates for Small Businesses: Core vs Spam Updates, How to Tell If One Affected You, What to Do and Where to Follow Announcements

Google algorithm updates for small businesses: core vs spam updates, how to tell if one hit your site, what to do and avoid, and honest recovery timelines.

Key takeaways
  • Core updates reassess how helpful and relevant pages are across the web, while spam updates target sites breaking Google's spam policies, so each needs a different response.
  • Before blaming an update, match your Search Console dates to the Search Status Dashboard, wait for the rollout to finish and rule out tracking, site changes and seasonality.

Google changes its ranking systems all the time, mostly in small, unannounced ways, and a few times a year it announces bigger changes: core updates, which reassess how helpful and relevant pages are across the whole web, and spam updates, which target sites that break its spam policies. If your traffic moves suddenly, check whether the timing matches a confirmed update on Google's Search Status Dashboard, wait for the rollout to finish, rule out problems on your own site, and only then improve the pages that lost ground. There is no quick technical fix for a core update, and nobody can honestly promise a recovery date.

Telling an update apart from a problem on your own site, such as pages dropped from the index, broken redirects or slow templates, is part of the audit in my WordPress SEO services. Many drops blamed on "the algorithm" turn out to be a setting changed during a redesign or a tracking tag that stopped firing.

If your traffic has already fallen and you don't know why, start with the step-by-step traffic drop checklist, which covers tracking, Search Console, redesigns and hacks. This guide focuses on updates themselves: the types, how to read them, how to respond and what recovery realistically looks like.

What a Google algorithm update is

Google doesn't rank pages with one algorithm. It uses many ranking systems that look at relevance, quality, usefulness, page experience, spam signals and more, and it changes them thousands of times a year. Almost all of those changes are small and never announced. The ones Google does announce are broad enough that many sites may notice, and those are what people usually mean by "a Google update".

An update doesn't single out your site. It changes how Google assesses pages in general, so some pages rise and others fall. A drop often means other pages are now judged more useful for a search, not that something on your page is broken.

Two things are often mistaken for updates. A manual action is a penalty applied by a human reviewer for a policy breach, and it appears in Search Console's Manual actions report with a message; algorithmic updates never send you a message. And changes to the results page, such as an AI Overview, more ads or a map pack appearing above you, can cut clicks even when your ranking hasn't moved.

Where to follow official announcements

  • Google Search Status Dashboard: the official list of ranking updates with their start and end dates, plus incidents affecting crawling, indexing and serving. This is the first place to check.
  • Google Search Central blog and documentation: explanations of bigger changes, and Google's guidance on core updates, including its self-assessment questions about helpful, reliable content.
  • Google Search Central's social accounts and YouTube channel: shorter announcements and explanations.
  • Search Console: messages about manual actions, security issues and indexing problems for your own site, though not about algorithm updates.

Third-party "volatility" trackers and forums are useful for context, but they report turbulence, not causes. Many unconfirmed "updates" are ordinary fluctuation. Treat an update as confirmed only when Google lists it.

How to tell whether an update affected your site

  1. Find the exact dates. In Search Console's Performance report, set the date range to the last few months and look at clicks and impressions by day. Note when the change started and whether it was sudden or gradual.
  2. Line them up with the Dashboard. Did the change begin during a confirmed rollout window? Rankings often move up and down during a rollout before settling.
  3. Rule out your own changes. A redesign, new plugin, changed URLs, a noindex setting or a broken tracking tag can coincide with an update. Seasonality matters too: a wedding planner's searches dip after the season, and a sweet shop's spike before Diwali.
  4. Find where it happened. Use the Pages and Queries tabs and the compare option. Is the drop site-wide, limited to one section such as the blog, or limited to certain kinds of searches?
  5. Look at the results yourself. Search your main queries. Who replaced you? Forums, bigger brands, government sites, an AI Overview? That tells you what Google now prefers for that search.

What to do after a core update

  • Wait for the rollout to finish, then give it another week or two before drawing conclusions.
  • List the pages that lost the most clicks, and compare each with the pages now ranking above it. What do they answer that yours doesn't?
  • Ask Google's self-assessment questions honestly: does the page offer original information or experience, would you trust it, is it made for people rather than to rank? E-E-A-T explained for small business websites turns these into practical steps.
  • Improve the pages that matter, adding real examples, local detail, photos of your own work and clearer answers. Merge thin or overlapping posts into one stronger page with redirects; how to update old blog posts covers refresh, merge and measure.
  • Strengthen trust signals: a real About page, who wrote or reviewed the content, full contact details and genuine reviews.
  • Fix technical health that holds good pages back: thin tag and archive pages in the index, slow mobile pages and broken internal links.

What to do after a spam update or manual action

If your site has used tactics Google treats as spam, such as bought link packages, private blog networks, hundreds of copy-paste location pages or mass-produced AI articles, a spam update may have caught up with them. Remove or improve those pages and stop the tactic. Google has said that when link spam is detected, the credit from those links is simply lost, so removing them won't bring rankings back to where the links had pushed them. SEO red flags and scams lists the offers to walk away from.

A manual action is different: Search Console tells you the reason and which pages are affected. Fix the problem across the whole site, document what you did, and submit a reconsideration request from the same report. If the cause was a hack, such as injected spam pages, clean it first; recovering rankings after a hack covers that path.

What not to do

  • Don't delete large numbers of pages or rewrite the whole site while the update is still rolling out.
  • Don't change every title and URL at once; you won't know what helped, and URL changes add risk.
  • Don't buy "recovery" packages, links or reviews. They make spam problems worse.
  • Don't disavow links without a manual action for unnatural links or clear evidence of a link scheme.
  • Don't judge one keyword on one day. Look at groups of pages over weeks.

Recovery timelines, without promises

After a core update, improvements can take months to show, because Google needs to recrawl your pages and reassess the site over time. Some sites see movement before the next core update; for others, change only appears when a later update rolls out. Some never return to their old positions, because the pages that replaced them really are more useful. After a spam update, Google has said it can take months for its systems to recognise that a site has cleaned up. After a manual action, things can change once a reconsideration request is accepted, but rankings still depend on the quality of what remains.

If anyone guarantees you'll recover by a date, treat that as a warning sign. How long SEO takes explains why search progress is never linear.

Protect your business from the next update

  • Keep a change log: a simple sheet of dates when you redesigned, changed plugins, published batches of content or changed hosting. It makes ruling out your own changes much faster.
  • Diversify where enquiries come from: Google Business Profile, email, WhatsApp, referrals and, where it makes sense, ads, so one update can't empty your pipeline.
  • Improve steadily: a few genuinely better pages each month beats a panicked rewrite after every update.
  • Avoid shortcuts that work until the next spam update; ethical link building shows what works instead.

FAQs

How often does Google update its algorithm?

Constantly. Google makes thousands of changes to Search each year, most of them small and unannounced. Larger core updates are announced a few times a year, along with occasional spam updates.

How do I know if a Google update hit my site?

Check whether your drop in Search Console started during an update listed on the Search Status Dashboard, rule out site changes, tracking problems and seasonality, then see which pages and queries changed. If the timing matches and nothing else explains it, an update is the likely cause.

How long does a core update take to roll out?

Often about two weeks, sometimes longer. The Dashboard shows when each one starts and finishes. Rankings can swing during the rollout, so judge the result after it completes.

Can I recover from a core update?

Often, but not always and not quickly. Recovery comes from making the affected pages genuinely more useful than what now ranks, and it may only show after a later update. There is no technical switch that undoes it.

Will Google tell me if an update affected my site?

No. Algorithm updates never trigger a message. Search Console only sends messages for manual actions, security issues and some indexing problems.

Do updates affect Google Maps rankings too?

Local results have their own ranking factors, mainly relevance, distance and prominence, and they can shift with updates as well. Keep your Business Profile complete and active alongside the website.
