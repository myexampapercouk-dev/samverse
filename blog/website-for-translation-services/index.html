Websites for Translation and Interpretation Agencies

What translation and interpretation agencies need on their website: language pairs, document types, certified work wording, turnaround and secure quote uploads.

Sameer Gupta 1 October 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways
  • Organise pages by language pair, service and document type, the way clients search, without mass-produced doorway pages.
  • Describe exactly what your certification includes and never guarantee acceptance, as the receiving authority decides.
  • Build a quote form around secure document uploads, and make sure file handling matches your confidentiality promises.

People usually look for a translator at a stressful moment: a visa file due next week, a university asking for English transcripts, a court date, or a conference that needs an interpreter by Monday. They search for things like "certified translation in Pune" or "German to English translator", open a few websites and contact the one that answers their questions fastest. Here's what a translation and interpretation agency's website needs to win those enquiries.

What clients check before they send a document

Whether it's an individual with one certificate or a company with a long technical manual, most clients want answers to the same questions:

  • Languages: do you handle my exact language pair, in the direction I need?
  • Document type: have you translated this kind of document before?
  • Certification: what comes with the translation, and will it suit my purpose?
  • Turnaround: can you deliver by my deadline?
  • Price: how is it charged, and how quickly can I get a quote?
  • Confidentiality: who will see my passport, medical report or contract?

Your homepage should answer each of these briefly and link to the page that answers it fully, with a clear "Get a quote" button on every page.

Language, service and document pages

Clients search by language and by document, so organise the site the same way. Three types of page usually work well:

Page typeExamplesWhat to include
Language pairsGerman to English, English to Arabic, Japanese, Tamil, MarathiDirections you handle, the documents you most often translate in that language, who does the work
ServicesLegal, technical, medical, website localisation, subtitling, transcription, interpretationScope, typical clients, file formats you accept, your review process
Document typesBirth and marriage certificates, degree certificates and transcripts, court papers, contracts, product manualsWhat the document is commonly needed for, what the client receives, how certification works for it

Only list languages you can deliver reliably through in-house or vetted translators. Interpretation deserves its own section: consecutive, simultaneous, conference, business meeting, phone and video interpreting, with the cities you cover in person.

Avoid creating hundreds of near-identical "X to Y translation" pages with only the language names swapped. They don't help visitors, and Google may treat them as doorway pages; see local landing pages done right.

Use the word "certified" with care

This is where translation websites most often overpromise. Terms like certified, sworn, notarised, attested and apostilled mean different things, and what's accepted depends on the country, embassy, university or court receiving the document. Requirements also change, so be precise:

  • Describe what your certification actually is, for example a signed and stamped declaration on your letterhead stating that the translation is accurate, along with the translator's details
  • Don't guarantee acceptance. The receiving authority decides. Say something like "Please check the requirements of the authority you're submitting to; we're happy to help you understand them"
  • Avoid titles you don't hold, such as "sworn translator" or "government-approved", unless you have that status in the relevant country and can show it
  • Be clear about your role in notarisation, attestation or apostille. These are carried out by notaries and government authorities. If you help clients arrange them, say so, and explain the extra time involved

Avoid "100% accurate" guarantees too. Instead, explain your quality process: qualified translators, a second linguist who reviews the work, formatting that mirrors the original, and how you handle corrections.

Be clear about turnaround and pricing

Turnaround is often the deciding factor, so give honest guidance rather than one fixed promise. Explain what affects delivery time:

  • Word count or number of pages
  • Language pair, as less common languages can take longer
  • Source format: an editable Word file is quicker than a blurred phone photo
  • Specialist subject matter that needs an expert translator
  • Certification, notarisation or couriering hard copies

If you offer an urgent service, explain when it's available and how it's charged. You don't need a rate card, but do say how you charge: per word, per page, per document, a minimum charge, or per hour or day for interpreters, with the final quote following once you've seen the document. See should you show prices on your website for the trade-offs, and list payment options such as UPI and bank transfer.

A quote form built around uploads

The quote form is the most important part of the site. A good one asks for:

  1. Source and target languages
  2. Document type and purpose (visa, university, court, business)
  3. File uploads, allowing several files in common formats like PDF, JPG and DOCX
  4. Deadline
  5. Whether certification or hard copies are needed, with a "not sure" option
  6. Name, phone, email and preferred contact method

Interpretation requests need different questions: date, duration, city or online platform, type of interpreting and subject. A simple first choice ("Translate a document" or "Book an interpreter") can route visitors to the right questions; see multi-step forms. Many people photograph documents on their phone, so test uploads on mobile and send an automatic acknowledgement saying when they'll hear back. A WhatsApp button helps with quick questions, but encourage clients to send sensitive files through the form.

Show how you protect confidential documents

Your clients send passports, medical records, financial statements, court papers and unreleased business documents. A short, specific confidentiality page reassures them:

  • Who can access files, and whether translators sign confidentiality agreements
  • Whether you'll sign an NDA for business clients on request
  • How long you keep files, and how clients can ask for deletion
  • How you deliver finished work securely

The website must match what you promise: uploaded files shouldn't sit in a public folder or the media library, and old uploads should be removed regularly. Your privacy policy should explain this in plain words; see India's DPDP Act and your website. Mention certifications such as ISO 17100 or ISO 27001 only if you're actually certified, with the scope stated correctly.

Trust signals and local search

  • Translator profiles: languages, qualifications and subject experience. First names only are fine if translators prefer privacy
  • Redacted samples showing your formatting, used only with permission and with personal details removed
  • Sectors served: legal, medical, manufacturing, education or government work, described honestly
  • Reviews from real clients on your website and Google Business Profile
  • Local searches: target phrases like "translation services in {city}", "certified translation near me" and "{language} translator in {city}", and keep your Google Business Profile accurate with office hours and walk-in details
  • Speed: keep pages light for urgent searches on mobile data
