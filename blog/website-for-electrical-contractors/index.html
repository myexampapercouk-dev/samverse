Websites for Electrical Contractors: Domestic, Commercial and Industrial Work, Licence Class Stated Precisely, Project Portfolio, Tenders, Safety, AMC and Service Areas

What an electrical contractor's website needs: domestic, commercial or industrial focus, exact licence details, projects, tenders, safety, AMC and areas.

Key takeaways
  • Organise the site by the kind of buyer you serve, because a homeowner, a facilities manager and a plant engineer look for completely different proof.
  • Copy licence names, classes and numbers exactly from your certificates, since licensing differs by state and tender teams cross-check.
  • Projects with real scope details, a safety page that describes your actual system, and an RFQ form that accepts drawings do more selling than any slogan.

An electrical contractor's website should make three things clear within a minute: which kind of work you do (domestic, commercial, industrial, or a mix), what licence and class you hold, stated exactly as on the certificate, and what you have built, with real project details. Around that it needs a way to receive tender documents and RFQs, a page describing how you work safely, an AMC offer for after handover, and an honest list of the areas you cover.

For contractors chasing factory, warehouse and infrastructure work, the site works much like a B2B supplier's, with capability pages and quote forms; that is the approach on my websites for manufacturers and industrial businesses page. Contractors focused on homes and shops need something closer to a local service site, which I cover below as well.

Three kinds of work, three kinds of buyer

The same company can wire a flat on Monday and commission an LT panel at a plant on Thursday, but the people choosing a contractor for each job look for different things.

DomesticCommercialIndustrial
Who decidesHomeowner or builderFacilities manager, architect, interior firmPlant head, project consultant, EPC firm, purchase team
What they search"Electrician near me", "house wiring contractor in [city]""Office electrical contractor [city]""HT electrical contractor", "substation installation contractor"
Proof they wantReviews, prompt visits, clear chargesSimilar fit-outs, timelines met, documentationLicence class, project scale, safety system, team and equipment

Structuring the site when you do more than one

  • Lead the homepage with the segment that brings the most valuable work, and link clearly to the others
  • Give each segment its own section with its own services, projects and enquiry form
  • Keep domestic call-out details off industrial pages, and RFQ forms off domestic ones
  • Use separate phone numbers or form routing if different people handle each type of enquiry

For industrial work, one page per major service helps both buyers and search: HT and LT installations, substations and transformers, panels and switchgear, cabling and trays, earthing and lightning protection, lighting, testing and commissioning. List only what your own team delivers.

Licences and credentials, stated precisely

This is the part tender teams and consultants check most carefully, and where vague wording does most damage. Electrical contractor licensing is handled at state level, and the names, classes, grades and voltage limits differ from one state to another. Use the exact wording on each certificate.

CredentialState exactlyAvoid
Contractor licenceIssuing authority, licence class or grade as written, number, validity date"Government approved contractor" with no detail
Supervisors and wiremenCertificates of competency or permits held by named staff, where they are willing to be namedImplying every worker holds a certificate
Government enlistmentDepartment and class, such as CPWD or state PWD, only if you are enlistedListing departments you have only worked for as a subcontractor, without saying so
ISO or other certificationStandard, scope and certifying body, only while currentExpired certificates or a logo without a scope
Manufacturer authorisationBrand and the specific authorisation, only if heldBrand logos used as if you are a partner

Put a renewal reminder in your calendar and update the page the week a licence is renewed. A lapsed licence on a public page can disqualify you faster than having no page at all. If you are unsure which class applies to the work you want to bid for, check with your state's licensing authority rather than describing it loosely.

A project portfolio buyers can actually use

Buyers want to see work like theirs, at a similar scale. For each project worth showing, record:

  • Client type and location: name the client only with permission; otherwise "pharmaceutical plant, Chakan" is enough
  • Scope in numbers: transformer rating, number of panels, cable lengths, connected load
  • Constraints: shutdown windows, working inside a running plant, tight handover dates
  • Your role: main contractor or subcontractor, and to whom
  • Photos: real panels, trays and substations from your sites, not stock images

Let visitors filter by segment and service. A handful of detailed write-ups beats a gallery of fifty unlabelled photos. Tenders and RFQs

Large jobs arrive as tender documents or RFQs, often with drawings. A generic contact form wastes them. Build a dedicated enquiry route:

  1. Project type, site location and expected start
  2. Scope summary and approximate connected load
  3. Uploads for the BOQ, drawings or single line diagram, with file size limits stated
  4. Tender reference, if any
  5. Name, company, role, phone and email

Route it straight to estimation, send an automatic acknowledgement, and add spam protection, since upload forms attract junk. RFQ forms for B2B websites covers file handling in more detail. Alongside the form, offer a downloadable company profile with licences, key projects, team and equipment, because consultants often need one for pre-qualification. Keep sensitive documents such as bank details or full financials for direct requests, not public downloads.

Safety: describe the system, not a slogan

Many companies ask about safety during vendor registration, and their EHS teams read this page closely. Describe what you actually do:

  • Permit-to-work and isolation procedures, including lockout and tagout
  • PPE issued for each type of work, and how its use is checked
  • Toolbox talks and training, and who runs them
  • Calibrated testing instruments, with how calibration is tracked
  • How incidents and near misses are reported

If you prepare method statements, risk assessments or job safety analyses for clients, say so and offer a sample on request. Consultants reading the page are often deciding whether your documentation will pass their own review, and a short, specific description reassures them more than a list of values.

Photos of your team in proper PPE on real sites support the words. Do not claim an accident-free record unless you track it properly and can show the records.

AMC and maintenance contracts

AMCs keep you close to clients between projects. Give them their own page with a scope table:

ItemWhat to state
Equipment coveredSubstations, transformers, panels, changeover, earthing pits, lighting
Visit frequencyHow often, and the checks done on each visit
Breakdown supportOnly response times you can genuinely keep
ReportsWhat the client receives after each visit
ExclusionsSpares, civil work, third-party equipment

Add a short AMC enquiry form asking for the equipment list, connected load and location. Businesses with lifts or fire systems have similar maintenance-led sites; see websites for lift and elevator companies and websites for fire safety companies.

Service areas, honestly stated

Domestic work usually covers a radius around your office; industrial work may cover several states. Say both plainly. Mention industrial areas you regularly work in, such as MIDC estates or industrial parks, on the relevant project and service pages rather than creating a near-identical page for each one. Before launch

  • Segment focus clear on the homepage
  • Licence details copied exactly, with validity dates
  • At least three projects with scope in numbers and permission recorded
  • RFQ form tested with a large drawing file
  • Company profile download up to date
  • Safety page reviewed by whoever runs safety
  • Pages quick to load on a phone over mobile data, for engineers checking on site

FAQs

Should an electrical contractor show their licence on the website?

Yes, with the issuing authority, class or grade, number and validity, copied exactly from the certificate. Update it when it is renewed.

Can one website serve domestic and industrial customers?

Yes, if each has its own section, projects and enquiry route. Mixing them on one page confuses both audiences.

Should I publish tender documents on my site?

Publish a company profile and licence details; keep financial and banking documents for direct requests.

Can I show work I did as a subcontractor?

Yes, if you describe your role accurately and have permission where the client is named. Do not imply you were the main contractor.

What should the homepage show first?

The kind of work you do and for whom, your licence class in one line, two or three strong projects, and the main action for your best buyers, whether that is a call or an RFQ. Everything else can sit one click away.

Do I need a separate page for every city I work in?

Only where you have real projects and specific detail to share. Otherwise list your service areas on one page.
