Request-for-Quote Forms for B2B Websites: What to Ask and How to Follow Up

How to design request-for-quote forms for B2B websites: what to ask, file uploads, product-specific forms, routing to sales, fast follow-up and spam protection.

Key takeaways
  • Ask for product, quantity, unit, delivery location and contact details, and leave out anything you will not use to quote.
  • Allow drawing and specification uploads, pre-fill the product on product pages and route each RFQ to the right salesperson.
  • Confirm receipt instantly, reply personally fast and use invisible spam protection that does not block genuine overseas buyers.

For a manufacturer, distributor or engineering firm, the request-for-quote (RFQ) form is often the most valuable part of the website. A buyer who fills it in usually has a real requirement. Yet many B2B sites use a generic contact form that asks too little to quote from, or a long one that puts buyers off. Here's how to design an RFQ form that gives your sales team what it needs and gets a reply back to the buyer quickly. For the wider picture, see how manufacturers get more B2B enquiries from their website.

An RFQ form is not a contact form

A contact form says "talk to us". An RFQ form says "price this for me". The buyer is further along, and often comparing several suppliers. Your questions should collect enough to prepare a meaningful quote, or at least a focused first call, without days of back-and-forth emails. At the same time, the buyer may be filling in forms on several supplier websites, so every field has to earn its place.

What to ask, and what to leave out

Name, company, email, mobile or WhatsAppGST number (if needed for the quote)Full postal address
Product or service requiredMaterial, grade or specification"How did you hear about us?" as a required field
Quantity and unitTarget price or budget rangeCompany turnover or employee count
Delivery city or countryRequired delivery dateAccount sign-up before quoting
Requirement detailsOne-off or repeat orderFields that a file upload already covers
  • Use a dropdown for units (pieces, kg, tonnes, metres) so quantities are never ambiguous
  • Mark optional fields clearly, and keep them genuinely optional
  • For export enquiries, ask for the country and preferred shipping terms (such as FOB or CIF) only if your team will use the answer
  • Put contact details at the end, after the requirement questions, so the buyer has already invested a little effort

File uploads for drawings and specifications

Engineering and custom-manufacturing buyers often can't describe a part in words. Let them send what they already have.

  • Accept the formats your team actually opens: PDF, images, spreadsheets, and CAD files such as DWG, DXF or STEP if you quote from drawings
  • State the size limit and allow several files; offer an email address or file-sharing link as an alternative for large assemblies
  • Restrict uploads to the file types you need, and make sure uploaded files can't be browsed or run from the web
  • Drawings are often confidential, so say who will see them, and mention NDAs if signing one is your normal practice
  • Prefer a secure link to the files in the notification email over large attachments, which can bounce

Product-specific forms

A buyer looking at a particular product shouldn't have to retype its name. On product and category pages:

  • Place a "Request a quote" button next to the specifications, opening a form with the product name and code already filled in
  • Show only the questions that matter for that product line: a valve may need size and pressure rating, a carton needs dimensions and print colours
  • For buyers sourcing several items, a "quote list" lets them add products while browsing and send one request; request-a-quote plugins for WooCommerce can do this, or it can be built to fit your catalogue
  • Capture the page URL and campaign source in hidden fields, so sales knows exactly what the buyer was looking at

Route every RFQ to the right person

An RFQ sitting unread in a shared inbox is a quote you may never get to send. Use the buyer's answers to route it:

  • By product line or division: each team gets its own enquiries
  • By region: domestic enquiries by state or zone, export enquiries to the export desk
  • By size: large quantities or existing key accounts flagged to a senior person

Send every RFQ to a CRM, or at least a shared Google Sheet, so nothing is lost when someone is on leave, and add an instant email or WhatsApp alert for the person responsible. See connecting WordPress forms to a CRM or Google Sheets.

Follow-up speed and what the buyer sees

Buyers who send the same requirement to several suppliers tend to remember the ones that replied quickly and clearly. Make the first minutes count:

  1. Show a thank-you page that confirms receipt and says when to expect a reply, such as "within one working day", but only if you can keep that promise
  2. Send an automatic email with a reference number and a copy of what they submitted
  3. Acknowledge personally soon after, even if the detailed quote takes longer, and ask any missing questions in one message
  4. Follow up on quotes that go quiet; buyers are often waiting on internal approvals

For a simple system that keeps replies fast, see how to respond to website enquiries fast.

Spam protection without blocking real buyers

RFQ forms attract bots and sales pitches. But heavy-handed protection can also block genuine buyers, especially from overseas.

  • Start with invisible measures: a honeypot field, a minimum time to submit and server-side validation
  • If spam continues, add a low-friction check such as Cloudflare Turnstile or reCAPTCHA rather than a puzzle
  • Avoid blocking whole countries unless you never sell there
  • Don't reject free email addresses; plenty of genuine smaller buyers use Gmail
  • Check your form plugin's spam folder regularly so a real RFQ isn't missed

More options are in how to stop contact form spam.

Measure and improve

Track each RFQ submission as a conversion along with the product and traffic source. Review monthly which pages produce RFQs, where people start the form but give up, and how many RFQs become orders. If many buyers abandon at the same question, simplify it or make it optional.
