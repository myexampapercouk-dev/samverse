Duplicate Content Explained: What It Is, the Penalty Myth and How to Fix It

What duplicate content is, whether Google really penalises it, the common WordPress causes and how to fix them with canonicals, redirects, noindex or merging.

Key takeaways
  • Duplicate content is the same or very similar content at more than one URL; Google says it is generally not penalised unless the intent is to deceive or manipulate results.
  • The real costs are split ranking signals, Google showing the wrong URL and wasted crawling, mostly caused by archives, URL parameters, site versions and staging copies.
  • Fix it with 301 redirects, canonical tags, noindex for low-value pages and by merging near-identical pages, and only link to and list the preferred URLs.

"Duplicate content" worries many business owners, usually because they've heard there's a Google penalty for it. The truth is more nuanced: normal technical duplication rarely causes penalties, but some kinds of duplication do hold sites back, and deliberate copying can get a site into real trouble. This guide explains what duplicate content is, what Google actually says about the "penalty", the common causes on WordPress sites and how to fix each one.

What is duplicate content?

Duplicate content is the same, or very similar, content available at more than one URL. It comes in a few forms:

  • Exact duplicates on your own site: the same page loading at two addresses, such as with and without www
  • Near-duplicates on your own site: pages that are mostly the same, such as service pages where only the city name changes
  • Duplicates across sites: your text copied by another site, or text you've used from elsewhere, such as manufacturer product descriptions

Most duplicate content isn't created on purpose. It's a side effect of how websites, and WordPress in particular, generate URLs.

The duplicate content penalty: myth vs reality

Google's stated position has been consistent for years: duplicate content on its own isn't grounds for action against a site, unless the duplication appears intended to deceive users or manipulate search results. Its documentation explains that most duplication is accidental and handled automatically.

What Google does instead is group duplicate URLs together, choose one as the main (canonical) version and show that one in results. The others aren't punished; they just aren't shown.

MythReality
Any duplicate text gets your site penalisedNormal duplication, such as a shared disclaimer or a quoted paragraph, isn't penalised; Google picks one version to show
There's a safe percentage of duplicate contentThere's no official percentage; what matters is whether each page offers something useful of its own
Duplicate content never mattersIt can split ranking signals and lead Google to show the wrong URL
Copying content is fine if you credit the sourceCopied or lightly reworded content adds little value and rarely ranks; at scale it can be treated as spam

Where it does become a real problem is deliberate duplication: scraping or spinning other sites' content, or mass-producing near-identical pages to target lots of searches. Those can fall under Google's spam policies and, in serious cases, lead to a manual action reported in Search Console.

How Google chooses which version to show

When Google finds several URLs with the same content, it looks at a number of signals to decide which one to treat as the canonical:

  • Redirects: a permanent redirect is a strong sign that the destination is the preferred URL
  • Canonical tags: a strong hint, but a hint rather than an order; Google can choose differently if other signals disagree
  • Internal links: the version your own menus and articles link to
  • Sitemaps: the URLs you list in your XML sitemap
  • HTTPS: Google generally prefers the secure version when both exist

The practical lesson is to make every signal agree. If your canonical says one URL but your menu links to another and your sitemap lists a third, don't be surprised when Google picks one you didn't want.

The real problems duplicate content causes

  • Split ranking signals: links and relevance spread across several URLs for the same content
  • Google choosing the wrong version to show in results, such as a URL with tracking parameters or an old address
  • Wasted crawling on large sites with many near-identical pages, so new or updated pages are picked up more slowly
  • Thin, scaled pages: many near-identical pages created to target keywords (like city-swapped pages) can be treated as spam. See avoiding doorway pages.
  • Copied content from other sites adds little value and rarely ranks

Common causes of duplicate content on WordPress sites

Tags, categories and other archives

WordPress creates archive pages for every category, tag, author and date. Each lists the same post excerpts, so a site with lots of thinly used tags can have dozens of pages repeating the same snippets. On a single-author site, the author archive is often a copy of the main blog page. See when to index or noindex category and tag pages.

URL parameters

Anything after a question mark in a URL can create a new address for the same page: tracking tags such as ?utm_source=, sorting and filtering on stores, comment reply links on older setups, and session or currency parameters. Google usually works these out, but on stores with many filters they can still waste crawling. See product filters and SEO.

HTTP vs HTTPS, www vs non-www and trailing slashes

If your site loads at both http and https, and with and without www, every page technically exists in four versions. The same applies when /page and /page/ both load. A site-wide redirect to one version fixes this; see moving WordPress to HTTPS properly if the switch was never finished.

Print pages, attachment pages and paginated comments

Print-friendly versions created by plugins, attachment pages for every uploaded image (newer WordPress installs disable these; older sites may still have them) and comments split across several pages can all produce extra URLs with little unique content.

Staging and development copies

A staging site on a subdomain or temporary address that Google can reach is a complete copy of your site. It should be password-protected or blocked from indexing; see staging sites explained.

Scraped and syndicated content

Other sites sometimes copy your articles automatically. Google is usually good at identifying the original, especially for an established site. If you republish your own articles elsewhere on purpose, ask the other site to link back to the original and, ideally, add a canonical tag pointing to it.

Product and location pages

Stores that use the manufacturer's description, like every other retailer, compete with many identical pages. Service businesses that copy one page and swap the city name create near-duplicates that rarely rank well.

How to find duplicate content

  • Search Console > Pages: look for "Duplicate without user-selected canonical", "Duplicate, Google chose different canonical than user" and "Alternate page with proper canonical tag" (the last is usually fine). See page indexing statuses explained.
  • URL Inspection: compare the canonical you declared with the one Google selected.
  • A website crawler: lists duplicate titles, descriptions and pages with very similar content.
  • A quoted search: put a distinctive sentence from your page in quotation marks in Google to see where else it appears.

How to fix duplicate content

SituationBest fix
Same page at http/https, www/non-www or with and without a trailing slash301 redirect to one preferred version
Two pages on the same topic competing with each otherMerge into one stronger page and 301 redirect the other
Duplicates you need to keep, such as tracking or sort parametersCanonical tag pointing to the main URL
Low-value archives or internal search resultsNoindex, or improve them so they're worth indexing
Staging copy indexedPassword-protect it and remove it from Google
Manufacturer or copied descriptionsRewrite with your own details, starting with best sellers

A few more habits help Google pick the right version:

  • Use canonical tags for necessary duplicates; see canonical tags explained
  • Link internally only to the preferred URLs, and list only those in your XML sitemap
  • Write unique product and service descriptions, starting with the pages that matter most
  • Control filter URLs on stores; see WooCommerce SEO
  • Handle URL parameters on the site itself; Google has retired the old URL parameters tool in Search Console

When merging pages, use 301 redirects so links and visitors carry over.

What not to do

  • Don't block duplicates in robots.txt: Google then can't crawl them to see the canonical or noindex tag
  • Don't mix signals: a noindex tag plus a canonical pointing elsewhere, or a canonical pointing to a redirected URL, sends confusing instructions
  • Don't canonicalise every paginated page to page 1: page 2 and beyond list different items and should point to themselves
  • Don't delete pages in bulk without checking which ones get traffic or links; redirect or improve them instead

Copying the same questions onto every page is a quiet form of duplication. The guide to FAQ SEO explains how to keep answers unique and useful.

The principle

Each page should exist for a reason and offer something unique. If two pages would answer the same question, combine them into one better page.

FAQs

Does Google penalise duplicate content?

Generally no. Google says duplicate content isn't grounds for action unless it's meant to deceive or manipulate results; it simply picks one version to show. Deliberate scraping and mass-produced near-identical pages are the exception.

How much duplicate content is acceptable?

There's no official percentage. Shared disclaimers, quotes and similar product specifications are normal. The question to ask is whether each page offers something useful that the others don't.

Is using manufacturer product descriptions a problem?

It isn't penalised, but those pages compete with every other shop using the same text, so they rarely stand out. Adding your own details, photos, sizing advice and answers to common questions gives Google a reason to show your page.

Someone copied my content. What should I do?

Often nothing: Google usually recognises the original. If the copy outranks you or harms your business, contact the site owner or their host, and look at Google's copyright removal request process; take legal advice if needed.

Should I use a canonical tag or a 301 redirect?

Use a 301 redirect when visitors don't need the duplicate URL at all. Use a canonical when the duplicate must stay accessible, such as a sorted or tracked version of a page.

Do WordPress tag pages cause duplicate content?

They can, if you have many tags with only one or two posts each. Either use fewer, meaningful tags or noindex the tag archives.
