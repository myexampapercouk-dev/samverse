Uptime Monitoring Explained: How to Know Your Website Is Down Before Your Customers Do

Key takeaways
  • An uptime monitor checks your site from outside every few minutes and alerts you by email, SMS or app when it stops responding properly.
  • A basic status check misses many failures, so add a keyword check on a key page, plus SSL certificate and domain expiry alerts.
  • Free tools such as UptimeRobot, Better Stack and Jetpack Monitor cover most small business sites; shorter intervals and SMS alerts suit stores and sites running ads.

Uptime monitoring is a service that checks your website from outside every few minutes and alerts you, by email, SMS or an app notification, when it stops responding properly. For a business site, set up at least a status check on the homepage, a keyword check on a key page such as contact or checkout, and alerts for SSL certificate and domain expiry. Free tools such as UptimeRobot, Better Stack and Jetpack Monitor are enough for most small sites.

Without monitoring, the usual alarm is a customer telling you, often after a night or a weekend of lost enquiries. With it, you know within minutes and can act before most people notice. Uptime monitoring is included in my WordPress maintenance plans, alongside backups and updates, but it takes minutes to set up yourself.

How uptime monitoring works

The monitoring service sends a request to your site at a set interval, just as a visitor's browser would, and looks at the answer. If the site doesn't respond, responds with an error code such as 500 or 503, or takes too long, the service usually re-checks from another location to rule out a network blip. If the failure is confirmed, it alerts you, and alerts you again when the site recovers. It also keeps a log of every outage and of response times, which is useful evidence if you need to raise a problem with your host.

Because the checks come from outside your hosting, they catch problems that the server itself can't report, such as an expired domain, a DNS mistake or the whole server being offline.

Types of checks, and what each one catches

HTTP(S) statusSite unreachable, server errors such as 500, 502 and 503, DNS failuresError pages that return a normal "200 OK" response
KeywordBlank pages, critical errors, database errors and hacked pages that still "load"Problems on pages you don't monitor

The keyword check matters more than people expect. When WordPress shows "There has been a critical error", the server can still return a normal response, so a basic status check reports the site as up. A keyword check that looks for text from your page notices that it's missing. If you do get that error, fixing the critical error explains what to do.

Choosing a check interval

  • Every 5 minutes is the usual free interval and is fine for most brochure and service sites.
  • Every 1 to 3 minutes suits online stores, booking sites and any site running paid ads, where each minute down costs money.
  • Confirmation from several locations before alerting reduces false alarms caused by one network's hiccup. Most services do this by default.

Shorter intervals don't prevent downtime; they shorten the time before you know about it. For a small business, the bigger win is making sure the alert reaches someone who can act on it.

How to set up monitoring for a business website

  1. Monitor the homepage with an HTTPS check, using the exact address visitors use, including or excluding www.
  2. Add a keyword check on your most important page: the contact page for a service business, a product or checkout page for a store.
  3. Turn on SSL certificate expiry alerts, so a failed auto-renewal is caught before browsers start showing warnings; see SSL certificate errors explained.
  4. Turn on domain expiry alerts, or at least put the renewal date in a shared calendar. An expired domain takes down the website and email together; see what to do when your domain expires.
  5. Set up alert contacts. At least two people, with email plus an app notification or SMS for the person who can act fastest. Avoid sending alerts only to a shared inbox nobody watches at night.
  6. Add maintenance windows for planned work, so you don't get alerts during updates you're doing yourself.
  7. Test it. Temporarily point a monitor at a page that doesn't exist and confirm the alert arrives on every channel.
  8. Choose a location close to your visitors if the tool lets you. A site for Indian customers is best checked from India or nearby, as well as from elsewhere.

Picking a good keyword

Choose text that only appears when the page has loaded properly, and that you won't change often:

  • Your business name or phone number in the footer, which loads last
  • "Add to cart" on a product page, or a label on the checkout form
  • The heading of your contact form

Some tools can also alert when a keyword appears, which is handy for phrases such as "critical error" or "Error establishing a database connection". Avoid text that changes daily, such as prices or dates, or you'll get false alarms.

What uptime percentages really mean

Hosts often advertise "99.9% uptime". Here's what different figures allow in a 30-day month:

UptimeDowntime allowed per month
99%About 7 hours 12 minutes
99.5%About 3 hours 36 minutes
99.9%About 43 minutes
99.95%About 22 minutes
99.99%About 4 minutes

A host's guarantee usually means a partial refund or credit if they miss it, and often excludes planned maintenance and problems caused by your own site. Your monitoring log is how you'd know whether the promise was kept.

If the alert shows a gateway error rather than a full outage, see how to fix a 502 bad gateway in WordPress and the related 504 timeout.

When an alert arrives

  1. Open the site on your phone using mobile data, to confirm it's really down.
  2. Read the error the monitor recorded: a timeout, a DNS failure, a 5xx error or a missing keyword each point somewhere different.
  3. Check the host's status page and your email for suspension or resource warnings.
  4. Think about what changed recently, such as updates or DNS changes.
  5. Contact your host or developer with the time it started and the error.

What uptime monitoring doesn't tell you

  • Whether forms deliver. A contact form can show its thank-you message while every email disappears. Send a test enquiry regularly; see why contact forms stop sending enquiries.
  • Whether checkout works. A store can be "up" while payments fail. Place test orders after updates.
  • Whether the site is hacked. Spam pages and redirects shown only to Google or mobile visitors leave the homepage looking normal; see signs your WordPress site has been hacked.
  • Whether backups are restorable. A heartbeat monitor can confirm a backup job ran, not that it can be restored.

That's why monitoring is one line of a wider routine; the WordPress maintenance checklist covers the rest.

Uptime monitoring for Indian hosting and WooCommerce stores

Many small business sites in India sit on shared hosting, sometimes on servers outside the country. That setup brings a few specific patterns worth watching for, and your monitoring should be configured with them in mind.

Check from more than one location

A site can be reachable from a monitor in Europe while visitors on a particular Indian network struggle, or the other way round. If your tool lets you choose check locations, include one in or near India and at least one elsewhere, and only alert when two locations agree. This cuts false alarms caused by a single network blip.

Watch the pages that earn money

For a WooCommerce or Shopify store, the home page loading is not enough. Add a keyword check on a product page and on the cart page, and if possible a check on the payment callback or order confirmation URL. Payment gateway plugins for Razorpay, PayU or similar can fail on their own after an update while the rest of the store looks fine.

Track SSL and domain expiry alongside uptime

A good share of "site down" calls are really an expired SSL certificate or a domain that was not renewed. Most monitors can warn you two to four weeks before either expires. Turn both on, and send the alert to an email address that someone in the business actually reads, not only to the developer.

Keep a simple incident note

Each time an alert fires, write down the time, how long it lasted, what caused it and what fixed it. After a few months this log tells you whether the problem is the host, a plugin, traffic spikes or something else, and it gives you facts to take to your hosting company instead of guesses.

FAQs

Is free uptime monitoring good enough?

For most small business sites, yes. A free plan checking every five minutes, with a keyword check and email or app alerts, covers the basics. Stores and sites running ads benefit from shorter intervals and SMS alerts on a paid plan.

How often should uptime be checked?

Every five minutes is fine for brochure and service sites. Every one to three minutes makes sense for online stores, booking sites and sites running paid campaigns.

Why did I get a down alert when my site was working?

Usually a short network blip, a brief server overload, a firewall blocking the monitor's IP address, or a keyword that changed on the page. If false alarms repeat, check the monitor's log and whether your security plugin or host is blocking it.

Doesn't my hosting company monitor my site?

Hosts monitor their servers, not whether your particular site, domain and SSL certificate are working. A plugin error or an expired domain won't show up on their dashboard, so independent monitoring is still worth having.

Will uptime monitoring slow my website down?

No. A single request every few minutes is far less than normal visitor traffic. If your security plugin blocks the monitor, add its IP addresses to the allow list rather than switching the monitor off.

Is free uptime monitoring good enough for a small business website?

For a brochure site, a free plan with a five-minute check, email alerts and SSL expiry warnings is usually enough. A store taking payments benefits from shorter intervals, keyword checks on cart and checkout pages, and alerts to a phone, which often need a paid plan.
