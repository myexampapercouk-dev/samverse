Website Accessibility Basics for Small Businesses: WCAG in Plain English, a 15-Minute Check and WordPress Fixes

Website accessibility basics in plain English: what WCAG asks for, contrast, alt text, keyboard use, headings, forms and captions, plus WordPress fixes.

Key takeaways
  • Accessibility means people can use your site whatever their eyesight, hearing, movement or device, and WCAG level AA is the usual target.
  • Most problems on small business sites are basic and fixable: low contrast, missing alt text, menus that fail with a keyboard, skipped headings and unlabelled form fields.
  • Automated checkers find only some issues, so add a keyboard test, a zoom test and a quick screen reader test, and build the fixes into your theme rather than patching pages.

Website accessibility means people can use your site whatever their eyesight, hearing, movement or the device they're on: text they can read, images described for screen readers, menus that work without a mouse, forms with clear labels and videos with captions. The international standard is WCAG, the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, and level AA is the usual target. For a small business, most of the work is a handful of basics done properly, and nearly all of them make the site easier for everyone.

The cheapest time to fix accessibility is when templates are being rebuilt anyway, so if you're planning a website redesign, put the checks in this guide into the brief. If you're starting from scratch, the same applies to a new site through WordPress website development.

This guide covers the basics for every visitor. If many of your customers are older, also read making your website easy for older visitors.

Who accessibility is for

It's easy to picture accessibility as something for a small group of blind users. In practice it covers far more people:

  • Vision: people who are blind and use screen readers, people with low vision who zoom in, and people with colour blindness.
  • Hearing: people who are deaf or hard of hearing and rely on captions and text alternatives to phone calls.
  • Movement: people with tremors, arthritis or injuries who use a keyboard, switch device or voice control instead of a mouse.
  • Thinking and attention: people with dyslexia, memory difficulties or anxiety, who benefit from plain language and predictable layouts.
  • Situations: anyone on a cracked phone screen, in bright sunlight, holding a child in one arm, or reading in their second language.

Older visitors often have several of these at once, which is why sites for clinics, temples, insurers and pharmacies feel the effect most.

WCAG in plain English

WCAG is published by the W3C, the body that sets web standards. At the time of writing, version 2.2 is the current one, with a future version still in development. It is built on four principles, often remembered as POUR:

PrincipleWhat it meansTypical examples
PerceivablePeople can see or hear the content in some formAlt text for images, captions for video, enough colour contrast
OperablePeople can use every control, however they browseKeyboard access, visible focus, large enough tap targets, no flashing content
UnderstandableContent and behaviour make senseClear labels, helpful error messages, consistent navigation, the page language set
RobustAssistive technology can read the codeProper HTML elements, buttons that are real buttons, named form fields

Each guideline has success criteria at three levels: A (the minimum), AA (the level most organisations and laws refer to) and AAA (the strictest, not expected across a whole site). Aim for AA.

The basics that matter most

1. Colour contrast

WCAG AA asks for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text, and 3:1 for buttons, form borders and icons people need to see. Pale grey text on white is the most common failure. A free contrast checker tells you the ratio of any two colours in seconds; choosing website colours and fonts covers picking a palette that passes.

2. Alt text for images

Every meaningful image needs a short description of what it shows or does, such as "Dental chair in the treatment room at the Baner clinic". Decorative images should have empty alt text so screen readers skip them, and an image used as a link should describe where it goes. The image SEO guide has more examples.

3. Keyboard access and visible focus

Everything you can click must work with the Tab, Enter, Space and arrow keys, in a sensible order, with a visible outline showing where you are. Dropdown menus, pop-ups and sliders are the usual failures. A "Skip to content" link at the top saves keyboard users tabbing through the whole menu on every page.

4. Headings in order

One H1 that says what the page is about, then H2s for sections and H3s within them, chosen for structure, not size. Screen reader users jump between headings the way sighted visitors scan.

5. Links and buttons that make sense

"Read the clinic timings" tells people where a link goes; "click here" doesn't. Links in paragraphs should look like links, usually underlined, not only a different colour. Tap targets should be large enough to hit: WCAG 2.2 sets a minimum of 24 by 24 CSS pixels at level AA, and larger is kinder.

6. Forms with labels and clear errors

Every field needs a visible label that stays visible while typing, not just placeholder text that vanishes. Errors should say what went wrong and how to fix it, next to the field, not only in red. Avoid puzzle CAPTCHAs that many people can't solve.

7. Captions and text alternatives

Videos that explain your service need captions; auto-generated ones are a start but need correcting. Never auto-play sound. If an audio or video clip holds key information, give a text version too; see using video on a business website.

8. Zoom, reflow and motion

The page should still work when zoomed to 200%, and on a narrow screen without sideways scrolling. Carousels that move on their own need a pause control, and animations should respect the "reduce motion" setting on phones and computers.

9. Language and scripts

Set the page language so screen readers pronounce text correctly, and mark sections in another language, such as a Hindi paragraph on an English page. Use real Unicode text, never images of text; Hindi and Devanagari fonts on websites covers readable Indian-language type.

A 15-minute accessibility check

  1. Automated scan. Run the accessibility audit in Lighthouse (in Chrome's developer tools or PageSpeed Insights) or a browser extension such as WAVE on your homepage, a service page and the contact page. These tools find only part of the problems, but they find them fast.
  2. Keyboard test. Put the mouse aside and press Tab from the top of the page. Can you see where you are, open the menu, reach every link and submit the form?
  3. Zoom test. Zoom the browser to 200%. Does text overlap, disappear or need sideways scrolling?
  4. Contrast spot check. Check body text, grey captions, text on photos and button text.
  5. Form test. Submit the form empty and with a wrong email address. Do the errors explain what to fix?
  6. Screen reader taste test. Turn on TalkBack on Android or VoiceOver on an iPhone, or the free NVDA on Windows, and listen to your homepage for a minute. Unlabelled buttons and images stand out quickly.

For a thorough, page-by-page version, use the website accessibility audit checklist.

WordPress-specific fixes

  • Theme choice. The WordPress.org theme directory has an "Accessibility Ready" tag for themes that passed a set of checks. It's a good starting point, though customisation can undo it.
  • Headings in page builders. In Elementor and similar builders, a heading's HTML tag is set separately from its size. Pick the tag for structure, then style the size.
  • Alt text. Add it in the Media Library when uploading, but check images already on pages, because the block editor stores alt text in each image block when it's inserted.
  • Menus. Test dropdowns and the mobile menu button with a keyboard. Icon-only buttons need a text label for screen readers.
  • Forms. Form plugins vary in how they output labels and errors; test yours rather than assuming.
  • Sliders and pop-ups. Turn off auto-rotation, or add a pause button, and make sure pop-ups can be closed with the Escape key.
  • Overlay widgets. Plugins that add an accessibility toolbar or promise one-click compliance don't fix the underlying code, and many accessibility specialists advise against relying on them. Fix the theme and content instead.

Accessibility rules in India and abroad

India's disability rights law and the government's guidelines for websites set accessibility expectations, mainly for government bodies and services they regulate, and the rules continue to develop. Several other countries apply accessibility requirements to some private businesses, especially those selling online. Whether any of this applies to your business, and what it requires, is a question for a lawyer; this guide is about doing the basics well, which is worth it either way.

Accessibility also helps SEO and enquiries

Many fixes overlap with search optimisation: clear headings, descriptive links, alt text and fast, readable pages help Google understand your content too. Labelled forms and readable buttons also mean fewer abandoned enquiries. Accessibility isn't a ranking shortcut, but an accessible site is usually a clearer, better-converting one.

FAQs

What WCAG level should a small business website aim for?

Level AA. It is the level most guidance and laws refer to, and it covers contrast, keyboard access, labels, captions and the other basics in this guide. Level AAA is not expected across a whole site.

Is website accessibility a legal requirement in India?

Requirements in India mainly apply to government websites and some regulated services, and they continue to develop. If you're unsure whether they apply to your business, or you sell abroad where rules differ, ask a lawyer.

Will an accessibility plugin make my website compliant?

No plugin can do that on its own. Toolbar and overlay widgets don't fix missing labels, poor structure or keyboard traps in the underlying code. Fixing the theme, templates and content is what makes the difference.

How do I write good alt text?

Describe what the image shows and why it's there, in one short sentence, without starting with "image of". Leave alt text empty for purely decorative images, and describe the destination for images that are links.

Does website accessibility help SEO?

Indirectly, yes. Headings, alt text, descriptive links and readable pages help search engines understand your content, and a site that's easier to use tends to keep visitors longer and convert better.

Accessibility is easiest to get right when templates are built with it from the start. If your current site fails the 15-minute check in several places, a website redesign is the moment to fix contrast, headings, menus and forms at the root rather than page by page.
