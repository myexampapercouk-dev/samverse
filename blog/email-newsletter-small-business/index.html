How to Start an Email Newsletter for Your Small Business

How to start an email newsletter for a small business: choosing a tool, adding sign-up forms to your website, getting consent, what to send and what to measure.

Sameer Gupta 30 September 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways
  • Use a dedicated email marketing tool rather than your inbox or hosting server, and place short, specific sign-up forms where interested visitors already are.
  • Only email people who chose to join, keep consent boxes unticked, make unsubscribing easy and authenticate your sending domain.
  • Send useful content consistently, often monthly, and judge success by clicks, replies and enquiries rather than open rates.

An email newsletter is one of the few marketing channels you actually own. Social media reach rises and falls with the algorithm, but a list of people who asked to hear from you stays yours. For a small business it doesn't need to be fancy: a useful email once a month, sent to people who genuinely want it, can bring back past customers and nudge undecided ones to enquire. Our guide to lead magnets and newsletters covers free offers that attract sign-ups; this one covers starting and running the newsletter itself.

Is a newsletter right for your business?

A newsletter works best when customers take time to decide, buy repeatedly, or benefit from regular advice. Good fits include:

  • B2B suppliers and manufacturers with long buying cycles
  • Consultants, CAs, coaches and agencies whose expertise is what they sell
  • Clinics and wellness businesses sharing seasonal health reminders
  • Online stores and D2C brands with new launches and repeat buyers

If most of your customers already chat with you on WhatsApp, email can sit alongside it: WhatsApp for quick updates, email for fuller content people can come back to.

Choose an email marketing tool

Don't send newsletters from your Gmail or Outlook inbox with everyone in BCC, and don't send them through your website's hosting server. Both make it likelier your emails land in spam, and neither handles unsubscribes properly. Use a dedicated email marketing service instead. Well-known options include Mailchimp, Brevo, MailerLite, Zoho Campaigns and Kit. When comparing them, check:

  • Free or entry-level plan limits on subscribers and monthly sends (these change, so check current plans)
  • Simple templates that look good on mobile
  • Sign-up forms you can embed on WordPress, or an integration with your existing form plugin
  • Automation, at least a welcome email for new subscribers
  • Reports on clicks, unsubscribes and bounces

Start with whichever feels easiest to use. You can export your list and move later, so this isn't a lifetime decision.

Add sign-up forms to your website

Most sign-ups will come from your website, so place forms where interested people already are:

  • End of blog posts, where readers have just got value from you
  • The footer, as a small, quiet form on every page
  • A dedicated newsletter page you can link from social profiles and your email signature
  • Thank-you pages shown after an enquiry or purchase
  • WooCommerce checkout, as an optional box that is unticked by default

Keep the form short: an email address, plus a first name only if you'll use it. Say exactly what people will get and how often, for example "One practical tip a month for clinic owners. No spam." A vague "Subscribe to our newsletter" gives nobody a reason to sign up.

Get consent right

People should choose to join your list. In practice that generally means:

  • A clear sign-up action, with any consent checkbox left unticked by default
  • Not adding customers, enquiry contacts or visiting cards to your list without asking them first
  • Never buying or borrowing email lists
  • A working unsubscribe link in every email
  • A privacy policy explaining what you collect and why

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act points in the same direction, expecting consent that is clear and easy to withdraw; see DPDP Act basics for websites. If you have subscribers abroad, other laws may apply too. This isn't legal advice, so check the current rules with your lawyer. Double opt-in, where new subscribers confirm by clicking a link, adds a step but keeps fake and mistyped addresses off your list.

What to send, and how often

The best small business newsletters are useful first and promotional second. Ideas that suit most businesses:

  • A practical tip, or an answer to a question customers often ask
  • Your latest article or case study, summarised with a link to read more on your site
  • Seasonal reminders, such as AC servicing before summer or festival order deadlines
  • A behind-the-scenes look at a project, new product or team member
  • An occasional offer, clearly labelled as one

Monthly is a realistic start for most businesses. Consistency matters more than frequency: a list that hears nothing for six months forgets who you are, and is more likely to mark you as spam when you reappear. Keep each email focused on one main idea with one clear call to action, and write a subject line that says what's inside.

Make sure it reaches the inbox

A well-written email is wasted in the spam folder. Before your first send:

  • Send from an address on your own domain, not a free Gmail address
  • Authenticate your domain in the email tool by adding the DNS records it gives you; see SPF, DKIM and DMARC explained
  • Remove addresses that bounce, and consider removing people who haven't opened or clicked anything for a long time

Gmail and Yahoo have tightened their requirements for bulk senders, including domain authentication and easy unsubscribing, so these steps matter more than they used to.

Measure what matters

Email tools show plenty of numbers, but some are more useful than others:

MetricWhat it tells you
Subscriber growthWhether your sign-up forms and placements are working
Click rateWhether the content interested people enough to act
Replies and enquiriesThe real business value of the newsletter
Unsubscribes and spam complaintsWhether you're sending too often or off-topic
Open rateA rough guide only, as privacy features in some email apps make it unreliable

Add UTM tags to the links in your emails (many tools can do this automatically) so Google Analytics shows which visits and enquiries came from the newsletter. Review the numbers every few months rather than after every send.
