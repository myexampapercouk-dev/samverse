The Real Risks of Very Cheap Websites: What Is Usually Left Out, Later Costs and How to Check an Offer

Key takeaways
  • A cheap website can be fine for a simple brochure need, as long as you own the domain, hosting and logins.
  • Very low prices usually mean nulled themes, no backups, no SEO basics and no handover, which turn into costs later.
  • Check ownership, licences, what happens after launch and who fixes problems before paying for any low-cost website.

A very cheap website can be fine as a placeholder, but the low price is usually reached by skipping work you can't see on day one: planning, mobile testing, speed, SEO basics, security, licensed software and ownership of the domain and hosting. Those savings tend to come back later as high renewals, charges for every small change, malware clean-ups, a rebuild and enquiries that never arrive. Before you pay, check whose name the accounts will be in, whether the software is licensed and how the developer's past sites work on your own phone.

This guide is about the risks behind very low-priced offers and how to check one. If you want a site that is planned, tested and owned by you from the start, see WordPress website development for what every site I build includes.

When a cheap website is perfectly fine

A low price isn't automatically a bad sign. A cheap or DIY site can be sensible when:

  • You need a simple placeholder with your name, phone number and location while the business is new.
  • You are testing an idea and expect to rebuild properly once it works.
  • A freelancer with low overheads uses a good, licensed theme, sets the accounts up in your name and keeps the scope small.

Even then, three things are non-negotiable at any price: the domain in your name, licensed software and a contact form that has been tested. The problems start when a cheap site is expected to do the job of a proper one: rank on Google, bring enquiries and represent the business for years.

How very cheap websites are made

  • One template for everyone: a theme demo imported as-is, with only the logo, colours and text swapped.
  • No planning: no thought about which pages customers need, what each should say or where the call to action goes.
  • Little or no testing: checked on one laptop, not on phones, browsers or slow connections.
  • Pirated software: "nulled" premium themes and plugins, which cost nothing upfront and can carry hidden malware; see the risks of nulled themes and plugins.
  • Bundled hosting in the seller's account: a low first-year price, with the real money made on renewals you can't easily move away from.

Five checks before you pay for a cheap website

  1. Open three of their recent sites on your phone, on mobile data. Do they load quickly? Does the menu work? Does tapping the phone number start a call?
  2. Run one of those sites through PageSpeed Insights on mobile. You're not looking for a perfect score, just for obvious problems such as huge images.
  3. Ask to see a form working. Try the contact form on the developer's own website, or ask them to show a test enquiry from one of their builds arriving in an inbox. Forms that look fine but send nothing are common on rushed builds.
  4. Ask in writing whose name the domain and hosting will be registered in, what each costs to renew, and whether you'll get your own administrator login.
  5. Ask which theme and plugins will be used and whether the premium ones come with genuine licences. A vague answer is an answer.

Red flags in a very cheap offer

  • No written scope listing pages, features and what happens after launch
  • The domain registered in the developer's name, "for convenience"
  • "Lifetime free hosting" or unlimited pages for a tiny one-time fee
  • Guaranteed first-page Google rankings
  • No live websites you can open and test yourself
  • Premium themes and plugins "included free" with no licence
  • Pressure to pay the full amount upfront

Getting real value on a small budget

The alternative to a risky cheap site isn't necessarily an expensive one. As a guide, my WordPress website cost guide puts a professionally built landing page at ₹5,000 to ₹15,000 and a small business site of five to eight pages at ₹15,000 to ₹40,000. To get the most from a small budget:

  1. Reduce scope, not quality. Five strong, fast pages do more than fifteen thin ones, and a single focused page can be a sensible start.
  2. Use a quality, lightweight theme rather than paying for a fully custom design.
  3. Write your own first drafts and supply photos, so you pay for building, not waiting.
  4. Launch the essentials first and add a blog, booking or store later.
  5. Keep every account in your own name; the website ownership checklist lists each one.

Already have a cheap website that isn't working?

  1. Secure access first: the domain registrar, hosting and an administrator login. If the previous developer holds them, regaining access from an old developer explains how to get them back.
  2. Check what's worth keeping: pages that rank, content that is still accurate, the domain itself.
  3. Decide whether to fix or replace it. If the theme is sound and the problems are specific, a refresh may be enough; website refresh vs redesign helps you decide.

Cheap website vs affordable website: how to tell them apart

A cheap website and an affordable one can carry a similar quote. The difference is in what the quote covers and whether you can verify it. When you compare offers from freelancers or small agencies in India, look for these signs of an affordable, honest offer:

  • A written scope: the pages, forms, integrations (UPI or Razorpay payments, WhatsApp chat, Google Maps) and the number of revision rounds are listed.
  • Accounts in your name: the domain, hosting and Google accounts are registered to your business email, with GST invoices for anything bought on your behalf.
  • Licensed themes and plugins: the developer tells you which ones are used and whether renewals are yours to pay.
  • A handover: admin logins, a short guide to editing pages and a working backup before final payment.
  • Clear after-launch terms: what free fixes are included, for how long, and what ongoing care would cost to arrange.

A cheap offer usually skips most of this list, not because the work is impossible at that budget, but because time is the first thing cut. FAQs

Is a cheap website worth it?

As a temporary placeholder, it can be. As the main way customers find and judge your business, a very cheap site often costs more over time in renewals, changes, fixes and lost enquiries than a properly built one.

Why are some websites so cheap?

Usually because the same template is reused with minimal changes, there's no planning or testing, pirated plugins replace licensed ones, or the seller expects to earn on hosting renewals and paid changes later.

What is the risk if the developer owns my domain?

You can't move host or developer without their cooperation, renewals are on their terms, and if they disappear or the domain lapses you can lose the address your customers and Google know. Register it in your own name from the start.

Can a cheap website be improved later instead of rebuilt?

Sometimes. If it runs on a maintained, licensed theme and you have full access, content, speed and design can be improved in stages. If it relies on pirated software or an abandoned theme, rebuilding is usually cheaper than patching.

How much should a small business website cost in India?

It depends on the pages, features and design work. As a guide, a professionally built small business WordPress site of five to eight pages typically costs ₹15,000 to ₹40,000, plus yearly domain and hosting. Offers far below that usually leave out testing, SEO basics, security or ownership.

Is a free website builder safer than a cheap developer?

A builder you set up yourself at least keeps the account in your name. The trade-offs are your own time, limited flexibility and, with some builders, no way to move the site elsewhere later without rebuilding it.
