How to Structure Your Website Navigation: Menus, Labels, Depth, Mega Menus, Footer, Breadcrumbs, Mobile Menus, URLs and Internal Links

How to structure website navigation: menu items and labels, depth, mega menus, footer links, breadcrumbs, mobile menus, URL structure and internal links.

Key takeaways
  • Plan the page hierarchy around what visitors come to do, then build a short main menu of five to seven plain labels with one clear call to action.
  • Keep important pages within two or three clicks of the homepage, use the footer and breadcrumbs for secondary routes, and make the mobile menu work with a thumb.
  • Menus are only part of navigation: clean URLs that follow the structure and contextual links between related pages help visitors and Google find everything.

Good website navigation starts with a simple structure: a short main menu of about five to seven items with plain labels, important pages no more than two or three clicks from the homepage, a visible call-to-action button, a footer for secondary links, breadcrumbs on deeper pages and a mobile menu that works with one thumb. Underneath, your URLs should follow the same structure, and pages should link to each other in the content, not just through the menu. That helps visitors find what they came for and helps Google understand which pages matter.

Navigation is one of the first things rebuilt in a website redesign: the menu is organised around how customers choose, usually one page per service or product family, and every old URL is mapped so nothing that ranks is lost. Site structure and internal linking are also part of my WordPress SEO services, because the way pages link together shapes what Google sees as important.

Start with what visitors come to do

Before touching the menu, list the handful of tasks most visitors arrive with. For most businesses they look like this: see what you offer, check whether you serve their area, look at prices or what affects them, see your work, check who you are, and contact you. Sources that show you the real list:

  • Your phone and WhatsApp: what do people ask about before they buy?
  • Search Console: the searches that bring people to your site and the pages they land on.
  • Analytics: your most-visited pages, and the pages people visit just before contacting you.
  • Staff: whoever answers enquiries knows what confuses customers.

The main menu should serve those tasks directly. Anything that doesn't can live in the footer or inside other pages.

Plan the structure before the menu

Navigation is a view of your site's structure, so sketch that first. A fictional example for an air-conditioning service company:

Home
  Services
    AC repair
    AC installation
    Annual maintenance contracts
    Commercial HVAC
  Areas we serve
    Kothrud, Baner, Hinjewadi ...
  Work (case studies)
  About
  Blog
  Contact
  • One page per main service, so each can answer its own questions and rank for its own searches; see writing service pages that convert.
  • A hub page for each group, such as Services, that introduces and links to every page beneath it.
  • Area pages only where you have something specific to say about each area, not copies with the place name swapped; multi-location websites covers this.

The main menu: items, order and labels

Five to seven top-level items is a comfortable range for most business sites. More than that and nothing stands out. Put the items visitors need most at the start, keep Contact at the end where people look for it, and show your main call to action as a distinct button, such as "Get a quote" or "Book appointment".

Labels should use the words customers use, not internal jargon or clever wording:

Vague or clever labelClearer label
SolutionsServices, or the service names themselves
What we doServices
Our journeyAbout
Portfolio showcaseWork, or Projects
ResourcesBlog, or Guides
Connect with usContact

If you have only three or four services, list them directly in the menu instead of hiding them under "Services". Whether you include "Home" is a matter of taste; the logo should link to the homepage either way.

Depth: how many clicks is too many?

Important pages should be reachable within two or three clicks of the homepage. A page buried five levels deep is hard for visitors to find and tends to get less attention from Google. For most business sites, two levels are enough: a group page and the pages within it. Deep structures make sense only for large catalogues or directories, and even then hub pages and filters should keep the click path short.

Dropdowns and mega menus

Dropdowns suit a group of five to ten related pages, such as services. Mega menus, large panels showing several columns of links, suit sites with many products or services in clear groups, such as an industrial supplier or a store. Either way:

  • Make the top-level item a real page, not just a label that opens a menu, so it can be tapped, linked and ranked.
  • Give mega menu columns short headings that match your categories.
  • Don't rely on hover alone; it doesn't exist on touchscreens, and keyboard users need to open menus too.
  • Keep it scannable. A mega menu with eighty links helps nobody choose.

Footer navigation

The footer is where people look for things they expect on every site: contact details, address, timings, a full list of services, policies, and links to social profiles. It's also a fair place for secondary pages such as careers or a glossary. Don't turn it into a block of keyword links to every area and service combination; it helps neither visitors nor rankings.

Breadcrumbs

On deeper pages, a breadcrumb trail such as Home / Services / AC repair shows where the visitor is and lets them move up a level in one tap. It also adds consistent internal links to your hub pages. Breadcrumbs explained covers types, schema and setup in WordPress.

Mobile menus

  • Label the menu icon with the word "Menu"; not everyone recognises three lines.
  • Keep the call, WhatsApp or quote button outside the menu, visible without opening anything.
  • Open submenus with a tap, as an accordion, with tap targets large enough for a thumb.
  • Make it easy to close, and make sure the page behind doesn't scroll while it's open.
  • Test it on a real phone with the text size turned up.

URL structure

URLs should be short, readable and broadly follow your structure, such as /services/ac-repair/ or simply /ac-repair/. Both work; consistency matters more than depth. Use lowercase words with hyphens, avoid dates and IDs in page URLs, and never change URLs that already rank without a 301 redirect from the old address. SEO-friendly URLs in WordPress covers permalinks and changing them safely.

Internal linking beyond the menu

Menus carry visitors to your main sections, but contextual links, written into the text of your pages, carry them to the specific page they need next: a blog post linking to the service it discusses, a service page linking to a relevant case study. These links also help Google understand how pages relate and which are most important. Pages that no menu and no other page links to become orphans that visitors and Google rarely find. See internal linking explained and how to find and fix orphan pages.

Setting up menus in WordPress

  • Classic themes: build menus under Appearance, then Menus, and assign them to the theme's locations, such as primary, mobile and footer. The navigation label can be shorter than the page title.
  • Block themes: edit the Navigation block in the header template part through the Site Editor.
  • Elementor: with Elementor Pro, the header is built in the Theme Builder with a menu widget that uses your WordPress menus.
  • Mega menus: some themes include them; otherwise a mega menu plugin can add them. Check the result on mobile and with a keyboard.
  • Keep one source of truth: update menus when pages are added, renamed or removed, so nothing points to a deleted page.

Test your navigation with real tasks

You know your site too well to judge its menu. Ask three or four people who don't, ideally customers, to find specific things on a phone without searching: "How much does an annual maintenance contract cost?", "Do they come to Wakad?", "Show me a project like mine". Watch where they tap first and where they hesitate. If two people look in the wrong place, the label or the grouping is wrong, not the people. After launch, your analytics show the same thing at scale: pages people reach by an unexpected route, and searches typed into your site search for pages that already exist but can't be found.

Common navigation mistakes

  • Too many top-level items, or labels nobody searches for
  • Services hidden two levels deep under vague groups
  • Dropdown parents that aren't real pages
  • A mobile menu with no visible phone or WhatsApp option
  • Different menus on different pages, so visitors lose their bearings
  • Changing menu labels and URLs during a redesign without redirects

FAQs

How many items should a website menu have?

Around five to seven top-level items suits most business websites. Fewer is fine for a small site; if you need more, group related pages under a clear heading rather than adding to the top level.

Does website navigation affect SEO?

Yes. Menus, breadcrumbs and internal links show Google which pages matter and how they relate, and pages close to the homepage tend to be crawled more often. Clear labels also help visitors stay and find what they need.

Should I use a mega menu?

Only if you have many pages in clear groups, such as a product catalogue. For a typical service business with a handful of services, a simple menu or dropdown is easier to use, especially on phones.

Should the menu labels match the page titles?

Not exactly. Menu labels should be short, such as "AC repair", while the page title can be fuller and more descriptive. The label and the page should clearly be about the same thing.

What's the difference between navigation and site structure?

Site structure is how your pages are organised and linked overall. Navigation is the set of menus, breadcrumbs and links visitors use to move through that structure. Plan the structure first, then design the navigation around it.

If visitors can't find your services or Google seems to ignore key pages, a website redesign can rebuild the structure and menus around how your customers choose, with every old URL redirected. For structure and internal linking on an existing site, see WordPress SEO services.
