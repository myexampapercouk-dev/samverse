How to Write Case Studies for Your Business Website: A Section-by-Section Template, Honest Results, Client Permission, Formats and SEO

Key takeaways
  • Tell each story in a fixed order: the client, the problem, your approach, what you delivered and the outcome, then a quote and a clear next step.
  • State results only as far as you can verify them, with the client agreeing to publish; if there are no numbers, describe what visibly changed.
  • Give every case study its own page with a descriptive title, link it to the service and industry pages it supports, and get permission before naming anyone.

A good case study tells one client's story in a fixed order: who the client is, the problem they had, how you approached it, what you delivered and the outcome, stated only as far as you can verify it, followed by a client quote and a clear next step. Get the client's permission before naming them or showing their work, give each case study its own page with a descriptive title, and link it to the service and industry pages it supports.

For agencies, case studies are often what wins the next client, and agency work raises its own permission questions. Write the problem before the solution, and keep the client at the centre. A case study that is mostly about your tools and process reads like a brochure; one that starts with a problem the reader recognises keeps them reading.

How this site's case studies are structured

The case studies on this site follow one fixed layout, so a visitor who has read one knows where to find everything in the next:

  1. A summary with the industry, the tools the site is built with and a link to the live website
  2. About the client
  3. What the website needed to do: the goals, as a short list
  4. What I built: the main features, as a short list
  5. How the site is organised, followed by two sections on the specific problems it solves
  6. What other businesses in that industry can take from it
  7. Related reading, and links to the matching service and industry pages

On Vansh Group, an electronics manufacturer, the two middle sections cover proving capability to large B2B buyers, and enquiries, downloads and insights: the contact form's service-type dropdown means enquiries arrive already tagged, and a company profile download gives buyers something to share internally. The case studies on this site make no claims about traffic or sales. They describe what anyone can check on the live site, which is a reasonable approach when you don't have outcome figures the client has agreed to share.

Results: only what you can verify

The outcome section is where case studies most often go wrong. Rules worth following:

  • Publish numbers only with the client's agreement, and only from a source you can name, such as their analytics, CRM or order records.
  • Give the timeframe and context: "enquiries in the three months after launch compared with the three months before" means something; "enquiries tripled" on its own does not.
  • Don't claim credit for everything. If the client also started advertising, hired a salesperson or had a seasonal peak, say so.
  • Never round up, estimate or invent. A made-up figure can damage trust with the client as well as with readers, and misleading claims can fall foul of consumer protection and advertising rules.
  • Without numbers, describe what changed: a booking system now taking appointments, the team updating the site themselves, a catalogue organised the way buyers search, pages that load quickly on phones when tested.

Getting permission, and white-label work

  • Agree it early: raise portfolio use in your proposal or contract, with the client's right to review the case study before it goes live.
  • Let them approve the draft, including the quote, any figures, screenshots and how they are named.
  • Agree what stays private: prices, internal numbers, admin dashboards, customer data and anything shown in screenshots of forms or orders.
  • Offer an anonymised version if they would rather not be named, such as "a Pune engineering company", without inventing details to fill the gap.
  • White-label work belongs to the agency. A developer or designer working under an agency's brand shouldn't publish those projects without the agency's consent, and an agency should check its client contract and any NDA before presenting the work.

Doctors, lawyers and other regulated professionals should check their professional body's current rules before publishing anything that resembles a client or patient story.

SEO for case study pages

  • A descriptive title, such as "[Client] Website Case Study" or "[What you did] for a [type of business] in [city]". This site uses titles like "Vansh Group Website Case Study".
  • Headings that name the problem and the industry, because those are the words a similar business searches with.
  • Real text, explaining decisions and trade-offs. A gallery of screenshots with one line under each gives search engines and readers little to go on.
  • Internal links both ways: from the case study to the service and industry pages, and from those pages back to the case study. Internal linking explained shows how.
  • Compressed images with descriptive alt text, such as "Homepage of the Vansh Group website", rather than "screenshot1".
  • A hub page listing every case study, grouped by industry or service. This site's work page groups them under doctors and clinics, manufacturers and B2B, agencies, and directories and local businesses.
  • Structured data, such as breadcrumbs and a description of the page; this site marks case studies up as CreativeWork. Don't add review or rating markup to your own case studies.

Before you publish: a quick checklist

  1. The client has approved the final text, quote, figures and images.
  2. Every number has a source and a timeframe, or has been removed.
  3. Screenshots show no personal data, prices or private dashboards.
  4. The page links to the related service and industry pages, and they link back.
  5. The title and headings name the client type, the problem and, where relevant, the city.
  6. The page ends with a clear next step.

FAQs

What should a business case study include?

The client, their problem, your approach, what you delivered, the outcome as far as you can verify it, a short client quote and a call to action, ideally with a summary box at the top.

Can I write a case study without results or numbers?

Yes. Describe what visibly changed, such as new features, a clearer structure or a site the team can now update, and explain the decisions behind it. Never invent figures to fill the gap.

Do I need the client's permission to publish a case study?

Yes, before naming them or showing their work. Let them review the draft, agree what stays private, and offer an anonymised version if they prefer.

Can an agency's developer publish white-label work in their own portfolio?

Not without the agency's consent. White-label work is presented under the agency's brand, and the agency in turn needs its client's agreement.

How is a case study different from a testimonial?

A testimonial is a short quote in the customer's words. A case study is the full story of a project, which can include a testimonial as one of its sections.
