How to Create a Meet the Team Page for a Small Business

How to create a meet the team page for a small business: who to include, real photos, bios that show expertise, accurate qualifications and staff consent.

Sameer Gupta 1 October 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways
  • Feature the people customers will meet or rely on, with consistent real photos instead of stock or AI-generated faces.
  • Write short bios that explain what each person does for customers, with qualifications stated exactly and only where current.
  • Get written consent from staff, avoid personal contact details, and update the page promptly when people join or leave.

People like to know who they'll be dealing with. A patient wants to see the doctor before booking, a homeowner wants to know who will come to measure the kitchen, and a business client wants to know who will actually do the work. A good team page answers that honestly. This guide covers who to include, how to write bios, what to check before publishing and how to keep the page accurate as your team changes. For the wider story of your business, see how to write an About page that builds trust.

Do you need a separate team page?

Not always. If your business is you and one or two colleagues, a short team section on your About page is usually enough. A separate page makes sense when:

  • Customers choose a specific person, such as a doctor, lawyer, trainer or stylist
  • You have several specialists and visitors want to know who handles what
  • Your team's experience is a big part of why customers pick you, as with consultancies, agencies and engineering firms
  • You're hiring and want candidates to see who they'd work with

For clinics and professional firms, individual profile pages for key people can also help, because patients and clients often search for them by name.

Who to include

Start with the people customers will meet or speak to, then add the people whose expertise shapes the result:

  • Founders and partners: the people accountable for the business
  • Client-facing staff: doctors, consultants, project managers, front-desk staff and site supervisors
  • Key specialists: designers, engineers, therapists or technicians whose skill customers are paying for

You don't need to list every employee. A larger team can be shown with a group photo and a line such as "supported by our service technicians across Pune". If you work with freelancers or associates, describe the relationship honestly rather than presenting them as full-time staff.

Real photos beat stock every time

A team page full of stock models is worse than no team page, because it suggests the real people are hiding. Use real photos, and make them consistent:

  • The same background, framing and lighting for every headshot, so the grid looks tidy
  • Natural expressions and the clothes people actually wear at work
  • A few "at work" photos alongside the headshots: the team on site, in the clinic or around the workshop table
  • A recent phone and good daylight are enough; one organised photo session is easier than chasing people individually

Never use AI-generated faces or stock photos to represent staff. For when stock images are fine and when they aren't, see stock photos vs real photos.

Write bios that show expertise

A good bio tells the customer what this person does for them and why they're good at it. Keep each one to roughly 50 to 120 words and follow a simple structure:

  1. Name and role, written plainly ("Senior Site Engineer", not "Chief Happiness Officer")
  2. What they handle for customers, such as "leads every kitchen installation from measurement to handover"
  3. Relevant experience, with specifics: the type of projects, the kind of patients, the industries they know
  4. Qualifications and memberships that genuinely apply
  5. One human detail, if they're happy to share it, such as the languages they speak

Write every bio in the same voice, usually the third person, and avoid clichés like "passionate" and "dedicated professional". Languages spoken are especially useful in India, where many customers prefer to explain their problem in Hindi or a regional language.

State qualifications accurately

Qualifications are where a team page earns trust, and where exaggeration does the most damage. Before publishing, check each claim with the person concerned:

  • Write degrees and certifications with their exact names, as they appear on the certificate
  • Use "certified" or "registered" only when the person holds a current certification or registration
  • Give years of experience the person can back up, not a rounded-up figure
  • Keep memberships current, and remove any that have lapsed

Doctors, advocates, chartered accountants and some other professions have rules from their professional bodies about how they may present themselves and advertise. Check the current rules for your profession, or ask your professional body, before adding registration numbers, titles or claims about specialisation.

Get consent and respect privacy

A team member's name, photo and bio are personal information, so ask before publishing them. Get agreement in writing (an email is fine), show them the final bio and photo, and let them choose what's included. Some people prefer to show only their first name, or no photo, and that's reasonable, especially for junior staff or anyone with personal safety concerns.

  • Don't publish personal mobile numbers or personal email addresses; use role-based addresses such as appointments@ on your own domain
  • Link to someone's LinkedIn profile only if they want it linked
  • Agree in advance what happens to their photo and bio if they leave

It's worth understanding the basics of data protection here; see India's DPDP Act and your website, and ask a lawyer if you're unsure what applies to you.

Keep it updated when people leave

An outdated team page causes real problems: customers ask for someone who left a year ago, or a former employee finds their photo still promoting your business. Build updates into your routine:

  • Add "update the website" to your joining and leaving checklists, alongside email access and ID cards
  • Remove a departed person's photo and bio promptly, and check other pages and your Google Business Profile for mentions
  • If someone had their own profile page, redirect it to the team page rather than leaving a broken link
  • Review the whole page every few months for changed roles, new qualifications and old photos

On WordPress, storing team members as a simple custom content type, with fields for photo, role and bio, makes updates quick and keeps the layout consistent, even when someone without design skills does the editing.

Design the page for scanning

  • A grid of cards showing photo, name and role, with the bio below or on a profile page
  • Customer-facing people first, rather than strict order of seniority
  • A clear next step, such as a "Book with this doctor" button or a link to your contact page
  • Compressed images, with each person's name and role as alt text, so the page stays fast on mobile
