How to Plan a Website Content Calendar for a Small Business: Themes, Festival and Seasonal Dates, a Realistic Cadence, Repurposing and a Template

Plan a website content calendar: themes tied to your services, Indian festival and seasonal dates, a cadence you can keep, repurposing and a free template.

A website content calendar is a simple plan of what you'll publish, when and why. Pick three to five themes tied to the services you sell, place topics in the months when customers search for them (including festivals and seasons), choose a pace you can keep for a whole year, and track every piece in one spreadsheet with its topic, target page, owner and dates. Review it monthly against Search Console, and update old posts as well as adding new ones.

What a content calendar is for

Without a plan, small business blogs tend to start with a burst of posts and then go quiet, or fill up with topics nobody searches for. A calendar fixes three things:

  • Relevance: every piece connects to something you sell.
  • Timing: seasonal topics go live before customers start searching, not during the rush.
  • Consistency: a pace you can keep, with owners and dates, so it actually happens.

It doesn't need special software or a marketing team. A spreadsheet and an hour a month to plan is enough for most businesses.

Step 1: Choose three to five themes

Themes are the broad subjects you want to be known for, each linked to a service or product page. Every article in a theme links to that page, which builds a cluster of related content around what you sell. For an AC service company, for example:

Step 2: Collect topics from real questions

  • Questions customers ask on calls, WhatsApp, email and at the counter, in their own words.
  • Objections before they buy: price, timing, trust, alternatives.
  • Search Console queries that already bring impressions to your site; finding content ideas in Search Console shows where to look.
  • Questions your sales or front-desk staff answer every week.
  • Recent projects that could become case studies.

Put every idea into one list, tagged with its theme, and you'll have more topics than you can write.

Step 3: Map the year, including Indian seasons and festivals

Many Indian festivals follow the lunar calendar and move by a few weeks each year, so check the dates for the year you're planning. A rough calendar to adapt:

New articles take time to be found, so plan seasonal pieces about two months ahead and publish them at least four to six weeks before the season. Reuse one permanent page per festival rather than a new one each year; seasonal and festival campaigns explains why. Only cover festivals and seasons that genuinely relate to what you sell.

Step 4: Choose a pace you can keep

Consistency beats bursts. Work out how many hours a month someone can realistically give to writing and checking content, then choose a pace that fits, such as one article a month, two a month, or a case study every couple of months alongside articles. Two good articles a month kept up for a year beat twenty in one week followed by silence.

Many small businesses find it easier to batch the work: plan a quarter at a time, write two or three pieces in one afternoon, and schedule them in WordPress using the Publish panel's schedule option, so posts go live on their dates without anyone remembering.

Step 5: Mix content types

  • How-to guides and checklists for common questions.
  • Comparisons, such as one option against another.
  • Cost and timeline guides that explain what drives the price.
  • Case studies from real projects, with the client's permission.
  • FAQs added to service pages, which count as content too.
  • Refreshes of older articles that are slipping or out of date.

Give refreshes a regular slot, such as one each month. Updating a post that already ranks often helps more than publishing a new one; how to update old blog posts covers the process.

Repurpose every piece

Writing is the slow part, so plan reuse into the calendar. One article can become a Google Business Profile post, a WhatsApp status or message to opted-in customers, an Instagram carousel, a LinkedIn post and a section of your newsletter, each linking back to the full article. Repurposing website content for social media has more ideas.

Tools to run it

  • A spreadsheet in Google Sheets or Excel is enough for most businesses and easy to share.
  • Task boards such as Trello or Notion suit teams that like moving cards through stages.
  • Editorial calendar plugins for WordPress, such as PublishPress Planner, add a calendar view of scheduled and draft posts inside the dashboard.
  • WordPress scheduling, built in, publishes posts at a set date and time.
  • Google Trends shows when searches for seasonal topics start rising in your state.

Pick whichever your team will actually open; the tool matters far less than the habit.

Review it every month

  1. Check which pages gained impressions and clicks in Search Console, and plan more on those themes.
  2. Look for queries with impressions but no matching article, and add them to the topic list.
  3. Check which articles lead to enquiries, calls and WhatsApp messages, not just visits.
  4. Move slipping or outdated posts into next month's refresh slot.
  5. Once a quarter, look at the next three months of seasons and festivals and fill those dates first.

Common mistakes

  • Planning a pace you can't keep, then stopping altogether.
  • Writing about festivals or trends unrelated to what you sell.
  • Publishing seasonal content after the season has started.
  • Articles that never link to the service they relate to.
  • Only adding new posts, never updating old ones.

FAQs

How often should a small business publish on its website?

As often as you can keep up for a year with good quality. For many small businesses that's one or two articles a month, plus regular updates to existing pages.

How far ahead should I plan content?

A quarter at a time works well: detailed plans for the next three months and rough themes for the rest of the year, with seasonal pieces placed first.

When should festival content go live?

At least four to six weeks before the festival, so it has time to be found, and earlier for B2B buyers, corporate gifting and weddings, which are planned well in advance.

Should I write about every festival?

No. Only the ones that connect to what you sell or how your customers buy. A generic greeting post rarely brings visitors or enquiries.

What should a content calendar include?

For each piece: the publish date, theme, working title, the customer question it answers, the page it links to, the format, the owner and its status.
