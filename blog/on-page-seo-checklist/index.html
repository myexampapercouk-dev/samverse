On-Page SEO Checklist: 15 Things to Check on Every Page You Publish, From Search Intent and Titles to Schema and Speed

An on-page SEO checklist for every page you publish: intent, title, meta description, headings, URL, links, images, schema, canonical, speed and freshness.

Key takeaways
  • On-page SEO starts before writing: match the type of page Google already shows for the search, and give each search one page so your own pages never compete.
  • Get the visible basics right on every page: a unique title and description, one clear H1, a direct answer near the top, real experience and a clear next step.
  • Then check the technical layer: internal links in and out, compressed images with alt text, matching schema, a self-referencing canonical, no stray noindex, and fast loading on a phone.

On-page SEO is everything on a page that you control and that helps it rank: matching the search intent, the title and meta description, headings, the content itself, internal links, images, structured data and technical signals such as the canonical tag and loading speed. The checklist below has fifteen checks to run on every service page, product page or article before and after you publish it, with links to detailed guides where a check needs more than a line.

It is the list I work through for each page in my WordPress SEO services: one page mapped to each search, titles and descriptions written to fit in results, headings, alt text and URLs tidied with any changed URL redirected, and the gaps in content noted. You can use it yourself on your own pages.

Start with the table, then use the sections below it, which explain each group of checks and how to do them in WordPress.

Before you write: intent and targeting (checks 1 and 2)

Search the phrase yourself on a phone and look at what ranks. If the results are all how-to guides, a service page won't rank for it however well it is optimised, and the reverse is true for "hire" or "near me" searches. Search intent explained shows how to read the results page and match it.

Then check your own site. If an existing page already targets the same search, improve that page instead of creating a second one, or the two will compete and neither will rank well. A simple spreadsheet mapping each target search to one URL prevents this as the site grows.

Titles, descriptions and URLs (checks 3 to 5)

  • Title tag: lead with the words searchers use, add what makes your page worth clicking, and keep it unique across the site. Google truncates titles by pixel width, so treat 50 to 60 characters as a guide rather than a hard rule.
  • Meta description: it doesn't affect rankings directly, but it shapes clicks. Summarise the page, include the main query naturally, and give a reason to click. Google sometimes rewrites it, usually when it doesn't match the search.
  • URL: set it once, before publishing, and keep it short, such as /services/ac-repair-pune/ rather than a long sentence with a date. Changing it later needs a 301 redirect.

In WordPress, Rank Math and Yoast both show a snippet editor below the post where you set the SEO title, description and slug. How to write meta titles and descriptions has formulas and examples, including for e-commerce pages.

Headings and content (checks 6 to 9)

  • One H1 that says what the page is about. Most WordPress themes use the page title as the H1, so don't add another heading block set to H1 inside the content.
  • H2s for the main sections, H3s within them, written so someone skimming the headings alone understands the page. Headings that match the questions people ask also help with featured snippets.
  • Answer first. Put a direct two or three sentence answer at the top, then the detail. Readers who only want the answer get it; the rest keep reading.
  • Depth with substance: cover the sub-questions a searcher would have next, with examples, prices or what affects them, your process and local detail. Length follows from that; padding doesn't help.
  • A clear next step at the end and, on long pages, near the top too. A page that ranks but doesn't lead anywhere wastes the visit.

Links and images (checks 10 and 11)

Internal links help Google find pages and understand how they relate, and they pass visitors on to the next useful page. Link out from the new page to two or three closely related pages with anchor text that describes the destination, never "click here". Then go back to existing pages on the same topic and add a link to the new one, because a page with no links pointing at it is hard for Google to find and judge. Internal linking explained covers anchors, hubs and how many links make sense.

For images, compress them, upload them at roughly the size they display, give files descriptive names such as ac-service-technician-pune.jpg, and write alt text that describes what the image shows. Use real photos of your work wherever possible. Image SEO covers formats, alt text and Google Images in detail.

Technical checks (checks 12 to 14)

  • Schema markup: Service on service pages, Product on products, Article on posts, BreadcrumbList site-wide, usually from your SEO plugin, and only describing what is on the page. Schema markup explained covers which types to use.
  • Canonical tag: the page's canonical should point to itself, at the exact URL you want indexed. Problems arise with duplicate versions, filtered URLs and copied pages; canonical tags explained covers when to point it elsewhere.
  • Indexability: no noindex set by mistake in the SEO plugin's advanced settings, "Discourage search engines" unticked in Settings > Reading, and the page appearing in your XML sitemap.
  • Speed and mobile: test the page in PageSpeed Insights and on a real mid-range phone. Large images, sliders and heavy scripts are the usual causes of a slow page. Core Web Vitals explained covers the measurements Google uses.

Trust and freshness (check 15)

Pages that ask readers to trust you with health, money or a big purchase need clear signs of who is behind them: an author or business name, relevant experience, real examples, contact details and accurate, sourced facts. Google's guidelines group these ideas under E-E-A-T; E-E-A-T explained shows how a small business demonstrates it.

Freshness matters where facts change, such as prices, rules, software and dates. Review important pages at least once a year, update what has changed, and only change the "updated" date when the content has actually changed.

How to check a page in five minutes

  1. Open the page and press Ctrl+U to view the source. Search for <title to see the title tag, "canonical" for the canonical URL, "noindex" for any stray robots tag, and "application/ld+json" for structured data.
  2. Check the SEO plugin's snippet preview for the title and description length.
  3. Open the page on your phone: read the first screen, tap the buttons and check the images load quickly.
  4. Run it through PageSpeed Insights on the mobile setting.
  5. After publishing, inspect the URL in Search Console to confirm Google can crawl and index it.

After you publish

  • Request indexing in Search Console for important pages, and add links to them from related pages.
  • Share the page where your customers are: WhatsApp, LinkedIn, email or your Google Business Profile.
  • After a few weeks, check the Search Console Performance report for the queries the page appears for, and improve it to answer them better.
  • Revisit older pages that have slipped; how to update old blog posts shows how to find, refresh and merge them.

Common on-page mistakes

  • The same title or description on many pages, often left as the theme default
  • Thin pages with a few lines of text, created for every keyword variation
  • Keyword stuffing that reads unnaturally to customers
  • Two pages competing for the same search
  • New pages with no internal links pointing to them
  • A noindex left on after launch, or a canonical pointing to the wrong page

FAQs

What is on-page SEO?

It is optimising the parts of a page you control, including content, title, description, headings, links, images, structured data and technical tags, so search engines understand it and searchers find it useful.

How is on-page SEO different from technical SEO?

On-page SEO works page by page on content and tags. Technical SEO covers the whole site: crawling, indexing, sitemaps, redirects, site speed and structure. The two overlap at canonicals, schema and speed, which is why they appear in this checklist.

How long should a title tag be?

Roughly 50 to 60 characters, since Google cuts titles by pixel width. Put the most important words first so they show even when the end is cut.

Does the meta description affect rankings?

Not directly. It affects whether people click your result, and Google may rewrite it if it doesn't match the search. A clear, accurate description still earns more clicks.

How many internal links should a page have?

There's no fixed number. Link wherever it genuinely helps the reader, usually a few related pages in the body plus your navigation, and make sure every important page has links pointing to it.
