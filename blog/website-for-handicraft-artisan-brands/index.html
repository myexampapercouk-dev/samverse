Websites for Handicraft and Artisan Brands: Craft Stories, Product Photos, Handmade Variation, Fragile Shipping, Wholesale Enquiries and GI Labels

Plan a handicraft website: honest artisan stories, photos that show texture, handmade variation notes, shipping fragile pieces and wholesale enquiries.

Key takeaways
  • Tell the story of the craft and the people who make it with their consent and in specific, checkable terms, and use GI names, handloom marks or fair trade claims only where they are genuinely held.
  • Explain what varies between handmade pieces and what does not, photograph texture and scale clearly, and give exact materials, dimensions, care and lead times on every product.
  • Treat retail, overseas and trade buyers as separate routes: a store for single pieces, clear shipping and breakage terms for fragile items, and a wholesale page with MOQs and a quote form.

A handicraft brand sells two things at once: an object and the skill behind it. The website has to show the piece clearly enough that a buyer can judge texture, size and finish on a phone, tell the story of the craft and its makers honestly, explain why no two pieces are identical, and get fragile items to buyers in India and abroad without breakage. For many artisan brands, wholesale and trade enquiries matter as much as retail orders, so the site needs a route for those too.

For selling single pieces online, my WooCommerce development service covers the store: catalogue and categories, Indian payment gateways, PayPal for international payments, cash on delivery and shipping rules, GST invoices and a fast mobile checkout. If most of your business is bulk orders from retailers, interior designers or importers, a catalogue with quote requests, brochure downloads and export enquiries, as in my websites for manufacturers and B2B suppliers, may suit you better than a cart. Many artisan brands need a bit of both.

Give each buyer an obvious way in: the shop in the main menu, a shipping page that covers international orders, and a separate "Wholesale and trade" link in the menu and footer.

Telling the craft story without overclaiming

The story is why many buyers choose handmade over factory-made, and it is also where brands most often exaggerate. Make it specific:

  • The craft: the technique, the region it comes from, the tools and materials, and how long a typical piece takes
  • The makers: who they are and how you work together, whether in your own workshop, with a cluster of families, or through a cooperative or NGO
  • The process: photos or a short video of each stage, from raw material to finished piece
  • A "made by" line on products, naming the artisan or cluster where you can

Ask artisans before publishing their names, photos and villages, and explain where they will appear. Show them as skilled makers with names and expertise, not as objects of charity. Claims about fair pay, natural dyes, sustainable materials or "zero plastic" should describe what you actually do, such as how and when artisans are paid, so a buyer could check them if they asked.

GI names, handloom marks and other labels

Labels carry real weight with buyers, which is why they need the most care:

  • GI names: many Indian crafts are registered Geographical Indications. Use a GI name only for products genuinely made in that region by that method, and find out whether the current rules require you to register as an authorised user before using the GI logo.
  • Handloom labels: the India Handloom Brand and the Handloom Mark are registration-based schemes, so display them only on products from a registered user, whether that is you or your supplier.
  • Fair trade and other certifications: mention them only if your business or the product is certified, and name the certifying body.
  • "Handmade": be precise. "Hand-painted on a machine-made base" or "hand-finished" is honest; calling the whole piece handmade is not.

When in doubt about any label, ask the body that issues it before it goes on your site. Websites for saree and ethnic wear stores covers the same care with Silk Mark and weave names.

Photographing handmade products

Handmade work sells on texture, finish and detail, which flat, over-edited photos hide:

  • Side light from a window, which brings out weave, carving, hammer marks and glaze
  • Scale: the piece in a hand, on a shelf or in a room, alongside the dimensions in the text
  • Every side: the back of embroidery, the base of pottery and the inside of baskets
  • Close-ups of the details that show handwork, such as brush strokes or knots
  • Lifestyle photos showing the piece in use, in homes that look like your buyers' homes
  • Making-of videos, hosted on YouTube and embedded so they do not slow the page

Handicrafts are often found visually, through Google Images and Google Lens, so descriptive file names and alt text matter more than usual; see image SEO for Google Images and Lens.

Product pages: materials, dimensions and the handmade variation note

Every product page should answer what a buyer would ask if they held the piece:

  • Materials, exactly: sheesham wood or mango wood, solid brass or brass-plated, natural or chemical dyes
  • Dimensions and weight in centimetres and inches
  • Care: how to clean brass, oil wood or wash block prints
  • Lead time for made-to-order pieces, as a range you can keep to
  • A clear variation note

The variation note works best when it says what varies and what does not. For example: "Each bowl is thrown and glazed by hand, so the glaze colour and size may vary slightly from the photo. The shape, quality and finish are the same." That is honest, sets expectations and reduces returns. It should never be an excuse for a photo that misrepresents the piece. For one-of-a-kind pieces, photograph each one separately, set its stock to one and say "the piece you will receive". Writing product descriptions that sell has a framework for the rest of the page.

Packing and shipping fragile pieces

Within India

  • Packaging that survives a courier network: a snug inner box, cushioning on every side and a strong outer carton, tested by sending a few pieces to friends in other cities
  • Volumetric weight: bulky packaging can cost more than heavy packaging, so set product weights and dimensions accurately for shipping rates
  • A breakage policy: what the buyer should do if a piece arrives damaged, such as photos within a set time, and whether you replace or refund. If you need an unboxing video for claims, say so before purchase, not after
  • COD limits on high-value or very fragile items, if refused deliveries are costly for you

Abroad

International shipping raises questions a domestic order never does, and the answers depend on the destination, so treat this as a list to check rather than rules. Some materials face extra requirements or restrictions in certain countries, such as untreated wood, plant fibres and seeds, and animal-derived materials like bone, horn, leather or feathers. Customs duties may be charged to the buyer on arrival unless you include them in the price. Exports also involve GST and paperwork questions for your CA. Your own store alongside marketplaces

Marketplaces and craft platforms bring buyers who are already shopping, but they leave little room for the story and keep most of the customer relationship. Your own website is where the full story, the makers and the trade route live, and where repeat buyers and gift buyers come back directly. Many brands use both and keep their own site as the home for new collections and wholesale. Own website vs marketplaces compares the costs and a hybrid approach.

Wholesale and trade enquiries

Bulk orders from boutiques, concept stores, interior designers, hotels and importers can be worth more than many retail sales, and trade buyers decide quickly whether a supplier looks organised. A trade page should cover:

  • Who you supply, with photos of your products in shops or projects you have permission to show
  • Minimum order quantities per product or per order
  • Customisation: colours, sizes, private labelling and packaging
  • Samples: whether you send them and on what terms
  • Lead times and capacity, stated as ranges you can genuinely meet, since handmade production cannot simply be sped up
  • A catalogue to download, either free or after a short form
  • A quote form asking for products, quantities, destination country and the date needed

If trade buyers order regularly, trade prices can sit behind a login so retail visitors never see them; see WooCommerce wholesale and dealer pricing.

Content that brings craft buyers from search

People search for handmade products in two ways. Some search for the product, such as "hand block printed cotton bedsheet king size" or "handmade brass diya set", which is why product names should describe the object, not just carry a collection name. Others search to learn, such as "how to clean brass at home" or "how to tell handloom from powerloom". A small library of care guides and craft explainers, each linking to the products it mentions, answers those questions and shows that you know your craft.

FAQs

Do I need a GI tag to sell handicrafts online?

No. You only need to be careful with GI names and logos: use them only for products genuinely made in that region by that method, and check whether you need to register as an authorised user before using the logo.

Can I ship handicrafts abroad from my website?

Often yes, but check with your courier which materials can go to each country, decide who pays customs duties, and ask your CA about export paperwork and GST before you take international orders.

How should I word the note about handmade variation?

Say exactly what can vary, such as colour, size or texture, and what stays the same, such as quality and finish. Keep the photos accurate so the note explains small differences rather than covering big ones.

Should I sell on marketplaces as well as my own website?

Many artisan brands do. Use marketplaces for reach, and your own site for the full story, repeat customers, gift buyers and wholesale enquiries.

Can I show wholesale prices only to approved trade buyers?

Yes. WooCommerce can hide trade prices behind a login, with a registration form you approve, minimum quantities and separate price tiers for trade accounts.
