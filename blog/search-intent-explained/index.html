Search Intent Explained: The Types of Intent, How to Read Them From Google and How to Match Your Pages

Search intent explained with examples: informational, navigational, commercial, transactional and local intent, how to read it from Google and match your pages.

Key takeaways
  • Search intent is the goal behind a query; Google ranks the page type that best serves that goal, so a great page of the wrong type rarely ranks.
  • Read intent from the current results: the page types, formats and angles on page one tell you what Google thinks searchers want.
  • Give each intent its own page, such as a guide for learning, a comparison for choosing and a service page for hiring, and link them together.

Search intent is the goal behind a search: whether the person wants to learn something, find a specific site, compare options, buy or hire, or find something nearby. Google ranks the type of page that best satisfies that goal, so matching intent (for example, a service page for "hire WordPress developer" and a guide for "how to speed up WordPress") matters more than how many times a keyword appears.

This guide explains the main types of search intent with examples, shows how to read intent from Google's results, and gives a practical check you can run before writing or rewriting any page.

The main types of search intent

IntentWhat the searcher wantsExample queriesPage that fits
InformationalTo learn or solve a problem"what is a child theme", "how to add Google Maps to website"Guide, tutorial, explainer
NavigationalTo reach a specific site or page"Razorpay dashboard login", "Samverse"The brand's own page

TransactionalTo buy, book or hire now"hire WordPress developer", "website maintenance packages"Service page, product page, pricing page

Many SEO guides talk about four types; local intent is often treated as a mix of commercial and transactional intent with a location. For Indian service businesses it is worth thinking about separately, because so many valuable searches include a city or "near me".

How to read intent from Google's results

The quickest way to understand intent is to search the query yourself (logged out or in a private window, set to your target country) and look at page one:

  • Page type: are the results blog guides, service pages, product pages, category pages, videos or directories?
  • Format: step-by-step tutorials, lists, comparisons, calculators, templates?
  • Angle: for beginners, for India, for this year, free, cheapest, fastest?
  • SERP features: a map pack signals local intent; shopping results signal transactional intent; "People also ask" boxes show related questions you should answer; video carousels suggest people want to watch.

If nine of ten results are guides, a service page will struggle to rank for that query, however good it is. Write a guide and link it to the service page instead.

Words that signal intent

  • Informational: how, what, why, guide, tutorial, examples, ideas, meaning
  • Commercial: best, top, vs, review, comparison, alternatives, pros and cons
  • Transactional: hire, buy, price, cost, packages, quote, services, company, agency
  • Local: near me, city or area names, "in Pune", "Mumbai"

These are hints, not rules. "WordPress website cost India" looks transactional but often shows guides that explain cost, because searchers want to understand the range before contacting anyone. The results page is the final answer. Keyword research for small businesses shows how to collect these queries in the first place, and long-tail keywords explains why specific phrases are easier to match.

Mixed and shifting intent

Some queries have mixed intent: results include guides and service pages together. In that case, the page that wins usually does both, such as a service page that explains the process and prices clearly, or a guide with an obvious route to hire help. Intent can also shift over time. A query that once showed blog posts may start showing videos or forum threads. Recheck important queries every few months, especially if rankings drop without any change on your side.

How to match a page to intent

  1. Choose the right page type based on what ranks now.
  2. Answer the main question in the first two lines. Someone searching "how long does SEO take" wants a timeframe immediately, not a history of search engines.
  3. Match the depth: a quick definition query needs a concise answer; a comparison query needs criteria, a table and a recommendation.
  4. Use the searcher's wording in the title, a heading and a FAQ question.
  5. Offer the next step for the likely next intent: a guide reader may be ready to compare; a comparison reader may be ready to hire.

For transactional pages, writing service pages that convert covers what buyers need to see.

Worked example: one topic, three intents

Take WordPress speed. "Why is my WordPress site slow" is informational; the right page is a diagnostic guide such as why is my WordPress site slow. "Best caching plugin for WordPress" is commercial; the right page compares options with criteria. "WordPress speed optimisation service" is transactional; the right page is a service page such as WordPress speed optimisation with scope, process and how to start. Trying to rank one page for all three usually means none ranks well.

Intent for Indian buyers: price, location and language

Searches from India often carry extra intent signals worth noticing. Price words such as cost, charges, packages and plans are common even in early research, so service pages benefit from explaining what drives the cost. City names and "near me" are frequent for services, which means location pages and a Google Business Profile do much of the work. Some searches use local terms, such as AMC for a maintenance contract, or mix Hindi and English; use the wording your customers actually type, which you can find in the Search Console Queries report. Matching these details often separates a page stuck on page three from one on page one for long-tail queries.

Use Search Console to spot intent mismatches

In Search Console, open the Performance report, filter to one page and look at its queries. If a service page gets impressions mostly for "how to" queries, it is attracting informational intent it can't satisfy, and a supporting guide will rank better for those. If a blog post gets impressions for "price" or "hire" queries, add a clear link to the service page or a short pricing section. High impressions with very low click-through often mean the title promises something different from what searchers want, so rewrite the title to match the intent shown by the queries.

Common intent mistakes

  • Targeting a guide query with a service page, or a hire query with a blog post.
  • Long introductions that delay the answer an informational searcher came for.
  • Creating several pages for the same intent with slightly different keywords, so they compete with each other.
  • Ignoring local intent: a service page with no city, area or Google Business Profile support.
  • Comparison pages that never actually recommend anything.

Connect intents with internal links

Build a path between pages of different intent on the same topic. Guides link to comparisons and service pages; service pages link to supporting guides that answer objections. This helps readers move towards a decision and helps Google understand which page is the main one for each query. For local intent, the local SEO guide for Indian small businesses explains how pages and your profile work together.

Fixing a page that targets the wrong intent

When a page ranks poorly because it serves the wrong intent, you have three options. Rework it into the page type Google is showing, for example turning a thin service page into a full guide if the results are all guides. Split it, keeping the service page for hiring intent and creating a separate guide for the informational queries. Merge it, if you have two pages fighting over the same intent: combine them into the stronger one and redirect the other. After any change, watch the page's queries in Search Console for several weeks before judging the result, as rankings take time to settle.

Intent across the buying journey

A typical buyer of a service moves through intents: first learning ("why is my website slow"), then comparing ("WordPress speed plugin vs developer"), then hiring ("WordPress speed optimisation service India"). Mapping your pages to these stages shows gaps. Many small business sites have only service pages, so they miss the earlier searches entirely; others have many blog posts and weak service pages, so readers never convert. A balanced site has a few strong service pages supported by guides and comparisons that link to them.

Intent and page titles

Your title is the first place a searcher judges intent match. For informational queries, promise the answer ("How Long Does SEO Take? What Happens Month by Month"). For commercial queries, promise the comparison and a verdict. For transactional queries, state the service, place and a reason to choose you. A title that clearly matches intent can earn clicks from a lower position than a vague title higher up.

Intent and AI answers in search

For simple informational questions, Google increasingly shows a direct answer or AI summary, so fewer people click through. Pages that win clicks for these queries offer depth the summary can't: worked examples, checklists, local detail and a clear next step. Commercial and transactional queries are still where business pages earn most of their enquiries, so give those pages the most care.

A quick intent check before you write any page

  1. Search the exact query and note the page types on page one.
  2. Write down the intent in one sentence: "The searcher wants to..."
  3. Decide the page type and the answer for the first two lines.
  4. List the questions from "People also ask" and related searches to cover as headings or FAQs.
  5. Decide the next step link: which page should the reader visit after this one?
  6. Check you don't already have a page targeting the same intent.

FAQs

What is search intent in SEO?

Search intent is the reason behind a search: to learn, to find a specific site, to compare, to buy or hire, or to find something nearby. Google ranks pages whose type and content best match that reason.

What are the four types of search intent?

Informational, navigational, commercial investigation and transactional. Local intent, such as "near me" or city searches, is often treated as a fifth type for service businesses.

How do I find the search intent of a keyword?

Search it and study page one: the page types, formats, angles and SERP features such as map packs or shopping results show what Google thinks searchers want.

Can one page target more than one intent?

Only when Google's results already mix intents for that query. Otherwise, use separate pages for learning, comparing and hiring, and link them together.

Does search intent matter for local businesses?

Yes. Local searches with a city or "near me" show map results and service pages, so local businesses need clear location details, service pages and a complete Google Business Profile.

Why does my page rank for the wrong keywords?

Usually because the page's type or content matches a different intent from the one you wanted. Rework the page for its real intent, or create a new page for the intent you are missing.
