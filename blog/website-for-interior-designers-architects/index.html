Websites for Interior Designers and Architects: Portfolio-First Design, Project Pages, Fast Images and Enquiries That Qualify Clients

Portfolio-first websites for interior designers and architects: project pages, fast galleries, your process, qualifying enquiry forms and design partners.

Key takeaways
  • Make the project page the heart of the site: the brief, the site as found, key decisions, materials, your exact scope and real photos, captioned so visitors can match your work to their taste.
  • Large images can stay sharp and fast if they are exported at sensible sizes, served in modern formats and loaded only when needed.
  • Ask enquiry type, project stage, budget and timeline so you know what each lead needs before you call, and keep design partner and careers enquiries on separate forms.

An interior designer's or architect's website should be portfolio-first: a small number of strong projects, each with its own page that tells the story from brief to finished space, organised so visitors can find work like the project they have in mind. Around that portfolio, the site needs to explain your process and how fees work, load large images quickly, and ask enough in the enquiry form to show whether a lead fits your budget, scope and timeline before you spend an evening on a site visit.

I build this kind of site as part of WordPress website development: a custom design for your brand, mobile-first and fast, with forms and WhatsApp, SEO basics, and projects you can add yourself after launch. This guide covers what a design practice's site needs, using a real example from an interior studio.

A structure built around the portfolio

  • Projects: a grid of your best work, with categories and, once you have enough, filters.
  • Project pages: one per project, telling its story.
  • Services: what you offer, from design-only consultations to turnkey execution.
  • Process and fees: how a project runs and how you charge.
  • Studio: the people, their training and how you work.
  • Partners and careers: separate routes for collaborators and job seekers.
  • Journal: articles on questions clients ask.
  • Enquiry: a form that qualifies the project.

Curate hard. Eight to fifteen projects that show your range and standard say more than fifty thumbnails, and a weak project pulls down the strong ones next to it.

The project page, section by section

Clients hiring a designer want to see how you think, not only what the finished room looks like. A project page that does that:

Captions, categories and filters

Visitors browse portfolios by taste and by room as much as by project type. Studio Agama Interiors, a Hyderabad interior design and turnkey execution studio I built a site for, captions its homepage project images by style and by room, such as "The Minimalist: Modern Interior", "Boho Styles: Sleeping Space" and "New Kitchen: Dinner Space", so a homeowner can see quickly whether the studio's work suits their own home. Useful ways to organise:

  • By type: apartments, villas, offices, retail, hospitality, institutional
  • By room or space: kitchens, bedrooms, living rooms, workspaces
  • By style: minimal, contemporary, traditional, eclectic, in words your clients use
  • By status, for architects: completed, under construction, concept

Filters earn their place once you have more projects than fit comfortably on one screen; before that, clear captions do the job.

Large images that still load fast

Design portfolios are image-heavy by nature, and a slow gallery undersells the work it shows. The fixes are mostly in how images are prepared and served:

  1. Never upload camera originals. Export images at the size they will actually be displayed, with a larger version only for full-screen viewing.
  2. Serve modern formats such as WebP, with WordPress providing smaller sizes automatically for phones.
  3. Load the first image on each page straight away, and lazy-load the rest of the gallery as visitors scroll.
  4. Open full-resolution images in a lightbox only when someone taps them.
  5. Embed walkthrough videos from YouTube or Vimeo so they load only when played, rather than as autoplay backgrounds.

Done this way, images stay sharp on a large monitor and fast on a phone over mobile data; image optimisation for WordPress goes through sizes, formats and compression step by step.

Explain your process and how fees work

Clients worry about what happens after they say yes, and about cost surprises. A process page answers both:

  • The stages, such as first consultation, site measurement, concept and mood boards, 3D views, approval, estimate, execution and handover
  • How many design revisions are included, and what happens after that
  • What the client sees before work starts; Studio Agama explains that clients see product samples and detailed 3D renderings first
  • How you charge, whether a design fee, a percentage of project cost, a rate per area or turnkey pricing, without needing to publish figures
  • How payments are staged, and whether they can be spread out
  • What support continues after handover

Studio Agama's FAQ also answers the comparison questions buyers ask: the difference between modular and full home interiors, whether one room can be designed without redoing the whole home, and how a studio differs from contractors and individual designers. Architects registered with the Council of Architecture should check its current professional conduct rules, which restrict how architects promote their services, and keep the site factual.

Enquiry forms that qualify the project

A designer's time is the product, so a lead that turns out to be the wrong size, stage or budget is expensive. Studio Agama's "Your Design Enquiry" form asks, beyond name, email, phone and locality, for the type of enquiry (a turnkey home, luxury interiors, commercial or multi-functional spaces, or renovation and space planning), how the person heard about the studio, the project stage, and the budget and time frame. The team can see the type, scale and budget of a job before the first call. A form for your practice might ask:

  • Project type and scope: full home, a single room, office, retail
  • Property and location: apartment or villa, approximate size, locality
  • Stage: just bought, under construction, ready to move in, or renovating
  • Budget: ranges you choose, plus "not sure yet"
  • Timeline: a target move-in or opening date
  • Source: how they found you, so you know which channels work

That is a lot of questions for one screen, so on a phone a form in short stages, starting with the easy choices, keeps more people going; multi-step forms explains how to qualify leads without losing them.

Design partner, trade and careers enquiries

A practice receives more than client enquiries: designers who want to collaborate, contractors and vendors offering their services, and job seekers. Mixing them into the client form clutters your inbox and slows replies to the people who might hire you. Studio Agama has a separate "Become a Design Partner" page with its own form, asking for name, mobile number, email, project stage and a message, and its contact page lists a separate email address for careers. Give each audience its own route, and ask job seekers to send a portfolio link rather than large files.

Local search, social media and the journal

Clients search by service and city, such as "interior designer in Gachibowli" or "office interior design in Pune", so service pages and project captions should use those words naturally. A journal adds depth: Studio Agama publishes articles on residential design trends in Hyderabad, Vijayawada and Vizag and on common interior design mistakes in Hyderabad. Many clients first see your work on Instagram or Pinterest, so link posts back to the full project pages, where the enquiry form is; turning Instagram followers into website enquiries shows how.

If your current site shows projects from years ago, loads slowly or can't be updated without a developer, a website redesign can rebuild the portfolio while keeping the pages that already bring in enquiries.

FAQs

How many projects should an interior designer show on their website?

Usually eight to fifteen of your strongest projects, chosen to show your range. Add new ones and retire weaker ones over time rather than showing everything.

Should we show prices or budgets on our website?

Many practices explain how they charge without publishing figures. Asking for a budget range in the enquiry form, with a "not sure yet" option, helps you see which leads fit.

Can I add new projects myself after launch?

Yes. Projects can be added from the dashboard with a template for the story, images and captions, so every project page stays consistent.

Can we show renders as well as completed photos?

Yes, as long as they are labelled as renders or concepts, so visitors know which projects are built and which are designs.

How do we stop large images slowing the site?

Export images at the size they are displayed, serve modern formats, lazy-load galleries and open full-resolution versions only on request.
