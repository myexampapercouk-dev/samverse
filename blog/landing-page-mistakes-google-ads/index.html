10 Landing Page Mistakes That Waste Your Google Ads Budget, and How to Fix Each One: Message Match, Speed, Forms, Tracking and Ad Policy

Key takeaways
  • Most wasted ad spend comes from the page, not the ad: a headline that ignores the ad, a slow mobile load, a long form or tracking that counts the wrong thing.
  • Fix tracking first, because without it you cannot tell which change helped, then fix anything broken or against policy, then speed, message match, the form and proof.
  • Change one thing at a time, judge it on real enquiries rather than clicks, and keep a short log so you know what worked and what to try next.

The landing page mistakes that waste the most Google Ads budget are a headline that doesn't repeat what the ad promised, slow loading on phones, a layout that breaks on small screens, long or broken forms, missing or wrong conversion tracking, no proof, too many choices and content that runs into ad policy problems. Each has a practical fix, and most can be checked in an afternoon.

1. The headline doesn't repeat what the ad promised

Someone searches "same-day AC repair Kothrud", clicks an ad that says exactly that, and lands on a page headed "Welcome to our company". They have to work out whether they are in the right place, and many don't bother. This is message match, and it is the most common reason a page with decent traffic produces few enquiries. Sending ad clicks to your homepage is the extreme version: it talks about everything, so it matches nothing.

  • Use the ad's words in the headline: the same service, location and offer.
  • Keep promises consistent: if the ad says "same day" or "free inspection", the page says it too, with any conditions.
  • One page per different promise: ad groups selling different services, offers or areas each need their own version.
  • Read the search terms report: if the page doesn't answer what people typed, change the page or add negative keywords.

Google Ads also rates landing page experience as part of Quality Score, so a relevant page helps the ads as well as the visitors.

2. Slow loading on mobile data

Most ad clicks come from phones, often on patchy 4G, and a visitor who gives up while the page loads has already cost you the click. The usual culprits are large hero images, sliders, background videos, several font families, chat widgets and tracking scripts added over time. Compress and resize images for phones, replace sliders and video at the top with one strong image, use one or two fonts, and load chat widgets and maps only where they are needed. Then test on a mid-range Android phone on mobile data, not the office Wi-Fi. Website speed on Indian mobile networks explains how to test the way your customers browse.

3. A layout designed on a laptop

Pages are often approved on a big screen, then squeezed onto a phone, with the button three scrolls down and a pop-up covering half the screen.

  • The first phone screen should show the headline, one supporting line and a button.
  • A slim bar at the bottom of the screen with Call and WhatsApp keeps the next step one tap away.
  • Phone links need the country code (+91), and the phone field should open the number keypad.
  • Remove or delay pop-ups on mobile, and make any that remain easy to close.

4. A form that asks too much, or doesn't work

Every extra field is another reason to leave. For a first call, name and phone number are often enough; you can qualify on the call. If your team genuinely needs something first, such as the service or the city, ask one dropdown question rather than six text boxes.

The quieter problem is a form that looks fine but fails: emails landing in spam, a captcha that blocks real phones, or error messages nobody can read. Submit the form yourself from a phone, check the enquiry reaches the person who will call back, and send visitors to a proper thank-you page. Why every form needs a thank-you page covers what to put on it and how it helps tracking.

5. A weak or hidden call to action

"Submit" tells the visitor nothing. "Get my free quote", "Book a site visit" or "Send me the price list" say what happens next. Put the main button on the first screen and repeat it after each section.

Offer more than one route to the same enquiry. A form, a WhatsApp button with a pre-filled message that names the campaign, and tap-to-call all lead to the same result, so they aren't distractions. Some buyers prefer a call at their own time: on Sahni Power Solutions, a generator rental website I built, "Request a Call Back" stays in the header and each section ends with "Enquire Now". It is a full website rather than a campaign page, but the idea carries over.

6. Too many choices

A landing page should ask for one thing. A full menu, blog links, a second offer or social media icons give the visitor somewhere else to go, after you have paid for them to arrive. Remove the main menu, keep the footer to your contact details and privacy policy, and cut any section that doesn't help the visitor decide. If you sell two different services, build two pages.

7. No proof near the decision

Visitors from an ad usually don't know you. Before sharing a phone number, they want signs that you are real and good at the job: genuine reviews or testimonials used with permission, photos of your actual work and team rather than stock images, registrations and memberships that genuinely apply, and client logos only where you have permission. Place proof beside the form and the button, not on a separate page, and never invent counters such as "500+ happy clients". The website trust signals checklist covers everything a cautious visitor looks for.

8. Tracking that is missing, or counts the wrong thing

Without conversion tracking you see clicks but not enquiries, and Google's automated bidding learns from whatever you call a conversion. Wrong tracking is worse than none, because it steers spend towards the wrong keywords.

  • Count successful submissions, through the thank-you page or the form's success event, not clicks on the submit button.
  • Count each enquiry once: the Google Ads tag or a GA4 import as the primary conversion for an action, not both.
  • Treat WhatsApp and call clicks as intent, and compare them with the chats and calls you actually receive.
  • Test before you spend with a real enquiry, and check it appears in GA4 and Google Ads.

9. Content that runs into ad policy problems

Google Ads policies apply to the page you send people to, not just the ad. The details change, so check Google's current advertising policies before launching, but at the time of writing these are common landing page causes of disapproved or limited ads:

  • A destination that doesn't work: errors, an "under construction" page, or a redirect somewhere unexpected.
  • Missing business information: visitors should see who you are, how to contact you and a privacy policy covering form data.
  • Claims you can't back up: "guaranteed results" or "No. 1 in Pune" can be treated as misleading, by ad platforms and under consumer protection rules.
  • Restricted categories: healthcare, financial services and some other sectors can have extra restrictions or verification steps, depending on the country. Property projects in India generally need to show registration details in ads; check your state's current rules.

If an ad is disapproved, Google Ads names the policy involved, which tells you what to fix. Meta has its own policies if you advertise on Facebook or Instagram.

10. Never testing, or changing everything at once

Some pages are never touched after launch. Others get a new headline, photos, form and offer in the same week, so when results move nobody knows why. Change one thing at a time, judge it on enquiries rather than clicks, and with modest budgets test big differences such as the offer or the headline, not button colours. A/B testing landing pages explains how to run a fair test with the traffic you have.

Sometimes the page isn't the problem: if enquiries arrive but few become customers, check how fast someone replies. Setting a sensible Google Ads budget shows how to work out what an enquiry is worth.

Fix them in this order

  1. Tracking, so every real enquiry is counted once and you can judge the other changes.
  2. Anything broken or against policy: failing forms, missing contact details, claims you can't support.
  3. Speed and the mobile layout, which affect every visitor.
  4. Message match, with one version per distinct promise in your ads.
  5. The form, the call to action and proof.
  6. Then test, one change at a time, with a short log of what changed and when.

If you do need more detail before calling, a short multi-step form can collect it without a wall of fields; see multi-step forms for lead qualification.

FAQs

Why are my Google Ads getting clicks but no leads?

Usually because the page doesn't match the ad, loads slowly on phones, asks for too much or has a form that doesn't deliver. Check tracking first, then test the page on your own phone on mobile data, then compare the headline with the ad and the search terms report.

What is a good conversion rate for a landing page?

There is no single figure worth chasing, because it depends on the industry, offer, price and how ready visitors are to buy. Compare a page with its own past results, and judge the campaign on cost per real enquiry against what an enquiry is worth to you.

Does a slow landing page cost more on Google Ads?

It can. Landing page experience is one of the factors in Quality Score at the time of writing, and a slow page also loses visitors you have already paid for. Google doesn't publish exact weightings, so treat speed as something that helps both the ad and the enquiry rate.

Should a landing page have a menu?

Usually not, because a menu gives visitors other places to go. Keep a short footer with contact details and the privacy policy, and offer the form, WhatsApp and call as routes to the same enquiry.

Can my landing page get my ads disapproved?

Yes. A page that doesn't load, lacks business information, makes claims you can't support or falls into a restricted category without meeting its requirements can lead to disapproved or limited ads. Google Ads names the policy involved, so start there.
