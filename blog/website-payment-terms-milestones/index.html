Fair Payment Terms for a Website Project: Advances, Milestones, GST Invoices, Late Payments, Refunds and Red Flags

Fair website payment terms: the advance, milestones tied to deliverables, GST invoices, late payments, refunds, cancellations and red flags to avoid.

Key takeaways
  • Pay a modest advance, then tie every later payment to something you can see, such as an approved design or a working staging site.
  • Whether you hire a freelancer or an agency, put the payment schedule and what each instalment buys in writing.
  • Instalments after launch are possible on some projects, but expect admin access or ownership transfer to be tied to the final payment.

Fair payment terms for a website project start with a modest advance to reserve the developer's time, then tie every later payment to something you can see and check, such as an approved design, a working site on a staging link or the launch itself. They also say how changes are priced, what happens when either side is late, how GST and invoices are handled, and what is refunded if the project stops early. Agree all of this in writing before any money changes hands.

For WordPress website development with me, the terms are part of the written scope: a fixed price, the pages and features, the revision rounds and what's excluded, agreed before work starts. Projects begin with an advance, with the balance due before launch, and monthly plans are billed monthly. You can see how that works in practice on the hire a WordPress developer page.

This guide applies whoever you hire. Payment terms are one part of a wider agreement, which the website design contract checklist covers, and they depend on how the project is priced; fixed price vs hourly billing explains the alternatives. It's practical guidance, not legal or tax advice.

Common payment structures for website projects

How you pay matters as much as how much. These are the structures you're most likely to be offered in India:

StructureHow it worksSuitsWatch out for
Full payment up frontEverything paid before work startsSmall, clearly defined fixes, or a fixed batch of tasksA warning sign for a new website, because you've paid before seeing anything
Advance and balanceAn advance to start, the rest before or at launchSmall business sites and landing pagesA long gap with no check-in between the two payments
Advance and milestonesAn advance, then a payment as each stage is approvedLarger sites, stores, redesigns and migrationsMilestones defined by dates instead of deliverables
Hourly or time billedInvoiced weekly or monthly for hours workedOpen-ended work, ongoing changes and investigationsNo estimate or cap, which leads to surprise bills
Monthly plan or retainerA fixed amount each month for agreed servicesMaintenance, support and regular content updatesUnclear limits on what the plan includes

For a fixed-price website, an advance followed by milestone payments is the most common pattern, and the rest of this guide assumes it.

The advance: what it's for

Asking for an advance is reasonable. It confirms you're committed, reserves time in the developer's schedule and covers early work such as planning the pages and designing the first layouts. In general terms:

  • It should be a portion of the total, not most of it. The bigger the project, the more sense it makes to spread payments across stages rather than front-load them.
  • Work should start when it's received, with the start date or first milestone stated in the proposal.
  • It should be acknowledged with an invoice or receipt, whether you pay by bank transfer or UPI.
  • The proposal should say what happens to it if the project is cancelled before much work is done (see the refunds section below).

Being asked for the full amount before anything is designed, on a new website, is a warning sign. So is a developer who starts work with no advance and no written scope, because that usually means nobody has agreed what "finished" looks like.

Tie milestones to deliverables, not dates

Each milestone payment should fall due when something specific is delivered and approved, not on a calendar date. Dates slip on both sides; a deliverable is something you can check:

StageWhat you should be able to checkPayment
AgreementA written scope, timeline and fixed priceAdvance
Design approvedHomepage and key page designs, revised within the agreed roundsMilestone payment
Build ready for reviewA working site on a staging link you can test on your own phoneMilestone payment
Approved for launchThe agreed scope complete on staging, with forms and key features testedBalance
Launch and handoverThe live site, your logins, a backup and a walkthroughNothing further, other than agreed extras

Smaller projects can merge stages; a five-page site may have just an advance and a balance. What matters is that each milestone has a definition of "done" that both sides agree before work starts.

Write a definition of done

"Design stage complete" can mean very different things. Something like "homepage and service page designs approved in writing after up to two rounds of revisions" leaves no room for argument. Approvals by email or WhatsApp are fine for most small projects, as long as they're written down.

Online stores, redesigns and migrations

For a WooCommerce store, a sensible milestone is the store working on staging with test orders through the gateway in test mode. Don't tie a payment to the gateway's approval itself, because KYC checks are in the provider's hands. For a redesign or migration, the redirect map for old URLs can be its own deliverable, checked before launch.

Balance before launch or after?

Both are common. Paying the balance before launch, once you've approved the finished site on staging, protects the developer from a site going live and the last invoice going unpaid. Paying on launch protects the client from paying for something that isn't live yet. Either can be fair if:

  • You've reviewed and approved the complete site on staging before the final payment is due.
  • The domain and hosting are registered in your name from the start, so the site can never be held back over a dispute.
  • A short bug-fix period after launch covers anything that doesn't work as agreed, without a new invoice.
  • Handover follows promptly: administrator access, licences, a backup and documentation. The website handover checklist lists what to expect.

Scope changes and extra work

New ideas once you see the site taking shape are normal; friction comes from changes added without an agreed price. A fair process:

  1. You request the change in writing.
  2. The developer confirms whether it's within the agreed scope and, if not, quotes the price and any effect on the timeline.
  3. You approve the quote before the work starts.
  4. The extra amount is added to the next milestone or invoiced separately.

Tweaks within the agreed revision rounds shouldn't cost extra. New pages, new features or a change of direction usually do, while fixing something that doesn't work as agreed is part of the original job. Bug or change request? explains where the line usually sits.

Delays, paused projects and late payments

Projects most often stall while waiting for content, feedback or access to hosting. Agree in advance what happens then, for example that a milestone becomes payable once the developer's part is complete, or that a project paused without a response for an agreed period is rescheduled.

The same applies to late payments. Common terms include a reminder after the due date, work pausing while a payment is overdue, and the schedule moving by the length of the delay. Interest or late fees only apply if the agreement says so, and the rules on them depend on the law that applies, so ask a lawyer before relying on them. Equally, if the developer keeps missing agreed dates without good reason, you should be able to raise it in writing and, in serious cases, end the project under the cancellation terms.

GST, invoices and paying from abroad

  • GST: ask whether the quote includes GST or whether it will be added, which depends on whether the developer is registered. Your CA can tell you whether you can claim it back.
  • Invoices: get one for every payment, with the business names, the amount, the tax if any and what the payment covers.
  • TDS: if your business deducts tax at source on payments to contractors, tell the developer before the first invoice, and check with your CA whether it applies.
  • Third-party costs: domain, hosting, premium themes and plugins are best paid directly to the providers, in your name, rather than bundled into the developer's invoice.
  • Overseas clients: agree the currency, who pays bank or transfer charges, and how the invoice will be issued. Tax treatment of services billed abroad differs, so that's a question for the developer's CA.

Refunds and cancellations

Sometimes a project ends early, because budgets or priorities change or the working relationship breaks down. Fair terms usually say:

  • Either side gives written notice before ending the agreement.
  • You pay for work completed up to that point, measured against the milestones.
  • If the advance covered work that hasn't been done, an agreed part of it is refunded.
  • You receive the work you've paid for, such as approved designs, files and access to anything already built.

Without written terms, these situations quickly turn into arguments. If the amounts are significant, ask a lawyer to review the agreement.

Retainers and monthly plans

Ongoing work, such as maintenance, support and regular content changes, is usually billed monthly rather than by milestone. Check what the fee includes, how bigger jobs are quoted and how much notice you need to give to stop. My WordPress maintenance plans, for example, are month to month, with small text, image and price updates included and bigger work quoted separately.

A payment schedule you can adapt

A short, plain schedule in the proposal prevents most disputes. For a small business site:

PAYMENT SCHEDULE (example)
Total: fixed price as quoted, plus GST if applicable
1. Advance: due on accepting the proposal; work starts on receipt
2. Design milestone: due when the key page designs are approved in writing
3. Balance: due when the complete site is approved on staging, before launch
Third-party costs: domain, hosting and premium licences paid by the
client directly, in the client's name
Changes outside scope: quoted in writing, approved before work starts,
billed with the next payment
Invoices: issued for every payment
Cancellation: written notice; client pays for work completed to date;
any unused part of the advance refunded as agreed

Red flags in payment terms

  • Full payment demanded up front for a new website.
  • A price with no list of pages, features or revision rounds to measure it against.
  • Milestones tied to dates, with no deliverable to check.
  • No mention of whose name the domain, hosting and licences will be in; the website ownership checklist lists each account.
  • Payments to a personal account with no invoice or receipt.
  • No cancellation terms at all.

Paying a freelancer versus an agency

The same principles apply to both, but the paperwork differs. An agency usually has a standard contract and payment schedule; read it for the advance, how milestones are approved and what happens to work in progress if you cancel. A freelancer may work from a shorter proposal, so make sure the payment schedule, the deliverable for each instalment and the ownership of accounts are written there, not only agreed on a call. With either, pay to a business account in the name on the invoice, keep every invoice and receipt, and avoid paying the full amount upfront, however small the project feels.

FAQs

How much advance should I pay for a website?

A portion of the total, enough to reserve the developer's time and cover the early planning and design, not most of the price. Larger projects should spread the rest across milestones you can check.

Is it normal to pay the balance before the website goes live?

Yes, it's common, as long as you've reviewed and approved the finished site on staging first and the domain and hosting are already in your name. A bug-fix period after launch covers anything that doesn't work as agreed.

Do I have to pay GST on website development?

It depends on whether the developer is GST-registered, so ask whether it's included in the quote or added on top. Your CA can tell you whether your business can claim it back.

What if I don't like the design after paying the advance?

That's what revision rounds are for, so use them with clear, consolidated feedback. If you decide to stop altogether, the cancellation terms decide what you pay for work done and what, if anything, is refunded.

Should hosting and the domain be part of the developer's invoice?

It's better to pay for them directly, in your own name. The developer can set them up, but you keep control whatever happens.

Can I pay a website developer in instalments after launch?

Some developers agree to it, usually for larger projects with a clear schedule in the contract. Expect the site, admin access or ownership transfer to be tied to the final instalment, and get the dates and amounts in writing before work starts.
