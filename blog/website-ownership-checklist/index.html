Website Ownership Checklist: Every Account Your Business Should Own, How to Check Who Controls It, and What to Give Your Developer

Key takeaways
  • Ownership means the account is in your business name on an email you control; access is what you grant developers through their own logins.
  • Use role addresses such as admin@ as account owners so a departing employee does not take the domain or Google accounts with them.
  • Keep a dated ownership record of every account, its owner and who has access, and review it whenever staff or developers change.

Your business should own every account its website depends on: the domain registrar, DNS, hosting, the WordPress administrator account, business email, Google Search Console, Analytics and Business Profile, any premium theme or plugin licences, and the payment gateway if you take payments. Each should be registered in your business name, use an email address you control, and let you add or remove other people. Developers and agencies should work through their own user accounts, never as the owner.

When you hire me as your WordPress developer, the domain and hosting should be registered in your name, you get your own administrator login, and any temporary access I used can be removed at the end. If the accounts currently sit in someone else's name, I can help you move them into yours, and a site on a previous developer's server can be moved to hosting you own.

This checklist is for confirming ownership before there's a problem. If you're already locked out, regaining control from a previous developer covers recovery, step by step.

Ownership vs access: the difference that matters

Many business owners believe they own their website because they have a password. A password is access. Ownership means three things:

  • The account is registered to your business, with your business email as the login and recovery address.
  • You pay the bill, or at least the invoices are addressed to you and renewals don't depend on someone else's card.
  • You control who else gets in. You can add a developer, change their role and remove them without asking anyone.

If a developer holds the owner account and gave you a login, you have access only while that relationship lasts, which is fine until the day it ends.

The accounts people forget

These smaller services cause just as much trouble in the wrong hands:

  • Google Tag Manager, if your tracking runs through it. You should be an Administrator of the account, not only a user of the container.
  • Meta business tools, called a business portfolio at the time of writing: your Facebook page, Instagram account, ad account and Pixel should belong to your portfolio, with your agency added as a partner.
  • Google Ads, with your own account and billing, linked to your agency's manager account if they run campaigns.
  • The email sending service behind your contact forms, if the site uses one.
  • Google Cloud, if the site uses a Maps or other Google API key, since the key and its billing sit in someone's account.
  • Backup storage, such as a cloud drive that a backup plugin sends copies to.
  • Uptime monitoring, CDN and security services, plus any WhatsApp Business API, CRM or booking tool connected to the site.
  • Brand and design files: logo files in vector format, the Figma or design files, fonts and the licences for any stock photos used.

How to check who owns each account, in about an hour

  1. Search your inbox for "renewal", "invoice" and "receipt" with the names of your registrar and host. Whoever receives those emails is usually the account holder.
  2. Look up the domain to find the registrar, then log in there with your business email. If the domain isn't in your account, it's in someone else's.
  3. Find where DNS lives with a nameserver lookup. If the nameservers point to Cloudflare or a host you don't have a login for, someone else controls your DNS.
  4. Check WordPress users. Under Users, filter by Administrator. You should see your own account, and you should recognise every other one. Old developers who still have Administrator access are a security risk as well as an ownership one; auditing your WordPress user accounts walks through the clean-up.
  5. Open each Google tool with your business Google account and note your role. "Full user" in Search Console or "Viewer" in Analytics means someone else is in charge.
  6. Check licences and payment accounts. In WordPress, open each premium plugin's licence screen and note the account email. In the payment gateway, check the business name and settlement bank account.

Write the results down as you go. A row you can't complete is your to-do list.

Where ownership usually goes wrong

  • The domain was bought in the developer's reseller account. The registrar then treats the developer as its customer. Ask for it to be moved into an account in your name; transferring a domain explains the codes and locks.
  • The site sits on the developer's shared hosting alongside their other clients. If they stop paying, or fall out with you, the site goes with it.
  • Premium licences are on the developer's account. When their licence lapses, updates stop, and security fixes stop with them. Licences often can't be transferred, so buying one in your name is usually simpler.
  • Analytics was set up in an agency's Google account. Your history stays with them unless they add you as an Administrator.
  • An SEO agency created your Business Profile and is the primary owner of your Maps listing and its reviews.
  • The payment gateway keys belong to a test account the developer created, or the gateway was opened under their details. Payments must settle to your business, under your own KYC.
  • Mailboxes sit inside the developer's Google Workspace or Zoho account, so your business email depends on their subscription.

Most of these began as shortcuts, not bad intent, and they're far easier to fix while the relationship is good.

Make ownership part of the project from day one

Ownership is easiest to get right before work starts. Ask for these in writing:

  • The domain, hosting and premium licences are bought in your business name, with your email, even if the developer sets them up.
  • You own the website, its content and the design once it's paid for, in whatever form your agreement sets out. The website contract checklist covers how to word this, and a lawyer can review anything important.
  • At the end you receive every login, a full backup and documentation, using the website handover checklist.

Keep an ownership record

Switch on two-factor authentication wherever it's offered, make sure recovery emails and phone numbers belong to the business rather than a former employee, and put every renewal date in a shared calendar. The domain, hosting, email and licence renewals checklist helps you track them. Review the record once a year, and whenever a developer, agency or staff member leaves.

What to do when a staff member who managed the website leaves

Ownership problems often start inside the business, not with a developer. When the person who set up the domain, email or Google accounts leaves, check the ownership record the same week: confirm each account's owner is a role address such as admin@ rather than their personal email, remove their user accounts, change shared passwords and move two-step verification to a company phone.

FAQs

Is it normal for a developer to register my domain for me?

It's common, and it's fine if they register it in your name, with your email, in an account you can log in to. The problem is a domain registered in the developer's own name or reseller account, because the registrar then treats them as the owner.

Can I let my developer keep the hosting if it's part of their package?

It can work, especially with a maintenance plan, but agree in writing that you can have a full backup or a move to your own hosting at any time. Owning the hosting yourself is simpler.

Should my developer still have Administrator access after launch?

Only if they're maintaining the site. Otherwise, change their account to Editor or delete it, attributing any content to your account, and invite them back when you need them.

What is the difference between an Owner and a Full user in Search Console?

An Owner can add and remove users and manage the property's settings. A Full user can see all the data and take most actions, but can't manage other users. Your business should be an Owner; developers and agencies only need Full access.

Who owns the design and content of my website?

It depends on your agreement, which is why it should be written down before work starts. Many agreements transfer ownership once the final payment is made, while the developer may keep the right to show the work in a portfolio. This isn't legal advice; ask a lawyer to check anything important.

What should I do if the domain is registered in a former employee's name?

Ask them, while you are still in touch, to change the registrant details to the business or start a transfer to your own registrar account. If they cannot be reached, contact the registrar with proof of business ownership and follow its dispute or change-of-registrant process.
