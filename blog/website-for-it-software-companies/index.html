Websites for IT and Software Companies: Services or Products, Case Studies You Can Publish, Tech Stack Pages, the Offshore Model, RFP Forms and Careers

How IT and software company websites win leads: clear positioning, permitted case studies, stack pages, RFP forms and an honest offshore model.

Key takeaways
  • Say in one sentence who you help and with what, and organise the site around that rather than a long list of technologies.
  • Case studies need client permission or careful anonymising, and they work best when they show the problem, your team's role and how the engagement ran.
  • Overseas buyers want the delivery model explained plainly: engagement types, overlap hours, communication, security and who owns the code.

An IT or software company website turns visits into leads when a buyer can tell, within a minute, what you build, for whom, how you work with clients in other cities or countries, and how to start a conversation. Most sites in this sector fail on the first point: they list every technology and service, and end up saying very little about what the company is actually good at.

For a newer firm, the startup website service covers the essentials: product and service pages, demo or enquiry forms, analytics and CRM connections. For an established company with a larger site, a custom WordPress website can carry services, case studies, careers and a blog that your marketing team edits without a developer.

Services company, product company, or both?

The structure of the site follows from how you earn money. Mixing the two stories on one homepage confuses both audiences.

Positioning that a buyer can repeat

A useful test: could a visitor explain to a colleague what you do after reading your homepage headline? Compare two lines:

"Innovative digital solutions for forward-thinking enterprises."

"We build and support order management and warehouse software for distributors in India and the Gulf."

The second one excludes people, and that is the point. Buyers who fit recognise themselves; buyers who do not leave early, which saves your sales team a call. If you genuinely serve several markets, give each its own industry page rather than widening the headline until it means nothing.

Service pages built around problems

A page titled "Mobile App Development" with a list of frameworks is the norm. A more useful page explains the situations you are good at: rebuilding an ageing app, launching a first version for a funded startup, or taking over code from another vendor. For each service line, cover:

  • The kinds of clients and projects it suits, and the ones it does not
  • How a typical engagement runs, from discovery to support
  • What the client needs to provide, and what your team handles
  • Related case studies, linked directly
  • A short FAQ drawn from real sales calls

The Third Eye Social site, which I built for an AI automation agency, takes this route: it explains a complex offer through separate use-case sections for each type of agent, rather than one long list.

Case studies you are allowed to publish

Many IT contracts include confidentiality clauses, and clients may not want their name or system shown. Before writing a case study:

  1. Check the contract and ask the client in writing whether they can be named, and whether their logo can appear.
  2. If not, anonymise: describe the sector, size and region instead of the name, and remove screenshots that reveal the interface or data.
  3. Describe your team's actual role, especially if you were one of several vendors.
  4. Use only results the client has agreed to share and that you can support; otherwise describe what was delivered instead.
  5. Let the client review the final draft.

A good structure is the starting situation, the constraints, what your team did, how the engagement was run, and what the client has now. Writing case studies for a business website goes into the format.

Tech stack pages: when they help

Pages for individual technologies, such as a page for React development or one for Laravel, can attract buyers who already know what they want built with. They help when you have real depth in that stack and can say something specific: versions you support, typical architectures, how you handle upgrades, and projects that used it. They hurt when they are thin pages repeated for twenty technologies with the name swapped, which looks like filler to both readers and search engines. Five strong stack pages are better than twenty weak ones.

Explaining the offshore model honestly

Clients in other countries often worry about the same things: communication, time zones, quality and control. A page on how you work with overseas clients can address them directly:

  • Engagement models: fixed scope, time and materials, or a dedicated team, with when each one fits
  • Overlap hours: the hours your team is available in the client's working day, for the regions you serve
  • Communication: who the client's point of contact is, how often they hear from you, and which tools you use
  • Process: how work is planned, reviewed and tested, and how changes in scope are handled
  • Ownership: who owns the code and accounts, and how handover works if the engagement ends
  • Contracts and payments: the currencies you invoice in and the governing terms you usually work under, stated generally

The article on offshore developers and time zones looks at scheduling from the client's point of view, and is a useful reference for how buyers think about it.

RFP and project enquiry forms

A general contact form is not enough for project enquiries. A short structured form helps your sales team reply with something useful:

  • Company name, country and work email
  • Type of project: new build, takeover of existing code, ongoing team, consulting
  • Rough timeline and budget band, optional
  • An upload field for an RFP or requirements document, with file-type and size limits
  • A checkbox asking whether they want an NDA before sharing details

Route enquiries to sales immediately and connect the form to your CRM. RFQ forms for B2B websites covers the design of these forms, and a confirmation page naming the person who will respond keeps the lead warm.

Trust signals, only the real ones

Enterprise buyers look for security and quality evidence. Only show what you hold, and keep it current:

  • Management system certifications, such as ISO standards for quality or information security, with the certifying body and scope
  • Partner status with cloud or software vendors, at the level you currently hold
  • A security page describing access control, data handling, code review and how client data is protected
  • Client logos only with permission

An expired badge or a partner tier you no longer hold does more harm than leaving it out. The website trust signals checklist lists the wider set.

Careers pages that also protect you

For many IT companies the careers section gets as much traffic as the services pages. Publish current openings with location, work mode, experience range and a clear application route, and remove filled roles. Add a short notice that you never ask candidates for money during hiring and only send offers from your company email domain. Fake job offers using real company names are a known problem, and the notice gives candidates something to check against. A section on how you hire, such as the interview stages and how long the process tends to take, improves the quality of applicants.

Content that earns B2B visits

Technical articles written by your engineers, on problems your clients face, tend to attract better visitors than general news posts. Share them on LinkedIn, where many B2B buyers spend time; turning LinkedIn activity into website visits explains how.

FAQs

Should an IT services company list every technology it uses?

List them on a single stack page if you like, but give separate pages only to the technologies where you have real depth and projects to show.

How do we publish case studies under NDA?

Ask the client first. If naming them is not allowed, describe the sector, scale and region, remove identifying screenshots and let the client review the draft.

Should we show prices on an IT services website?

Most custom work cannot be priced upfront, but you can describe engagement models and what affects cost, so buyers know what to expect before a call.

Do we need separate pages for overseas clients?

A page explaining how you work with overseas clients helps. Separate pages per country make sense only if your offer, contracts or contact details differ by market.

Can the RFP form accept documents?

Yes, with file-type and size limits and a secure link in the notification email. Offer an NDA option for clients who want one before sharing details.
