Portfolio Websites for Freelancers, Photographers and Creatives: What to Include and How to Win Clients

Key takeaways
  • A portfolio website you own is where serious clients check you out: show your best 6–12 pieces, explain your thinking in case studies and make the next step obvious.
  • Say who you work with and what you offer, add an about page with a real photo and real testimonials, and ask for project type, budget and timeline on the form.
  • Keep image-heavy portfolios fast with resized WebP images and lazy loading, and target your niche and location so the right clients find you on Google.

A good portfolio website shows a small selection of your best work, explains how you approached it, says clearly who you work with and what you offer, and makes it easy to get in touch. For freelancers, photographers, designers and other creatives, it's the one place online you control completely, and usually where a serious client goes after seeing your work on Instagram, Behance or LinkedIn.

Below is what to include, how to curate and present your work, the extra needs of different creative fields, and how to get found. I build portfolio and personal brand sites on WordPress; see WordPress website development, or bring your own design with Figma to WordPress.

Do you need a portfolio website if you're on Behance or Instagram?

Platforms are good for being discovered. A website is better for being hired:

Use both: post work on platforms, and link every profile back to your site, where the enquiry happens.

Curate ruthlessly

  • Show your best 6 to 12 pieces, not everything you've ever made. Weak work drags strong work down.
  • Feature the work you want more of. If you want branding projects, lead with branding, even if most of your income today comes from social media posts.
  • Group work by type, such as branding, packaging, weddings, product photography or web design, so visitors find what's relevant to them.
  • Refresh it regularly. Swap older pieces out when better ones come along, and remove work that no longer represents you.

Show your thinking with case studies

Clients hire people who solve problems, not just people who make nice things. For your three or four key projects, write a short case study:

  1. The client and the brief: who they are and what they needed.
  2. Constraints: budget, deadline, brand guidelines and audience.
  3. Your approach: the options you explored and why you chose one.
  4. The result: the final work, and what changed for the client, in their words where possible.

My own case studies follow a similar pattern: the client, their goals, what was built and the tools used. See how to write case studies for a simple structure, and only share client names and results you have permission to use.

Make it clear what you offer

  • A services section explaining what you do, who it's for and the steps of a project.
  • Pricing signals, such as "projects usually start from" or package options, which filter out poor-fit enquiries before they reach you.
  • Availability and typical turnaround, so clients with a deadline know whether to ask.
  • Who you work with: industries, locations, or agencies and studios if you also take white-label work.

Writers and video creators: see websites for authors and content creators.

Build trust

  • An about page with a real photo of you and a short story of how you got here
  • Client logos and named testimonials, used with permission
  • Links to your LinkedIn, Behance, Dribbble or Instagram profiles
  • Your location or the regions you serve, and a professional email address on your own domain

Make contacting you easy

  • A short enquiry form asking for a name, contact details, project type, budget range and timeline
  • WhatsApp and email alongside the form, since many clients would rather message
  • A clear call to action at the end of every project and case study page
  • A reply time you can keep, such as one working day

Keep an image-heavy portfolio fast

Portfolios are image-heavy by nature, and a gallery that crawls on a phone undoes the work you're showing. The essentials:

  • Resize images to the largest size they're displayed at, rather than uploading straight from the camera.
  • Compress them and serve WebP, using an image optimisation plugin if your site runs on WordPress.
  • Lazy-load images further down the page, but not the first image visitors see.
  • Embed videos from YouTube or Vimeo, ideally showing a thumbnail until someone clicks play.
  • Choose decent hosting; the cheapest shared plans struggle with large galleries.

See image optimization for WordPress for the details.

Protecting your work

Disabling right-click doesn't stop anyone determined, and it annoys genuine visitors. Upload images at web size rather than full resolution, add a subtle watermark if your field expects one, and keep your originals safe. For client work under an NDA, show it only with permission, or describe the project without naming the client or showing confidential material.

Get found by the right clients

  • Target your niche and location in titles and headings: "wedding photographer in Jaipur" or "Shopify designer for fashion brands" rather than "creative portfolio".
  • Give each case study its own page with a descriptive title, so it can rank for the kind of project it shows.
  • Name and describe images properly, with descriptive file names and alt text; see the image SEO guide.
  • Set up a Google Business Profile if you serve clients locally, as most photographers and interior designers do.
  • Write occasionally about projects, your process or questions clients often ask.
  • Ask clients to credit or link to you where it's appropriate.

Which platform to build it on

WordPress suits most freelancers who want to grow the site over time: portfolio themes and Elementor give you full control of the layout, you can add a blog, client galleries or a shop later, and you own everything. Hosted builders such as Squarespace or Wix are quicker to start with, but give you less control and are harder to move away from. My own site is a static, custom-coded build because I'm a developer and publish a lot of pages; for most creatives, that's more work than it's worth, because every update needs code.

A launch checklist for your portfolio

  • Your best 6–12 pieces, grouped by type, with three or four written up as case studies
  • A headline that says what you do and for whom
  • Services, about and contact pages
  • An enquiry form, WhatsApp and email, tested on your phone
  • Images resized and compressed, and key pages tested on mobile data
  • Your own domain and a professional email address
  • Links to and from your social and portfolio profiles

FAQs

Do freelancers need a portfolio website?

If clients hire you for the quality of your work, yes. Platforms help people discover you, but a website you own lets you present your best work your way, explain your services and take enquiries without competitors shown alongside you.

How many projects should a portfolio include?

Usually 6 to 12 of your best pieces, grouped by type, with three or four written up as case studies. A few strong projects make a better impression than dozens of mixed ones.

What should a creative portfolio website include?

A clear headline, a curated work section, case studies, a services page, an about page with a real photo, testimonials and a contact page with a short form, WhatsApp and email.

Is WordPress good for a portfolio website?

Yes, especially if you want to add a blog, client galleries or a shop later. Portfolio themes and page builders like Elementor give you control of the layout, and you own the site and its content outright.

Can I show client work I did under an NDA?

Only with the client's permission. Otherwise, describe the type of project and your role without naming the client or showing confidential material, or create a similar self-initiated piece that shows the same skill.

How much does a portfolio website cost?

It depends on the number of pages, whether the design is custom, and features such as galleries or a shop. A portfolio is usually similar in size to a small business site, which my WordPress website cost guide puts at ₹15,000 to ₹40,000 in India for a professional build.
