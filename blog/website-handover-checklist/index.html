Website Handover Checklist: What You Should Receive When Your Website Project Ends, From Logins and Backups to Documentation, Licences and Training

Key takeaways
  • A proper handover gives you working access to every account, your own Administrator login, a launch backup you control, documentation and training.
  • Log in to each account yourself, change shared passwords and confirm every licence is in your business name before you sign off.
  • Agree the handover list and support terms in writing before work starts, so launch day is a checklist rather than a negotiation.

At the end of a website project you should receive working access to every account the site depends on, your own WordPress Administrator login, a full backup stored somewhere you control, a short handover document, training on everyday edits, every licence in your business name, the design and source files, and analytics connected to your own Google account. Check each item yourself before you sign off. What you receive now decides how easily you can run, update and, if needed, move your website for years.

When you hire me as your WordPress developer, the handover is part of the job: a walkthrough on a video call, your logins confirmed, a full backup taken at launch, and any temporary access I used removed unless you keep me on for maintenance. This checklist is written for any project, though, whoever builds your site.

Handover is different from making sure accounts are registered in your name in the first place; for that, see the website ownership checklist. Handover is the moment you confirm you've actually received everything.

Agree the handover before work starts

The easiest time to agree what you'll receive is before the project begins. Add a short handover list to your quote or contract, alongside payment terms and ownership; the website design contract checklist covers what else to agree. Then, on handover day, you're ticking off a list rather than negotiating.

Accounts and logins

You should receive, or already hold, working access to everything the site depends on:

  • Domain registrar and DNS, including Cloudflare or a similar service if used
  • The hosting control panel, which gives SFTP and database access
  • Business email admin
  • Payment gateway, courier, CRM, booking and email marketing accounts connected to the site
  • Any service used to send website emails (SMTP) or to block spam on forms

Credentials should be shared securely, ideally through a password manager, not pasted into a WhatsApp chat or email. Log in to each one yourself before you sign off, then change any password that was shared.

WordPress admin users

  • Your own Administrator account, with your business email, not a shared "admin" login.
  • The developer's own account, separate from yours. If they're maintaining the site, it stays; if not, change it to a lower role or delete it, attributing their content to your account.
  • Staff accounts with the right roles, usually Editor for people who update content.
  • No unknown users. Remove test accounts and anyone you don't recognise.
  • Two-factor authentication on every Administrator account, where the site supports it.

An activity log makes a handover easier to audit, because it shows which accounts made which changes. See the WordPress activity log guide for what to record.

Backups

  • A full backup, files and database, taken at launch and stored somewhere you control, not only on the same server
  • Automatic backups scheduled, with the frequency and retention written down
  • At least one test restore done, so you know the backups work
  • Instructions for restoring, or the name of whoever will do it

The WordPress backup and restore guide explains how to check each of these.

Documentation

A short handover document saves hours for you and for any developer who works on the site later. It doesn't need to be long. Something like this is enough:

WEBSITE HANDOVER NOTE: example.com
Built with:        WordPress, theme, child theme (yes/no), Elementor or block editor
Plugins:           name, what it does, free or licensed, whose licence
Custom code:       where it lives and what it does
Forms:             each form, who receives the emails, where entries are stored
Integrations:      payments, WhatsApp, CRM, booking, and the account each uses
Backups:           tool, schedule, where copies are stored, how to restore
Caching and CDN:   what is used, how to clear it
Scheduled tasks:   reminders, imports, feeds
Do not change:     settings that would break something
Known limitations: anything left for later
Support:           who to contact, and on what terms

Passwords and API keys don't belong in this document. Keep them in your password manager instead.

If a licence stays in your developer's name, get that in writing, along with what happens if you part ways. Put every renewal date in a shared calendar; the domain, hosting, email and licence renewals checklist helps you track them.

Training and a guide for your team

You should be able to handle everyday updates without calling your developer. A good handover includes a live walkthrough, ideally recorded, showing how to:

  • Edit text and images on pages
  • Add blog posts, team members, projects or products
  • Update menus and the footer
  • Manage orders, bookings or enquiries, if your site takes them
  • Check form entries and where they go
  • Get a page back from its revisions after a mistake

Short written notes or screen recordings are useful when a new staff member takes over the website. How to edit your WordPress website content yourself makes a good written companion to the walkthrough.

Source files and design assets

  • Design files, such as Figma files, if a designer was involved
  • Logo files in vector formats, plus brand colours and fonts
  • Original photos and videos, not only the compressed versions on the site
  • Custom theme or plugin code, and access to the code repository if one was used
  • Details of any staging site, how to use it, and confirmation that it's hidden from Google

Analytics, Search Console and tracking

  • Search Console verified, with your business Google account as Owner and the sitemap submitted.
  • Google Analytics 4 under your account, with you as Administrator, and form submissions, calls and WhatsApp clicks tracked as key events where they matter.
  • Google Tag Manager, if used, with your account as Administrator.
  • Google Business Profile linked to the new site, with you as primary owner.
  • Search engine visibility switched on. Under Settings > Reading, the option that discourages search engines should be unticked on the live site. It's often left on after a build.

Setting up GA4 and Search Console explains each step if anything is missing.

Support terms after launch

Launch is rarely the end of small fixes. Before you sign off, be clear about:

  • How long the free bug-fix period lasts and what it covers
  • What counts as a bug and what counts as a new request; see bug or change request?
  • How to report problems and the expected response
  • Who is responsible for WordPress, theme and plugin updates from now on
  • Maintenance plan options, if you want ongoing help
  • What happens in an emergency, such as the site going down or being hacked

Handover sign-off checklist

  1. I've logged in to WordPress, hosting, domain, email and Google accounts myself
  2. Shared passwords are changed and stored in our password manager
  3. The developer's access is removed or set to the role we agreed
  4. I have the handover document and the training recording
  5. Licences and renewal dates are listed, with the account each one is under
  6. A launch backup is stored in our own storage, and scheduled backups are running
  7. Design files, brand assets, original media and any custom code have been delivered
  8. Search Console and GA4 are under our account, and search engines aren't blocked
  9. Support terms are agreed in writing

Only when every item is ticked is the project truly finished.

If your developer won't hand things over

Most handover gaps are oversights, and a polite list of what's missing usually fixes them. If a previous developer has gone quiet or refuses, start with the domain, because whoever controls it controls the website and email. FAQs

What should a website handover include?

Access to every account, your own Administrator login, a launch backup you control, documentation, training, licences in your name, design and source files, analytics under your Google account and written support terms.

Should my developer keep admin access after launch?

Only if they're maintaining the site. Otherwise, change their account to a lower role or remove it, and invite them back when you need them.

Do I get the source code of my WordPress website?

You should. A full backup contains the WordPress files, theme, plugins and database, and any custom theme or plugin code should be handed over, with repository access if one was used. Who owns the code should be set out in your agreement.

What if my developer bought the theme or plugin licences?

Ask for them to be transferred to your account if the vendor allows it, or buy your own licence and add it to the site. Otherwise updates can stop when the developer's licence lapses.

How should passwords be shared at handover?

Through a password manager's sharing feature, not email or WhatsApp. Once you've logged in yourself, change any password that was shared.
