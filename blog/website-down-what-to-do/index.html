Website Down or Not Opening? What to Check and Do, in the Right Order

Website down or not opening? Check if it's down for everyone, read the error, then check domain and DNS, hosting limits, SSL and recent changes, in that order.

Key takeaways
  • First confirm the site is down for everyone, not just your network, by testing on mobile data and with an online down checker.
  • The error message tells you which layer failed: DNS errors point to the domain, timeouts and suspension pages to hosting, certificate warnings to SSL, and PHP or database errors to the site itself.
  • Check in this order: domain and DNS, hosting status and resource limits, SSL, then recent changes to the site; restore a backup only once you know the domain and hosting are fine.

When your website is down, first check whether it's down for everyone or just you: open it on your phone using mobile data and run it through an online "is it down" checker. If it's down for everyone, read the error message, because it tells you which layer failed. Then check, in order, your domain and DNS, your hosting account (outages, suspension and resource limits), your SSL certificate, and finally recent changes to the site itself, such as plugin updates.

Working through those checks in order usually finds the cause quickly, and it stops you restoring backups or reinstalling WordPress when the real problem is an unpaid renewal. Below is the full sequence, what each error means and who to contact. Step 1: Is it down for everyone, or just you?

A surprising number of "outages" are local. Before changing anything:

  • Switch networks. Turn off Wi-Fi on your phone and load the site on mobile data. Then try a different browser or a private window.
  • Use a down checker such as downforeveryoneorjustme.com, which tests the site from outside your network.
  • Ask someone in another city to try it, if your customers are spread out.

If it works everywhere except your office or home, the problem is local. Restart the router, clear your computer's DNS cache (on Windows, run ipconfig /flushdns in Command Prompt), or try a public DNS service such as 1.1.1.1 or 8.8.8.8. One cause people miss: on shared hosting, the server's firewall can block your office IP address after repeated failed logins, often from someone with a saved old password. Everyone else sees the site, and you don't. Your host can check and unblock the IP.

Step 3: Check the domain and DNS

If the browser can't find the site at all, start with the domain. A quick test: if your business email on the same domain has also stopped, the domain or DNS is the likely culprit; if email still works, the domain is probably fine and the problem is the hosting or the site.

  1. Log in to your registrar (GoDaddy, Hostinger, BigRock, Namecheap or whoever you bought the domain from) and check the expiry date and status. An expired domain takes down both the website and email. What to do when your domain expires explains grace and redemption periods.
  2. Check the nameservers. They should point to your host or to Cloudflare, whichever manages your DNS. A changed nameserver, often after someone "helpfully" moved the domain, sends visitors nowhere.
  3. Check the A record points to your current server's IP address, especially after a hosting move.
  4. Allow for DNS caching. After a DNS change, some networks keep the old address until the record's cache time runs out, which is why a site can work on Wi-Fi and fail on mobile data for a few hours.

A developer can confirm what the world sees with two commands, or use an online DNS propagation checker:

nslookup yourdomain.com
dig yourdomain.com +short

Step 4: Check the hosting account

  • Status page and announcements. Most hosts publish server outages and maintenance. If there's a known outage, there's little to do but wait and keep customers informed.
  • Billing. Unpaid renewals lead to suspension, sometimes with a shorter warning than people expect. Check the card on file hasn't expired.
  • Emails from your host about suspension, malware or resource abuse. If the account was suspended for malware, the site has to be cleaned before it comes back; see hosting suspended for malware.
  • Resource limits. On shared hosting, CPU, memory, entry processes, disk space and inodes are all capped. A traffic spike, aggressive bots or a heavy plugin can hit the cap and the site starts returning 503 or 508 errors. A full disk can also stop the database from writing. The hosting panel's resource usage page shows which limit was hit.

For 502 and 504 errors that come and go under load, fixing 502 and 504 gateway errors explains PHP workers and what to ask your host.

Step 5: Check the SSL certificate

If visitors see a privacy or certificate warning instead of the site, the certificate has usually expired or doesn't cover the address being used, such as www versus non-www. Free Let's Encrypt certificates last 90 days and renew automatically, but renewal can fail silently after a DNS change or when Cloudflare sits in front of the site. Reissuing the certificate in the hosting panel usually fixes it within minutes. SSL certificate errors explained matches each warning to its fix.

Step 6: Check what changed on the site

If the domain, hosting and SSL are fine and the error comes from WordPress itself, think about the last day or two. Did anyone update plugins, install something new, edit code, change PHP version, move hosting or change DNS? The most recent change is the most likely cause, and undoing it is often the fix.

  • A critical error after an update: fixing "There has been a critical error" covers the recovery mode email, the debug log and disabling plugins over File Manager.
  • "Briefly unavailable for scheduled maintenance": an update was interrupted and a leftover .maintenance file needs deleting.
  • Redirects to spam sites or a Google warning: treat it as a hack, not an outage.

Step 7: Restore a backup, if it's the right fix

Restoring a recent backup is the right move when the site itself is broken and you can't find the cause quickly. It won't help with an expired domain, an unpaid hosting bill or a server outage, so rule those out first. On an online store, a full restore also removes orders placed since the backup, so roll back the single plugin or restore files only where possible. The backup and restore guide covers both.

Who to contact, and what to tell them

ProblemWho can fix it
Expired domain, wrong nameserversYou, through the registrar account, or the registrar's support
Server outage, suspension, resource limits, firewall blocksYour hosting company
Cloudflare errorsWhoever manages your Cloudflare account, then the host
Plugin, theme, database or code errorsYour developer or maintenance provider
Hacked site or Google warningA developer who handles malware cleanup

Whoever you contact, send the exact URL, a screenshot of the error, when it started, what changed beforehand, whether email still works and whether it fails on every network. That saves a round of questions. If nobody has the registrar or hosting logins, that's a problem to fix as soon as the site is back.

While the site is down

  • Make sure your phone number and WhatsApp still work, and mention the outage on your Google Business Profile and social channels if it lasts more than a few hours.
  • Pause Google or Meta ads pointing at the site, so you're not paying for clicks to an error page.
  • Tell staff what to say to customers who call.

Prevent the next outage

  • Uptime monitoring with SSL and domain expiry alerts, so you hear about problems first; see uptime monitoring explained.
  • Auto-renew on the domain and hosting, with a card that won't expire, and renewal reminders going to an inbox someone reads.
  • Automatic off-site backups, so a restore is possible even if the server is lost.
  • Careful updates, with a backup first and big changes tested on staging.
  • A written plan listing logins, contacts and who does what; see a simple website disaster recovery plan.

FAQs

How do I check if my website is down for everyone or just me?

Load it on your phone using mobile data instead of Wi-Fi, try a private browser window, and use an online down checker that tests from outside your network. If it works elsewhere, the problem is your network, DNS cache or a firewall block on your IP address.

Why does my website open on Wi-Fi but not on mobile data, or the other way round?

Usually DNS. After a DNS change, one network can keep the old address cached for a while. Less often, the host's firewall has blocked an IP range, or a particular network has a routing problem to your server. Your host can check both.

My website is down but my email works. What does that mean?

The domain is probably fine, because email on the same domain is still being delivered. The problem is more likely the hosting server or the website itself, so check the host's status, resource limits and recent changes to the site.

How long does it take for a site to come back after renewing an expired domain?

Often within a few hours of renewal, but cached DNS can take longer to clear on some networks. If the domain had moved into its redemption period, recovery costs more and takes longer, so renew as soon as you notice.

Should I restore a backup as soon as the site goes down?

Not until you've ruled out the domain, hosting and SSL. A backup only fixes problems inside the website, such as a bad update or broken code, and on a store it can wipe out recent orders.
