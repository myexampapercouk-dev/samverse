How to Build an Online Course Website on WordPress

Build an online course website on WordPress: choosing an LMS plugin, structuring courses, hosting video safely, quizzes, certificates, payments and support.

Key takeaways
  • Compare LMS plugins against your real needs, such as quizzes, certificates, drip scheduling and payments, and total up the add-ons you will pay for.
  • Host course videos on a dedicated service with domain restrictions and adaptive streaming rather than in the WordPress media library.
  • Plan payments, GST, refunds and student support before launch, and use better hosting because logged-in students bypass page caching.

Selling courses from your own website means you keep your students, your pricing and your brand, instead of depending entirely on a marketplace. WordPress with a learning management system (LMS) plugin can run anything from a single recorded course to a full online academy. The guide on selling digital products on WordPress covers e-books and templates; this one goes deeper into courses.

Is WordPress the right platform?

Hosted course platforms are quicker to start with because they handle hosting, video and payments for you. A WordPress course site takes more setup, but gives you more control.

Hosted course platformWordPress with an LMS plugin
SetupFast, little technical workNeeds hosting, plugins and configuration
Design and brandingLimited to the platform's templatesFully customisable
SEO and contentOften basicStrong, with your blog and course pages on one site
Ongoing costsPlatform plans and sometimes transaction feesHosting, plugin licences and maintenance
OwnershipYou work within their rulesYou own the site and student data

WordPress makes most sense when courses are a long-term part of your business, you want your courses and content marketing on one site, or you need features a platform doesn't offer.

Choosing an LMS plugin

Several established LMS plugins exist, each with a different pricing model:

  • LearnDash: a paid, long-established plugin with many integrations
  • Tutor LMS: a free version, with a Pro plan that adds more features
  • LifterLMS: a free core plugin, with many extras sold as paid add-ons
  • Sensei LMS: from Automattic, with paid courses sold through WooCommerce

Rather than picking by popularity, compare them against your needs: how easy the course builder is, quiz types, certificates, drip scheduling, the student dashboard, reports, payment options and whether they work well with your theme. Add up the cost of the add-ons you'll actually need, and check current plans, since features move between free and paid versions.

Structuring your courses

Good structure makes a course easier to finish, and students who finish are the ones who recommend you.

  • Start with a clear outcome: what will a student be able to do at the end?
  • Break it into modules and short lessons, each focused on one idea, with notes or worksheets to download
  • Offer a free preview lesson so buyers can judge your teaching before paying
  • Decide how content is released: everything at once for self-paced learners, or dripped weekly for cohort-style batches
  • Set access rules: lifetime access, a fixed period such as one year, or access while a subscription is active
  • Consider language: many Indian learners prefer Hindi or a regional language, or a mix, so say clearly which language each course uses

Hosting course videos

Don't upload course videos to your WordPress media library. Large files strain ordinary hosting, play badly on slow connections and are easy to download.

  • YouTube (unlisted): free and reliable, but anyone with the link can watch and share it, so it suits free previews rather than paid lessons
  • Vimeo: paid plans let you restrict playback to your own domain
  • Bunny Stream: usage-based video hosting with options to restrict where videos play
  • VdoCipher: focuses on encrypted playback and viewer watermarks, aimed at ed-tech and test-prep businesses

Choose a service with adaptive streaming, so videos adjust to the student's connection, and add subtitles where you can. No setup can stop someone recording their screen, but domain restrictions and watermarks make casual sharing much harder.

Quizzes, assignments and certificates

  • Quizzes: use them to check understanding, not to trip people up. Set a passing mark, allow retakes and use question banks so answers aren't simply passed around.
  • Timed tests: useful for exam preparation and test series, but test them carefully on mobile phones
  • Assignments: file uploads with manual grading work well, but plan instructor time before promising feedback
  • Certificates: include the student's name, course, completion date and a unique ID that can be checked on your site

Be honest about what a certificate means. Your own completion certificate is fine, but don't imply government recognition or accreditation you don't have; see the note on certification in websites for software training institutes.

Payments, pricing and access

You can sell courses through your LMS's own checkout or through WooCommerce. WooCommerce is often the more flexible route in India because it works with many Indian gateways and suits sites that also sell books or kits.

  • Payment methods: UPI, cards and net banking for Indian students, and international cards if you teach abroad; see how to choose a payment gateway
  • Pricing models: one-time purchase, instalments for higher-priced programmes, an all-access subscription or course bundles
  • Automatic access: enrolment should happen instantly after payment, without you adding students manually
  • GST and invoices: the treatment of online courses can differ from physical goods, so check with your CA before launch
  • Refund policy: state clearly when refunds are available, for example within a short period and before much of the course is completed

Student support and engagement

Recorded courses still need people behind them. Plan support before launch:

  • A Q&A area on each lesson or a discussion forum, so answers help other students too
  • Regular live doubt-clearing sessions on Zoom or Google Meet for higher-priced courses
  • A WhatsApp or Telegram group, if you have the time to moderate it
  • An FAQ covering login, devices, access period and certificates
  • Automatic emails that nudge inactive students back to their next lesson
  • Stated response times, so students know when to expect help

Hosting and speed for course sites

Students are logged in, and pages for logged-in users usually can't be served from the page cache, so every lesson view does real work on the server. A course site with many active students needs better hosting than a brochure site; see how to choose WordPress hosting in India. Keep plugins lean, test lesson pages on a mid-range phone and check load times during your busiest hours.
