Website Brief Template: What to Send Your Web Developer for an Accurate Quote, With a Copy-and-Fill Version

Key takeaways
  • A website brief covers goals, audience, pages, features, content, examples, a budget range, timeline and account access, and one or two pages is enough.
  • Be specific where cost hides: list every page and feature, say who writes the content, and give an honest budget range so developers can suggest the right approach.
  • Send the same brief to every developer, ask each to quote against it line by line, and the quotes become comparable instead of guesses.

A website brief is a short document, usually one or two pages, that tells a developer what your website must achieve and what it needs to include: your goals, audience, pages, features, content, example sites you like, a budget range, your timeline and who controls the domain and hosting. Send the same brief to every developer you approach, and the quotes you get back can be compared line by line instead of being guesses.

When you hire me as your WordPress developer, a WhatsApp message is enough to start a conversation, but a brief like this answers most of the questions I would otherwise ask, so the fixed quote and timeline are accurate from the first reply. The template works for any developer or agency, and for any kind of site, from a five-page business site to a store built through WordPress website development.

Once you have quotes back, how to compare website quotes shows how to line them up.

What a good brief does for you

  • Accurate quotes. Developers price what they can see. Vague requests get padded prices or low quotes that grow later.
  • Comparable quotes. Everyone prices the same scope, so differences in price reflect differences in approach.
  • Fewer surprises. Features you forgot to mention are the most common source of extra charges mid-project.
  • A faster start. Less back-and-forth before work begins.
  • A record of what you asked for, which becomes the basis of the written scope in your contract.

How to fill in the sections that drive cost

Goals

Write goals you could check later, such as "more WhatsApp enquiries for knee treatment" rather than "a modern website". If there are several, put them in order, because the first goal shapes the homepage and the calls to action.

Pages

Name every page rather than giving a number. "Up to ten pages" means different things to different people; "Home, About, six service pages, Contact" doesn't. If you're unsure, list what customers ask you most and let the developer suggest a structure.

Features

Describe what should happen, not just the feature's name. "An enquiry form that emails reception and opens WhatsApp" is quotable; "a contact form" could be five minutes of work or a day. For stores, give the number of products, whether they have sizes or colours, and how you ship and take payment.

Content

Content readiness is often the biggest factor in how long a project takes. Say honestly who will write each page, what photos exist, and whether product data is in a spreadsheet. How long a WordPress website takes explains why.

Design and examples

Two or three example sites are worth more than words like "modern" or "clean", but only if you say what you like about each: the layout of one, the photography of another, the way a third explains its prices. Sites from other industries are fine. Add anything you definitely don't want, and share your logo files and brand colours; if your logo only exists as a blurry image, see logo, favicon and brand basics.

Budget range

A range lets a developer suggest an approach that fits, such as a quality theme instead of a fully custom design. As a guide, my WordPress cost guide puts most professionally built small business sites in India at ₹15,000 to ₹40,000, larger business or B2B sites at ₹35,000 to ₹80,000, and WooCommerce stores from ₹30,000 to ₹90,000 or more. The website cost calculator gives a rough range for your own mix of pages and features.

Technical details and access

Say where the domain and hosting are and whose name they're in. If a previous developer registered them, sort that out early; the website ownership checklist lists what you should control.

Extra sections for redesigns, stores and agencies

  • Redesigns: list the pages that bring traffic or enquiries today, note any URLs that must not change, and offer Search Console and Analytics access, so nothing that ranks is lost; see redesigning without losing rankings.
  • Online stores: product count, variations, payment methods including UPI and cash on delivery, shipping zones, GST invoice needs and any marketplace or stock system to connect.
  • Agencies briefing a developer: the design files with mobile layouts, the content or who supplies it, staging or hosting access, the launch date your client expects and whether the work is white-label.

Using the brief to compare quotes

  1. Send the same brief to each developer or agency.
  2. Ask each to quote against it line by line, saying what's included, what's excluded and what they'd change.
  3. Put the answers side by side, with anything not mentioned marked as a question.
  4. Ask the same follow-up questions to everyone, such as who owns the accounts and what happens after launch.
  5. Compare the total cost over a few years, including hosting, licences and maintenance, not just the build price.

A developer who asks good questions about your brief before quoting is usually a better sign than one who replies with a price in minutes. Once you choose, turn the brief and the quote into a written scope; the website contract checklist covers what to agree.

Common brief mistakes

  • Design only: describing colours and style but not goals, pages or features.
  • A wish list without priorities: mark each item as must have, nice to have or later.
  • No content plan: assuming the developer will write everything without saying so.
  • Hiding the budget: it doesn't get you a lower price, only less relevant suggestions.
  • Copying a big brand's site: "like Amazon" describes years of engineering, not a brief.
  • No named decision-maker, so feedback arrives late and contradicts itself.

FAQs

How long should a website brief be?

One or two pages is enough for most business websites. A store or a site with custom features may need a little more detail on products and workflows, but clarity matters more than length.

Should I tell developers my budget?

Yes, as a range. It helps them suggest an approach that fits, and it saves everyone time if the scope and budget are far apart. A good developer will tell you what's realistic rather than simply quoting the top of your range.

What if I don't know which pages or features I need?

Fill in the goals, audience and examples, and leave the rest blank. A developer can suggest a page structure and features based on those, and you can confirm them before the quote.

Do I need a brief for a small website?

A short one, yes. Even a five-page site benefits from a named page list, who writes the content and any deadline, because those are what change the price and the timeline.

Is the brief the same as the project scope?

No. The brief is what you ask for; the scope is what the developer agrees to deliver, at a price, after clarifying the brief. Check that the scope matches your brief before paying an advance.
