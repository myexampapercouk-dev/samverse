Exit-Intent Pop-ups: When They Help, When They Hurt and How to Use Them Well

When exit-intent pop-ups win leads and when they annoy visitors, what Google says about intrusive mobile pop-ups, and best practices for triggers and offers.

Key takeaways
  • Exit-intent pop-ups help when they offer something genuinely useful that matches the page, not when they appear instantly on every visit.
  • On mobile, avoid full-screen pop-ups that hide content; Google treats intrusive interstitials as a poor experience, so use small banners or inline forms.
  • Keep pop-ups off contact, booking and checkout pages, show them once per visitor, and judge them by total enquiries rather than sign-ups alone.

Pop-ups have a bad reputation, and mostly they've earned it: a box that covers the page the moment you arrive, asking for your email before you've read a word. But a well-timed pop-up with a genuinely useful offer can capture visitors who would otherwise leave and never come back. The difference is almost entirely in when, where and how you use it.

This guide explains what exit-intent pop-ups are, when they help, when they hurt, and how to set one up without annoying visitors or Google.

What an exit-intent pop-up is

An exit-intent pop-up appears when a visitor looks like they're about to leave, rather than when they arrive. On a desktop or laptop, the script watches the mouse: when the cursor moves quickly towards the top of the browser, where the tabs, address bar and close button are, the pop-up appears.

On phones there is no mouse, so tools guess using other signals, such as a quick scroll back up the page or a period of inactivity. These guesses are much less reliable, so a "leaving" pop-up often appears while someone is still happily reading. That's one reason to treat mobile very differently, as covered below.

When pop-ups help

Most visitors aren't ready to enquire or buy on their first visit. An exit-intent pop-up is a last, polite chance to stay in touch. It tends to work when:

  • The offer is genuinely useful. A checklist, cost guide or price list related to the page, not just "join our newsletter". See lead magnets and newsletters for ideas.
  • It matches the page. Someone reading an article on choosing solar panels is offered a solar buying checklist, not a generic discount.
  • It offers another way to reach you. On a service page, "Have a quick question? Chat with us on WhatsApp" can rescue a visitor who wasn't ready to fill in a form.
  • It helps shoppers finish. On an online store, a reminder about delivery charges, cash on delivery or easy returns can answer the doubt that was making someone leave. See abandoned cart recovery.
  • There's a real, time-bound offer. A festival sale with a genuine end date, linked to the offer page.

When pop-ups hurt

  • Instant pop-ups on arrival. The visitor hasn't seen your content yet, so they have no reason to trust you with their details.
  • Pop-ups on every page, every visit. Closing the same box five times is how people learn to dislike a website.
  • Interrupting the action you want. A pop-up over your contact form, booking page or checkout can stop an enquiry or sale that was about to happen.
  • Hard-to-close designs. A tiny, faint close button, or one that appears only after a delay.
  • Guilt-tripping and fake urgency. "No thanks, I don't want to grow my business" buttons and countdown timers that reset on every visit damage trust.
  • Stacking. A pop-up on top of a cookie notice on top of a chat widget leaves no room for the page itself.

Mobile pop-ups and Google's guidance

Google has long said that intrusive interstitials, meaning pop-ups that make content hard to reach, give mobile users a poor experience, and that pages where content isn't easily accessible may not rank as highly. The examples it gives include:

  • A pop-up that covers the main content as soon as someone arrives from search, or while they're reading
  • A standalone screen that must be dismissed before the content can be seen
  • A layout where the top of the page looks like a pop-up and the real content is pushed below it

Some things are generally not treated as intrusive: notices required by law, such as cookie consent or age checks, login boxes for private content, and small banners that use a reasonable amount of screen space and are easy to dismiss.

How much weight this carries isn't public, and it's one signal among many, but the practical rule is simple: on mobile, avoid full-screen pop-ups. Use a small bar at the bottom of the screen, a slide-in, or a form placed within the content instead. See mobile-first design explained.

Best practices

DecisionBetter choiceAvoid
TriggerExit intent on desktop, or after someone has scrolled most of an articleShowing it the instant the page loads
PagesBlog posts, guides, product and category pagesContact, booking, checkout and thank-you pages
FrequencyOnce per visitor, then hidden for a few weeks; never again after they sign upEvery page, every visit
OfferOne specific, useful thing related to the page"Subscribe for updates" with no clear benefit
FormJust an email address or WhatsApp number, with a note on what they'll receiveLong forms asking for company, budget and phone
ClosingA clear close button; the Escape key and clicking outside also workHidden or delayed close buttons
MobileA small bottom banner or an inline formA full-screen overlay

Two more details matter. If you collect emails or WhatsApp numbers for offers, make consent clear and mention it in your privacy policy. And make the pop-up usable with a keyboard and screen reader, so focus moves into it when it opens and returns to the page when it closes.

Setting it up on WordPress

There are a few common routes:

  • Page builder pop-ups: Elementor Pro includes a pop-up builder with exit-intent, scroll and timing triggers, which is handy if your site already uses it.
  • Pop-up plugins: tools such as Popup Maker handle triggers, page targeting and frequency; some offer exit intent only in paid versions, so check current plans.
  • Hosted services: platforms such as OptinMonster add advanced targeting and testing, usually on a subscription.

Whatever you choose, watch the speed cost. Pop-up tools add scripts and styles to every page they load on, so load them only where needed and check your mobile speed scores before and after. A pop-up with a large image or extra fonts can slow the page for everyone, including visitors who never see it.

Measure whether it's worth it

A pop-up's own sign-up rate only tells half the story. Also check:

  • Whether total enquiries, sales or sign-ups actually rose, rather than just moving from your inline forms to the pop-up
  • Whether engagement dropped on pages where the pop-up shows
  • Whether the people who sign up ever become customers
  • Any comments from customers or staff about the pop-up

Change one thing at a time, such as the offer or the trigger, and give each version enough traffic to judge fairly. See CRO basics for a simple testing approach.
