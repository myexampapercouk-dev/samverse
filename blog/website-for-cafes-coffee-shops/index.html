Websites for Cafes and Coffee Shops: What Customers Look For

What a cafe or coffee shop website needs: a cafe-friendly menu, real photos of the space, work-friendly details, events, ordering options and local SEO.

Key takeaways
  • Answer the pre-visit questions first: opening hours, menu, price range, vibe, location and whether laptops are welcome.
  • Build a cafe-specific menu page with drink options, milk alternatives, eggless markers and seasonal specials, and link table QR codes to it.
  • Use real photos, a current events page and a light Instagram gallery, and keep Google Business Profile details consistent for local searches.

People rarely choose a cafe on coffee alone. They want to know what the place feels like, whether they can sit with a laptop for two hours, whether there's something eggless to eat, and whether it's open right now. A cafe website that answers those questions quickly turns a "cafe near me" search into a visit. If you run a full-service restaurant, our guide to restaurant websites and online ordering covers that side; this one focuses on what is specific to cafes and coffee shops.

What people check before visiting a cafe

Most cafe visits are decided on a phone within minutes, around the same few questions:

  • Is it open now? Opening hours, including early mornings, late evenings and holiday changes
  • What's the vibe? Quiet and cosy, lively, family-friendly or good for a first date
  • What will it cost? A rough idea of the price range, which the menu answers
  • Can I work here? Wi-Fi, power sockets and whether laptops are welcome
  • How do I get there? Location, landmarks, parking and whether there are stairs

Put the answers on the homepage, not three clicks deep: hours, address with a map link, a "View menu" button and a one-tap call or WhatsApp button.

A menu page built for a cafe

A cafe menu has quirks a restaurant menu doesn't. Build it as a proper web page rather than a PDF or a photo of the chalkboard, and include:

  • Drinks with their options: hot and iced versions, cup sizes, and milk alternatives such as oat or almond milk, with any extra charge
  • Beans and brew methods: if you serve pour-over, cold brew or single-origin coffee, say where the beans come from
  • Food by time of day: breakfast, all-day plates, sandwiches, bakes and desserts, with breakfast timings stated clearly
  • Dietary markers: veg and non-veg symbols, plus "eggless" or "contains egg" on bakes, which many customers in India look for
  • Seasonal specials: a small section you can update in minutes, instead of reprinting everything

Link your table QR codes to this same page, so the menu customers see at the table is always the current one.

Show the space as it really is

For a cafe, the room is part of the product. Visitors want to picture where they will sit before they arrive, so use real photos rather than stock images:

  • Each seating area: window tables, sofas, communal tables, the counter and any outdoor seating
  • The space at different times of day, if the mood changes from bright mornings to warm evenings
  • Your signature drinks and dishes, photographed in natural light
  • The team behind the counter, if they are happy to be featured

Also answer the questions people otherwise call about: whether pets are welcome, whether there's step-free access, and whether you take reservations or are walk-in only. Compress photos so the gallery stays fast on mobile data.

Information for people who want to work

Students, freelancers and remote workers are regular cafe customers, and they plan ahead. A short "Working from here" FAQ sets fair expectations:

QuestionWhat to tell them
Is there Wi-Fi?Yes or no, and whether they should ask staff for the password
Are there power sockets?Roughly where they are, and whether they are limited
Are laptops welcome all day?Any laptop-free hours, such as weekend lunch
Is there a minimum order or time limit?Your policy, stated politely

If you'd rather not attract all-day laptop users, say so kindly. Being clear avoids awkward conversations at the table and disappointed reviews later.

Events, workshops and private bookings

Many cafes host open mics, board game nights, book clubs, live music, coffee tastings or art workshops. An events page turns these into a reason to visit and a reason to come back:

  • Upcoming events with date, time, price and what's included
  • Online registration, with UPI or card payment for paid workshops so seats are confirmed
  • A short archive of past events with photos, which shows the cafe is lively
  • A private booking enquiry form for birthdays, small gatherings, team outings or shoots, asking for the date, group size and budget

Remove events once they're over; an events page still promoting last season's open mic makes the site look abandoned.

Takeaway, delivery and selling coffee beans

Ordering on a cafe website is usually lighter than for a restaurant, but a few options work well:

  • Order ahead for pickup: office-goers can pay for their coffee in advance and collect it without queuing
  • WhatsApp ordering for regulars and bulk orders, such as coffee and snacks for an office meeting
  • Delivery app links for customers who prefer them, shown clearly but not ahead of your direct options
  • Retail products: roasted beans, ground coffee, drip bags, mugs and gift boxes can be sold through a small online shop, including repeat orders for beans

Selling beans online widens your reach beyond the neighbourhood, but it brings packaging, shipping and GST questions, so start with a few products and check the tax side with your CA.

Instagram and your website

Instagram is where many people discover cafes, so it deserves a place on your website, but not at the cost of speed:

  • Link to your Instagram profile clearly in the header or footer
  • Instead of a heavy live feed widget, show a small, hand-picked gallery of your best posts as optimised images; live embeds load extra code, as explained in our guide to third-party scripts that slow websites
  • Point the link in your Instagram bio to your menu or events page, not just the homepage
  • Keep hours, prices and menu items consistent across Instagram, your website and Google

Local SEO for cafes

Most cafe searches are local and often made on the spot. To appear for them:

  • Complete your Google Business Profile with the right category, hours, menu link, photos and the attributes Google offers, such as Wi-Fi or outdoor seating; see the Google Business Profile checklist
  • Use your area naturally in page titles and text, for example "cafe in {area}" or "coffee shop near {landmark}"
  • Mention what makes you a good fit for common searches, such as "work-friendly cafe", "pet-friendly cafe" or "breakfast in {area}", but only if it's true
  • Add structured data (schema.org has a CafeOrCoffeeShop type) with your address, hours and menu link
  • Ask happy regulars for Google reviews, and reply to them, including the critical ones

Finally, keep the site fast: people search for cafes on the move, and a slow page sends them to the cafe down the road.
