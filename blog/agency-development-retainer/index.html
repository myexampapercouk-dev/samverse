Agency Development Retainers: How a Monthly Arrangement With a WordPress Partner Works

How a monthly development retainer between an agency and a WordPress partner works: what it covers, hours vs scope, request queues, unused time and reviews.

Key takeaways
  • A monthly retainer reserves a WordPress partner's time or an agreed scope for an agency's steady stream of small requests, while large builds are still quoted as separate projects.
  • Run requests through one queue with a standard template, one person setting priorities and estimates for bigger tasks, so a single request cannot quietly use up the month.
  • Agree in writing how unused time, overage and notice work, and review the retainer's size, request types, response times and quality every few months.

When an agency sends a WordPress partner work every week, quoting each task separately starts to slow everyone down. A monthly development retainer replaces that with an agreed amount of the partner's time, or an agreed scope, each month, so requests can start without a fresh quote and the partner keeps capacity free for you. This guide covers how such an arrangement usually works between an agency and its partner. It's different from a client-facing white-label care plan, which is a product you sell, and from occasional overflow work, which is quoted project by project.

When a retainer makes sense

  • Requests arrive every week: content changes, landing pages, fixes and small features across several client sites
  • You resell maintenance, and the technical work behind it needs a dependable home
  • Quoting and approving each small task takes longer than doing it
  • You want a partner who already knows your client sites, standards and tools

A retainer fits less well when work is irregular or mostly large one-off builds. Those are usually better quoted per project, with the retainer covering the smaller work around them.

What a retainer usually covers

Write down what the retainer is for, so both sides know what goes into the queue and what gets quoted separately.

Usually includedUsually quoted or agreed separately
Content and layout changes on client sitesNew website builds and redesigns
Landing pages and extra templates from approved designsDesign and copywriting
Bug fixes and plugin conflictsHosting fees and premium plugin licences
Maintenance tasks behind your care plansOut-of-hours emergency cover, unless specifically agreed
Speed and SEO fixes from your auditsLarge custom features that need their own scope

Hours-based or scope-based

Retainers generally take one of two shapes, and some agencies combine them.

Hours-basedScope-based
How it worksA set amount of the partner's time each month, logged against tasksAn agreed list of task types or deliverables each month, without tracking time per task
Works well whenRequests vary a lot in size and typeThe work is predictable, such as maintenance across a known set of sites
Watch out forTime logs too vague to check against client sitesDisagreements over what fits within the agreed scope

Either way, agree what happens when a request turns out bigger than expected: the partner estimates it and waits for your approval before it uses up the month. The trade-offs are similar to those in fixed price vs hourly billing.

Request queues and priorities

A retainer runs smoothly only if requests arrive in one place, in a consistent format, in a clear order:

  • One queue: a shared board or ticket system, not requests scattered across email, chat and calls
  • A request template: client site, page URL, what's needed, screenshots or designs, and any deadline
  • One person setting priorities: usually your project manager, so the partner never has to choose between two account managers' "urgent" tasks
  • Priority levels: for example urgent (site down or checkout broken), high (this week), normal and low
  • Estimates for bigger tasks before work starts, so one request doesn't quietly use the whole month
  • A short weekly check: what's done, what's next and what's waiting on your team or the client

If your partner works in another time zone, agree response times by severity and an emergency path; see working with an offshore WordPress developer across time zones.

Rollover and unused time

Quiet months happen, so decide in advance what happens to time or capacity you don't use. Common approaches:

  • No rollover: the partner kept the time free for you, so unused time lapses. Simple, but it can feel wasteful in a slow month.
  • Limited rollover: unused time carries into the next month only, or up to an agreed cap, then lapses
  • A quiet-month backlog: a list of useful non-urgent work, such as speed improvements, plugin clean-ups or your agency's own website, so spare time is put to good use

Agree overage too. When a busy month needs more, extra work should be approved in advance and either billed on agreed terms or scheduled into the next month. Whatever you choose, put it in writing, because unclear rollover rules cause more friction than almost anything else in a retainer.

Reviewing the arrangement

A monthly summary from the partner keeps things transparent: tasks completed per client site, time or scope used, what's queued and any recurring problems worth fixing at the source. Every few months, step back and ask:

  • Is the retainer the right size, or are you regularly over or well under?
  • Are the right kinds of requests going through it, or would some be better quoted as projects?
  • Are turnaround and response times being met on both sides?
  • Is quality consistent, or do the same issues keep coming back?
  • Does the work map cleanly onto what you bill your clients?

Agree a notice period as well, and how the arrangement ends: open tasks finished or handed back, documentation shared and the partner's access to client sites removed.

Setting one up

Most retainers work best after a few projects together, once you know the partner's quality and communication. Then put the scope, hours or deliverables, priorities, response times, rollover and overage rules, billing, confidentiality and notice into one short written agreement, and review it after the first couple of months.
