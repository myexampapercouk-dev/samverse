How Manufacturers Can Get More B2B and Export Enquiries From Their Website: A Complete Guide

A B2B manufacturer website guide: pages for every buyer, applications pages, RFQs, technical downloads, dealers, export buyers and SEO for B2B searches.

Key takeaways
  • Plan the site for everyone in the buying process: engineers need specifications and drawings, purchase teams a quick RFQ, quality teams verifiable certificates and approvers a company profile.
  • Applications pages, a technical downloads library and a separate route for dealers are the parts most manufacturer websites miss, and each one answers a different kind of buyer.
  • B2B searches are specific, so match each type of query, from product plus specification to application and comparison, with a page written for it, and show only certifications you currently hold.

A manufacturer's website wins B2B enquiries when each person in the buying process can check what they need without phoning you. The engineer finds specifications and drawings, the purchase team finds a quick way to request a quote, the quality team finds certificates it can verify, and the person who approves a new supplier finds evidence that you can deliver. Add pages for the applications you serve, a downloads library, a route for dealers and export buyers, and product pages written in the terms buyers search, and the site starts bringing in RFQs instead of sitting behind your visiting card.

Who visits a manufacturer's website, and what each person checks

B2B purchases are rarely made by one person. The engineer who finds you on Google is often not the person who approves a new supplier, so the site has to work for several readers:

VisitorWhat they are checkingThe page that should answer it
Design or maintenance engineerDoes the product meet the specification? Dimensions, ratings, materials, tolerancesProduct family pages with specification tables, drawings and datasheets
Purchase or procurement teamCan I get a price quickly? Minimum order, lead time, deliveryAn RFQ form on every product page, plus phone, email and WhatsApp
Quality or vendor development teamAre the certifications real and current? What checks do they run?A quality page with certificate numbers, scope, validity and copies
Owner or senior managementIs this company stable? Who runs it? Who else buys from them?About, infrastructure, leadership and clients pages, and a company profile PDF
Importer or overseas buyerDo they export? To which countries, on what terms?An export page, international contact details and familiar units
Dealer or distributorIs my territory open? What support do dealers get?A dealer page with its own form, separate from the RFQ

Many manufacturer websites serve only the first one or two rows, and then only partly. Planning a page for every row is often the biggest single improvement available.

A site structure that works for most manufacturers

  1. Products: categories, then product family pages, using the names buyers search with. How to build a product catalogue website covers family pages, specification tables and filters in detail.
  2. Applications or industries: one page for each use or sector you genuinely serve.
  3. Capabilities and infrastructure: named machinery, processes, capacity and in-house facilities.
  4. Quality and certifications: your quality process and only the certificates you currently hold.
  5. Downloads: datasheets, drawings, manuals, the company profile and catalogues in one library.
  6. Dealers or where to buy, if you sell through a network.
  7. About, leadership and clients.
  8. Insights: articles that answer the questions buyers ask early in a search.
  9. Contact and RFQ, with routing by product line or region.

Vansh Group, an electronics OEM I built a site for, keeps all five of its services on the homepage as cards (PCB and circuit development, electrical appliance development, sheet metal, injection moulded parts, and tool and die development), so a procurement team sees the whole range on one screen before deciding which conversation to start.

Applications pages: the pages most manufacturers miss

Engineers often search by the job rather than the product: what to use for a particular process, plant or environment. An applications page meets that search. For each industry or use you genuinely serve, explain the conditions the product faces, which of your products fit and why, the specifications that matter for that use, photos of real installations (with the client's permission if they can be identified) and the standards relevant to that sector.

Two rules keep these pages useful. Write one only where you have real experience and something specific to say, because ten pages with the industry name swapped are thin content that helps nobody. And link both ways: from each applications page to the product families, and from each product page back to the applications it suits.

RFQs and enquiry routing

A request for quote should carry the product name from the page it was sent from, ask for quantity, delivery location and requirement details, accept drawings and reach the right person immediately. Keep dealer applications, job applications and supplier offers on separate forms or addresses, so RFQs are not buried among them. Request-for-quote forms for B2B websites covers what to ask, file uploads, routing and follow-up.

A technical downloads library

B2B buyers need documents to forward internally and keep on file. Put them in one downloads section, and link each file from its product page too:

  • Datasheets for each product family, with a revision number or date on the document and beside the download link
  • Drawings and CAD files in the formats engineers use, if you are willing to share them
  • Installation and operating manuals, which help after the sale and bring maintenance engineers back to your site
  • Certificates with their validity dates
  • The company profile and catalogues for the approver who will never read your product pages

Vansh Group offers its company profile as a PDF, alongside two catalogues covering LED fixtures and the complete LED lights it makes as an OEM, because the person who finds a supplier's website is often not the person who signs off on it. Keep most files open so they can be found and shared, and replace old versions rather than piling up duplicates; SEO for PDFs and brochures explains file names, document titles and when gating makes sense.

Certifications: only what you hold

A certificate logo is a claim that a quality team will check. Show only certifications your company currently holds, with the certificate number, the certifying body, the scope (which site and which products it covers) and the validity date, and remove lapsed ones promptly. Don't let a product certification that covers some models appear to cover your whole range. Product-level approvals belong on the product pages they apply to.

Dealers, distributors and export buyers

If you sell through a network, the website has two jobs. For customers, a "where to buy" page or dealer locator by state or city sends local buyers to the right dealer instead of your head office. For prospective dealers, a "become a dealer" page explains territories, support and what you expect, with its own form. Dealer price lists and order forms can sit behind a login once the network is large enough to justify it; websites for wholesalers and distributors covers dealer portals and bulk orders.

Export buyers want the same proof as domestic buyers plus a few specifics: countries you already ship to, minimum order quantities, lead times, shipping terms such as FOB or CIF, and the units they use, all stated only where accurate. Showing your time zone and response hours beside the contact form helps. Websites for export businesses goes into language, localisation and speed for overseas visitors.

SEO for B2B search queries

B2B searches are lower in volume than consumer searches but far more specific, and a single enquiry can be valuable. Match each type of query with the right page:

Type of searchExamplePage that should rank
Product plus specification"2 inch SS 316 flanged ball valve"Product family page with a full specification table
Product plus supplier and place"control panel manufacturer in Pune"Category page, with your location in the title and on the contact page
Application"pumps for effluent treatment plants"Applications page
Selection or comparison"PLC vs DCS for a chemical plant"An insights article linking to the right products
Company or model nameYour brand or a model numberHomepage and product pages, consistent with your marketplace listings

Articles work for manufacturers when they answer questions engineers and buyers actually ask. India Automation Hub, a news and resource portal for the automation industry that I built, groups its archive into families such as control systems, robotics and industrial software, and publishes pieces like a comparison of DCS and PLC for refinery, pharma and chemical plants: the depth technical readers expect. Vansh Group's Insights articles take a buyer's angle instead, covering how companies are diversifying supply chains and what to check on a factory visit before signing with an OEM, and end by inviting readers to arrange a consultation and a facility tour.

Trade publications are another route to buyers. India Automation Hub, for example, has a separate address for vendors' product releases, and coverage in publications your buyers read brings visitors who already trust the source.

Mistakes that cost manufacturers enquiries

  • One "Products" page of photos with no specifications
  • Specifications only inside images or PDFs, which buyers can't copy and Google reads poorly
  • Certification logos that have lapsed, or client logos used without permission
  • A contact form with no product, quantity or location fields, sent to an inbox nobody checks daily
  • Stock photos of factories instead of your own shop floor
  • Catalogue PDFs several versions old, still linked from product pages

FAQs

What pages should a manufacturing company website have?

Product categories and family pages, applications or industries served, capabilities and infrastructure, quality and certifications, a downloads library, about and leadership, insights articles, and a contact page with an RFQ form. Add dealer pages if you sell through a network.

Should a manufacturer show prices on its website?

Usually not for made-to-order or volume-priced products, where a catalogue with quote requests suits better. Standard items and spares with fixed prices can be sold online alongside the catalogue.

Is a marketplace listing enough, or do we need our own website?

Marketplaces such as IndiaMART bring enquiries, but your listing sits beside competitors and you control little of the presentation. Your own website is where buyers who search your name, or check you after finding you elsewhere, see the full picture. Many manufacturers use both and keep them consistent.

Do manufacturers need a blog?

Not a blog for its own sake. A few well-researched articles that answer the selection, comparison and application questions your sales team hears can bring the right engineers to your product pages.

Which certifications should we show?

Only the ones you currently hold, with the number, certifying body, scope and validity date, and a copy buyers can check. Product certifications belong on the products they cover.
