Websites for Digital Marketing Agencies: Niche Positioning, Service Pages, Case Studies With Permission, Qualifying Lead Forms, Proposals and the Development Partner Question

What a digital marketing agency's own website needs: a clear niche, honest service pages, permitted case studies, qualifying forms and a proposal flow.

Key takeaways
  • Decide who the agency is for before designing anything, because the homepage headline, service pages and case studies all hang off that one sentence.
  • Case studies need written client permission and real context; a short, honest story with modest figures persuades more than a wall of logos.
  • If you resell websites built by a development partner, be clear with yourself and your clients about who builds what, and never pass off a partner's portfolio as your own.

A digital marketing agency's website has to do one job well: convince a busy business owner, within a few minutes, that you understand businesses like theirs and can be trusted with their budget. That means a plainly stated niche, a page for each service that answers buyer questions, case studies you have permission to publish, an enquiry form that sorts serious leads from tyre-kickers, and a tidy route from first contact to proposal. Your own speed and tracking are part of the pitch too.

Many agencies also sell websites to their clients, and some build them through a partner. I do that kind of behind-the-scenes build work, described on the WordPress developer for agencies page, so the last part of this guide covers how to handle that relationship honestly on your own site.

How a prospect actually reads your site

Before planning pages, walk through the visit a typical prospect makes after finding you on Google, LinkedIn or a referral. It is rarely more than five pages:

  1. Homepage headline: is this agency for a business like mine?
  2. One service page: what exactly do they do, and what will it cost me in time and money?
  3. One case study: have they done this for someone similar?
  4. About or team: who would actually handle my account?
  5. Contact: how much effort is it to start a conversation?

If any of those five pages is vague, the prospect closes the tab and opens the next agency on their list. Every section below strengthens one of these stops.

Positioning: the sentence everything hangs on

"Full-service digital marketing agency" tells a prospect nothing they can use to choose you. Pick an angle that a real buyer would recognise as theirs. Here are common ways to position, with example headlines to show the difference in tone:

Positioning byExample headlineWorks well when
Industry"Patient enquiries for private clinics in Maharashtra"Most of your best clients share a sector
Service"Google Ads management for B2B manufacturers"One service brings most of your revenue
Outcome"More qualified site visits for real estate developers"You can describe a result without promising a number
Market"An Indian performance team for UK e-commerce brands"You serve overseas clients from India

A niche on the homepage does not stop you taking other work. It means the first screen speaks directly to the clients you want most. Two or three industry pages can then cover secondary sectors without diluting the main message. Streak Creative, a growth marketing agency whose site I built, does something similar: its site is organised around its own named methodology and offers aimed at specific industries, which you can see in the Streak Creative case study.

Service pages that answer buyer questions

Give each core service its own page. A careful buyer, especially one who has been let down by an agency before, reads these closely. Each page should cover four things.

Scope and fit

Say who the service suits and who it does not. "Best for businesses already spending on ads who want better leads, not first-time advertisers" is more useful than a list of adjectives.

What the client provides

Access to ad accounts and analytics, approvals, product information, creatives or a budget for them. Buyers appreciate knowing this before the first call.

Reporting and rhythm

How often you report, what the report contains and whether there is a monthly call. An anonymised sample report, linked from the page, answers this better than any description.

Pricing signal

You do not have to publish rates, but say how pricing works: a fixed retainer, a starting point, or what moves the quote. State plainly that ad spend is paid separately from your fee. That single line prevents many awkward conversations.

Case studies with permission

A case study persuades when the reader can see themselves in it. That needs context, not a screenshot of a graph going up. A structure that works:

  1. The client, described by sector, size and city, or by name if they have agreed
  2. Where they started and what was not working
  3. What you did, in order, including what you tried that did not work
  4. What changed, over what period, in terms the client has approved
  5. A short quote from the client, only if they have given it in writing

Before publishing, run through this permission checklist:

  • Written approval to name the client, show their logo and quote them
  • Approval of the exact figures you publish, with sensitive ones such as revenue removed or turned into ranges the client accepts
  • Dashboard screenshots cropped to hide account IDs, customer data and spend the client wants private
  • No work presented as yours that you did white-label for another agency, unless that agency agrees
  • A note in your records of who approved what and when

If a client prefers to stay anonymous, an anonymised study described by industry and size still helps. The guide to writing case studies for a business website covers structure in more depth.

Lead forms that qualify without scaring people off

Most agency enquiries start with "can you look at our account?" A short multi-step form lets you ask enough to judge fit without a wall of fields on one screen.

From enquiry to proposal

The website's job does not end at the form. The steps after it shape whether the prospect signs.

  • Immediate acknowledgement: an automatic email or WhatsApp message saying who will reply and roughly when
  • A clear first step: an audit, a discovery call or a proposal request, with what it includes and whether it is free stated on the site
  • A calendar link for prospects who pass your qualification, so they can book the call themselves
  • A proposal that matches the website: same service names, same scope wording, same reporting promises

Some agencies publish proposals as private, unindexed web pages rather than PDFs. They are easier to read on a phone and can include the sample report and relevant case studies. Either works, as long as the scope, minimum term, notice period and who owns the ad accounts and website are written down clearly.

Selling websites through a development partner

Clients often ask their marketing agency for a new website, landing pages or ongoing maintenance. If your team does not build in-house, a white-label partner lets you say yes. Handled honestly, it is a normal arrangement. A few principles keep it that way:

  • Describe web design as a service you deliver and manage, without implying you have an in-house development team if you do not
  • Do not show a partner's other projects as your agency's portfolio
  • Agree in writing who owns the code, who holds licences and hosting, and what happens if the partnership ends
  • Make sure the client's website, domain and accounts are in the client's name, not yours or the partner's
  • Run the partner's builds through your own quality check before the client sees them

For campaign pages your team designs, a partner can also build focused landing pages with tracking already set up, so your media team can launch faster. The white-label WordPress development guide goes further into contracts and workflow.

Your own site is a sample of your work

A prospect considering you for SEO or ads may well test your homepage in PageSpeed Insights or look at how your tracking is set up. A slow, script-heavy agency site undermines the pitch.

  • Skip autoplaying showreels on mobile, and compress the ones you keep
  • Load only the chat, heatmap and tracking tools you genuinely use
  • Track form submissions, calls and WhatsApp clicks as separate conversions
  • Give each service page a distinct title, description and heading structure

If the metrics are new to your team, Core Web Vitals explained is a plain-English starting point.

A pre-launch checklist for an agency website

  • Homepage headline names who you help and what with
  • Every service page states scope, client inputs, reporting and how pricing works
  • At least two case studies with written permission on file
  • Team page with real names, roles and photos
  • Sample report available
  • Multi-step enquiry form, tested, with acknowledgement message and thank-you page
  • Partner-delivered services described accurately
  • Speed checked on a mid-range phone over mobile data

FAQs

Should a digital marketing agency publish its prices?

Not necessarily, but explain how pricing works. A starting retainer, a range or a list of what affects the quote filters out poor-fit leads and makes the first call more productive. Always say that ad spend is separate.

Can we show client logos without a full case study?

Only with permission. A logo implies an endorsement, so ask each client in writing. Many agencies find two or three detailed, approved case studies do more than a row of logos.

Is it dishonest to resell a partner's web development?

No, as long as you do not misrepresent it. You manage the project and take responsibility for it. What you should avoid is claiming an in-house team you do not have, or presenting the partner's other work as your own.

How many industry pages should an agency have?

One for each sector where you have genuine client work and something particular to say. Two or three sectors with genuine case studies are better than a page for every industry with the same text and a different name swapped in.

Do we need a separate landing page for our own ads?

Usually, yes. Sending agency ads to your homepage dilutes the message. A focused page per offer or niche, with one call to action and proof that matches the audience, is the advice you would give a client.
