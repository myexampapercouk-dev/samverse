Putting Your Restaurant Menu Online: PDF vs Web Page, Prices, Dietary Labels, QR Codes and Menu SEO

How to put your restaurant menu on your website: web page vs PDF, prices, veg and allergen labels, photos, menu SEO, QR menus and multiple outlets.

Key takeaways
  • Build the menu as a real web page with dish names, prices and markers as text, and keep a PDF only as a printable extra.
  • Give every dish the same details in the same order, with dietary and allergen labels supplied by your kitchen, and photos only for the dishes that sell the restaurant.
  • Point QR codes, your Google Business Profile and every outlet to stable menu addresses, and give one person the job of keeping prices current everywhere.

Your restaurant menu should be a proper web page: dish names, descriptions, prices and veg or non-veg markers written as text, organised into sections with jump links, quick to read on a phone and easy for your staff to update. A PDF or a photo of the printed card is slow to open, hard to read on a small screen and largely invisible to Google. Keep a PDF only as a printable extra, and point your QR codes and Google listing at the web page.

The menu is the first thing I plan on restaurant websites: a mobile-friendly digital menu your team can change from the dashboard, linked to table reservations, WhatsApp or online ordering, Google Maps and opening hours. This guide covers the menu page in detail, from how each dish is written to schema, QR codes and menus for more than one outlet. For taking orders from the menu, see online ordering on your restaurant website.

Prices: show them, and keep them true

Diners use the menu to decide whether you fit their budget, so a menu without prices sends them to a delivery app to find out. Show the price on every dish, and say clearly whether taxes are included and whether a service charge applies, following current consumer guidelines on service charges. If your delivery-app prices differ from dine-in prices, say which menu the website shows. For restaurants abroad, show the local currency: on Southern Flavours, an Indian restaurant in Solihull, UK that I built a site for, every dish in the online shop shows its price in pounds.

Dietary labels and allergens

Indian diners rely on veg and non-veg markers, and many look for Jain, no onion and garlic, eggless or vegan options. Allergies are more serious still: nuts, dairy, gluten, egg and sesame can make someone ill. A few rules:

  • Put a short key at the top of the menu explaining every symbol and label you use.
  • Take allergen and dietary information from your kitchen, dish by dish, and update it when a recipe changes.
  • Avoid absolute claims such as "gluten-free" or "nut-free" if the kitchen can't prevent cross-contact; "made without wheat, prepared in a kitchen that uses wheat" is honest.
  • Add a line inviting guests with allergies to tell staff before ordering.
  • For longer menus, a "veg only" toggle saves vegetarian diners a lot of scrolling.

FSSAI's labelling and display rules include menu requirements for some food businesses, particularly larger chains, so ask your FSSAI consultant what applies to your licence and number of outlets. Cafes have a few labels of their own, such as plant milks and eggless bakes; see websites for cafes and coffee shops.

Photos: the dishes that sell the restaurant

You don't need a photo of every dish. A photo beside each signature dish, and a small gallery of the rest, keeps the menu fast and makes the best dishes stand out. Southern Flavours does this with a "Signature Creations" section of eight dishes that mixes South and North Indian cooking, from idli and dosa to dal tarka and saag paneer, which tells a first-time visitor the restaurant is not only for South Indian food. Shoot in daylight, on the plates you actually serve, and compress every image so the menu still loads quickly on mobile data.

Easy to use on a phone

  • Section jump links that stay at the top of the screen as diners scroll
  • Sections in the order people eat: starters, mains, breads and rice, desserts, drinks
  • Text large enough to read without zooming, with the price aligned so it is easy to compare
  • A search box once the menu runs to more than a few screens
  • A "Book a table" or "Order" button near the menu, so the decision leads straight to action; Southern Flavours puts a "Menu & Ordering" button in the hero, next to "Table Reservations"

Menu SEO and schema

People search for dishes as well as restaurants: "best Chettinad chicken in Koregaon Park" or "South Indian breakfast near me". A text menu lets Google see that you serve them. To help it further:

  • Give the menu page a clear title with your name and area, such as "Menu | Restaurant name, Area".
  • Write fuller descriptions for the dishes you are known for, rather than one-word entries.
  • Give a distinct menu its own page when people search for it, such as a weekend breakfast buffet, a thali lunch or a party menu.
  • Link your Google Business Profile's menu link to the menu page, and keep the two consistent.
  • Add Restaurant schema with a link to your menu page, so search engines connect the menu with the restaurant.

Schema.org also has markup for menus, sections and individual dishes. At the time of writing, Google doesn't show a special search feature for it, so treat it as a help to understanding rather than a way to rank; schema markup explained covers what structured data can and can't do.

Keeping the menu current

An out-of-date menu causes awkward moments at the table and bad reviews online. Decide who updates it and when:

  1. Change prices on the website the same day the printed menu changes.
  2. Mark dishes unavailable for the day from the dashboard instead of leaving them on the page.
  3. Add seasonal and festival dishes with an "available until" note, then switch them off.
  4. Check the website menu against your Google Business Profile and delivery apps once a month.

If you also take orders online, building the ordering menu from the same dish data saves keeping two lists in step.

QR code menus that never need reprinting

A table QR code should open your menu web page, not a PDF. Point it at a stable address such as yourrestaurant.com/menu/, so changing dishes or prices never means reprinting the codes, and add UTM tags so you can see how many scans you get in analytics; QR codes for your business explains how. Keep printed menus available for guests who prefer them, including older diners.

Menus for more than one outlet

If all outlets serve the same dishes at the same prices, one menu page with a note on any outlet-only items is enough. If dishes or prices differ, give each outlet its own menu page linked from its own location page and its own Google Business Profile, so a diner in one area doesn't order a dish only served in another. Each outlet's hours, phone and booking link belong on the same page; websites for businesses with several branches covers location pages in more depth.

FAQs

Is a PDF menu bad for my restaurant website?

As the only menu, yes: it is slow and awkward on phones and does little for search. As a printable extra beside a web page menu, it is fine.

Should I show prices on my online menu?

Yes. Diners use prices to decide where to eat, and a menu without them often sends people elsewhere to check. State whether taxes are included.

Can I update the menu myself?

Yes. On the sites I build, the menu is managed from the WordPress dashboard, so staff can add dishes, change prices or mark items unavailable without a developer.

Does menu schema help my restaurant rank?

Restaurant schema with a link to your menu helps search engines understand your site. At the time of writing, Google doesn't display a special feature for menu markup, so a clear, text-based menu page matters more.

Where should my table QR codes point?

To your menu web page at a fixed address, so you can change the menu without reprinting the codes.
