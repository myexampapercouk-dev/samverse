Websites for Tailoring and Stitching Services: Measurement Guides and Visits, Design Uploads, Alterations, Bridal Orders and a WhatsApp-Led Order Flow

How a tailoring or stitching website should work: design photo uploads, measurement guides and home visits, alteration prices, bridal orders and WhatsApp.

Key takeaways
  • Build the site around the order as it really happens: a reference photo, measurements, your price and date, an advance, a trial fitting and delivery, with WhatsApp carrying the conversation.
  • Offer clear measurement routes, such as a shop visit, a home visit, a well-fitting sample garment or a self-measure guide, and say which garments each one suits.
  • Publish starting prices, extras and alteration rates, state timelines in your own words for normal and wedding-season orders, and give bridal work its own page.

Most tailoring orders still begin with a photo on WhatsApp: a blouse someone saw at a wedding, a screenshot from Instagram, an old kurta that always fitted well. A tailoring website should not fight that habit. Its job is to make the photo, the measurements, the price and the date arrive in one tidy conversation, and to show enough of your finished work that a stranger trusts you with expensive fabric.

For most tailors and stitching studios that is a straightforward WordPress website: service and price pages, a gallery, an upload form, appointment booking and WhatsApp buttons, all editable by you after launch. If you also sell fabrics, stitching kits or made-to-measure pieces paid for online, a WooCommerce store can sit alongside it.

The sections below follow an order through its stages, because each stage needs something different from the site.

Stage one: the customer shows you what they want

A design request is mostly pictures, so make sending pictures easy. A short request form can take:

  • The garment: blouse, salwar suit, lehenga, kurta, shirt, trousers, sherwani or something else
  • Reference images, uploaded straight from the phone gallery, several at once
  • A photo of the fabric, if the customer already has it
  • The occasion and the date it is needed by
  • A WhatsApp number for your reply

Next to the form, add a WhatsApp button whose message is already filled in with the garment type, so customers who prefer chatting can send their photos there instead. Every photo in your gallery can carry a "make one like this" button that does the same with that design named. Phone photos are large; the form should accept them and the site should shrink them before they fill your hosting space.

Keep the WhatsApp side organised too. A business number with labels such as new request, quoted, advance paid, trial and ready lets anyone in the shop see where an order stands. WhatsApp Business for small businesses explains labels, catalogues and quick replies.

Stage two: measurements

Fit is the whole product, and measurement is where tailoring differs from buying clothes online. Offer the routes you genuinely provide, and say which garments each suits:

Measurement routeWorks well forWhat to tell customers
Visit the shopEverything, and essential for bridal and fitted workYour hours, whether to book a slot, and to bring the inner wear and footwear they will wear with the outfit
Home visitBusy customers, elderly customers, families with several ordersThe areas covered, any visit charge, and who visits
A sample garment that fitsRepeat styles such as kurtas, shirts and simple blousesHow to send it, and that it comes back with the order
Self-measurement with your guideLoose or simple garments, and customers in another cityThat fitted blouses measured at home may need an alteration after the trial

If women customers often ask for a woman to take measurements at home or in the shop, and you have one, say so clearly. If you do not, do not imply it.

A measurement guide worth publishing

A page that explains how to take each measurement, with a simple diagram or a short video, saves time even for customers who visit. For a blouse, that usually means bust, under-bust, waist, shoulder width, armhole, sleeve length, sleeve round, front neck depth, back neck depth and blouse length. For a shirt: chest, waist, shoulder, sleeve length, cuff, collar and shirt length. Explain each in one or two sentences: where the tape starts and ends, and whether to measure snug or relaxed.

Returning customers should not have to measure again. Keep measurements in your own records, linked to their phone number, and let them write "same as last time" on the request form. Avoid storing measurements in a public account area unless you have a good reason; a quick lookup by your staff is simpler and safer.

Stage three: your price and your date

Customers hesitate to message a tailor without any idea of price. A "starting from" list for each garment, with the extras that change it, answers most of that hesitation:

  • Base stitching for each garment, simple style
  • Lining, padding and cups for blouses
  • Design work: piping, princess cuts, back designs, tassels, hooks or a zip
  • Hand or machine embroidery, priced after seeing the design
  • Express stitching, if you offer it, with its surcharge

Timelines must come from you, written in your own words: how long a normal order takes, how far ahead to book during the wedding season and before festivals, and when you stop taking orders for a particular date. Put the date question on the request form so you can say no early rather than late. Before Diwali, Eid, Navratri and the wedding months, a banner with your last booking date avoids awkward calls; seasonal and festival campaigns covers planning for peaks.

Confirm the order once an advance is paid by UPI or a payment link, and send the price, delivery date and trial date in one message so nothing is remembered differently later.

Stage four: trial fitting and alterations

State your trial policy on the website: whether a trial is included, how many rounds of correction are free, and what counts as a new job, such as a customer changing the design after cutting. That one paragraph prevents many disagreements.

Alterations deserve their own page, with their own prices. They are frequent, quick and often the first job a new customer gives you before trusting you with a full outfit. List the common ones: shortening or lengthening, taking in or letting out, sleeve changes, zip replacement, saree fall and pico, and resizing ready-made garments. People search for these in plain words, such as "saree fall pico near me" or "jeans alteration in Aundh", so a page that uses the same words can be found.

Bridal and custom orders

A bridal order is a project, not a single stitching job, and often several family members are involved. Give it a separate page that explains:

  1. How to book a consultation, and what to bring: fabric, jewellery photos, references
  2. How design is agreed, whether by sketch, sample or reference images
  3. The advance and payment stages
  4. How many trial fittings are planned, and how close to the wedding the final one should be
  5. Who to contact for changes, so instructions do not arrive from four relatives at once

Bridal clients usually book makeup and mehendi around the same time; if you work with artists you trust, a short list of partners helps them and brings referrals back to you. Show bridal work only with the client's permission, and ask before showing faces. If heavy embroidery such as aari or zardozi is done by partner artisans, say so honestly; customers value the craft whoever does it.

A gallery arranged the way customers ask

Customers rarely ask for "blouses". They ask for a boat neck with a deep back, puff sleeves or a high collar. Arrange the gallery by garment, then by neck design, sleeve style and back design, and label each photo with the fabric and the type of work. Use your own finished pieces, photographed in daylight on a hanger, a form or a willing customer, and keep it current. Preparing photos for your website covers sizes and backgrounds.

If you also sell ready-made pieces, the needs change towards size charts and returns; websites for fashion boutiques covers that side.

Local searches a tailor can win

Tailoring is local. Searches such as "ladies tailor near me", "blouse stitching in Kothrud" or "gents tailor in Wakad" are where new customers come from. Help them find you:

  • Give each main garment and the alteration service its own page, with real photos and starting prices
  • Name the areas you cover for home visits and pickups
  • Fill in every part of your Google Business Profile: tailor as the category, hours, photos of finished work and the website link; the Google Business Profile checklist lists the rest
  • Ask happy customers for reviews after delivery, never in exchange for a discount

FAQs

Can customers upload design photos on my tailoring website?

Yes. A request form can take several images from the phone gallery, along with the garment type, occasion and date, and send them to your email or a shared folder. A WhatsApp button covers customers who prefer to chat.

Should I publish stitching prices?

Publish starting prices for each garment and list the extras that change them. Customers then know roughly what to expect, and you still confirm the final price after seeing the design and fabric.

Can customers book a measurement visit online?

Yes. An appointment form or booking tool can offer shop slots and home visit slots by area, with a reminder sent before the visit. The guide to online appointment booking compares the tools.

Is self-measurement reliable enough?

For loose and simple garments it often is, with a clear guide. For fitted blouses and bridal wear, a measurement by your staff or a sample garment that already fits is safer.

Can I take advance payments for stitching orders?

Yes. You can send a UPI or payment gateway link after agreeing the price, or take the advance on the website, and confirm the order once it is paid.
