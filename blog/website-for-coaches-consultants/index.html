Website for Business, Career and Life Coaches: A Clear Niche, Programme Pages, Discovery Call Booking, Lead Magnets and Honest Proof

How a coaching website turns visitors into discovery calls: a sharp niche, programme pages, easy booking, a lead magnet and claims you can stand behind.

Key takeaways
  • Visitors decide in seconds whether a coach is for them, so the homepage headline should name who you help and the change you help them make.
  • Give each offer its own page with format, duration and who it is not for, and make the discovery call the single next step from every page.
  • Proof works when it is specific and permitted: client stories with consent, talks you have given, and no income or outcome promises.

A coaching website works when a stranger can tell within a few seconds that you help people like them, see exactly what working with you involves, and book a discovery call without sending an email first. Most coaching sites fail on the first point, not the last: the design is fine, but the visitor cannot tell who the coach is for.

Coaches and independent consultants sit in the same family as other professional practices, so the build is close to my websites for consultants and professionals: a profile that earns trust, a page per service, articles and a consultation request on every page. If you sell a cohort or a workshop through ads, a single-purpose landing page for that launch usually beats sending people to the homepage.

Write the niche sentence before anything else

Everything on the site follows from one sentence: who you help, with what, and what changes for them. Try filling in this line:

I help [specific person] who [specific situation] to [specific change], through [format].

"I help first-time engineering managers who were promoted from individual roles to run their teams without working weekends, through 1:1 coaching over three months" tells the right reader they are in the right place, and tells the wrong reader to keep looking. Both outcomes save you time. "I help people unlock their potential" could be any coach in the country.

Once you have the sentence, it becomes the homepage headline, the first line of your About page, your LinkedIn summary and the opening of your Google Business Profile description. If you serve two very different groups, such as corporate teams and individuals, give each a clear route from the homepage rather than blending them into one vague message.

Map your offers, then give each one a page

Most coaches have more than one offer, and visitors get confused when they are all described on one long services page. Lay them out first:

OfferWho it suitsWhat the page must explainNext step
1:1 coachingPeople who want personal attention and flexibilityNumber and length of sessions, duration, support between sessions, online or in personDiscovery call
Group programme or cohortPeople who like structure, peers and a lower price pointStart date, weekly schedule, group size, what each module coversJoin the waitlist or enrol
Self-paced coursePeople not ready for coaching, or on a tight budgetModules, total hours, access period, whether any live support is includedBuy or preview a lesson
Workshops and corporate sessionsHR and L&D teamsTopics, formats, duration, group sizes, how sessions are customisedRequest a proposal
SpeakingEvent organisersTalk topics, past events, audience sizes, travelSpeaking enquiry

If corporate workshops are your main business, the site starts to look more like a training company's; websites for corporate training companies covers that version.

What every programme page needs

  • Who it is for, and who it is not for, in plain words
  • The problem it addresses and what tends to change, described honestly
  • The format: sessions, duration, mode, tools used, homework
  • What is included between sessions: messages, reviews, recordings
  • The investment, or a starting figure, or a clear note that you share fees on the call
  • Questions people ask before signing up, answered on the page
  • One booking button, repeated at the end

The Dr. Sudhir Arora site is a useful reference here. It organises its menu around the challenges visitors bring, with a page for each, and its online course is split into an overview, the method behind it and a separate format and schedule page, so a visitor can check the practical details without wading through the philosophy.

The discovery call is the main conversion

For most coaches, every page should lead to one action: book a short call. Make that as easy as booking a table at a restaurant.

  1. A "Book a free call" button in the header and at the end of every page.
  2. A booking page with an embedded calendar showing real available slots, in the visitor's own time zone if you coach internationally.
  3. Three or four short questions before the slot is confirmed: what they want help with, their current situation, how they heard of you.
  4. An automatic confirmation by email, with the video link and a reminder before the call.
  5. A short "what to expect" note: how long the call is, that it is not a sales pitch, and what they might think about beforehand.

Online appointment booking compares the tools. If you want to filter out people who are not a fit, a paid strategy session instead of a free call is a reasonable option; say clearly what the session includes so it does not feel like a toll gate.

WhatsApp, but not instead of booking

A WhatsApp button helps people who have a quick question about timings or fees. Keep it secondary. If every enquiry arrives as a chat, you end up booking calls by hand and losing track of who asked what.

A lead magnet for people not ready to book

Most visitors will not book on their first visit. A free resource keeps them in touch: a short self-assessment, a checklist, a workbook, a five-day email course or a recorded workshop. The best ones are small and specific, and lead naturally to your paid work. A "Career change readiness check" suits a career coach; "Twelve questions to ask before you hire your first manager" suits a business coach.

The Dr. Sudhir Arora site puts its free self-assessment second in the main menu, right after Home, which shows how central a good free tool can be. Lead magnets and newsletters covers the setup, but in short:

  • Ask only for a name and email to deliver the resource
  • Say what they will receive afterwards and how often
  • Send a short welcome sequence that introduces you and ends with an invitation to book a call
  • Make unsubscribing easy, and keep the list in a tool you control

Speaking and media page

If you speak at events, appear on podcasts or write for publications, a dedicated page does two jobs: it gives organisers what they need, and it reassures potential clients. Include:

  • Two or three talk topics, each with a one-paragraph summary and the audience it suits
  • Events, podcasts and publications you have genuinely appeared in, linked where possible
  • A short and a long bio, and a downloadable headshot
  • A short video clip of you speaking, if you have one
  • A speaking enquiry form that asks for the event date, city, audience and format

Use publication logos only where you appeared in that publication, and check whether the publisher allows its logo to be used.

Proof without exaggeration

Client stories persuade far more than a wall of short quotes, but they need consent and honesty. A simple structure works: where the client was, what you worked on together, what changed, and what they did themselves. Ask permission in writing, agree whether they want their full name, first name or only their role shown, and let them read the story before it goes live. Claims to avoid

RiskySafer
"Clients double their income in 90 days""We work on pricing, positioning and the conversations that lead to higher fees"
"Guaranteed promotion""Preparation for promotion conversations and leadership reviews"
"Cure your anxiety""Tools for managing everyday stress", with a note on when to see a clinician
"Six-figure coach" headlinesYour actual credentials and the work you do

Results depend on the client, and saying so builds credibility rather than weakening it. If your coaching touches health or mental wellbeing, say clearly what it is not. The Dr. Sudhir Arora site, for example, says plainly that its programme does not replace medical or psychiatric treatment, a line every wellbeing coach should consider. Consumer protection and advertising rules on misleading claims apply to coaches too; check the current position if you are unsure.

Your About page is a sales page

Visitors read the About page to decide whether they would enjoy talking to you. Lead with the client, not your biography: what you help with and why you care. Then your story, briefly, with the turning points that explain your approach. Then credentials you have actually earned, such as coaching certifications with the issuing body named, degrees and relevant industry roles. A natural photo matters more here than anywhere else on the site.

Taking payments

Once a client says yes, chasing a bank transfer by message is awkward. A WordPress site can take payments for packages, cohorts and courses through an Indian gateway with UPI, cards and net banking, and can send a receipt automatically. Group programmes and courses can sit behind a login with lessons released on a schedule; online course websites on WordPress covers the options. For 1:1 coaching, a payment link sent after the discovery call is often enough. If you are GST-registered, ask your CA how invoices should be issued for domestic and overseas clients.

Content that brings the right people

Articles answering the questions your clients ask before they hire you, such as "how to prepare for a promotion conversation" or "signs you are ready to leave a corporate job", help you appear in search and show how you think. One useful article a month, linked to the right programme page, does more than daily social posts that disappear. Keep your posting cadence realistic; an article section last updated two years ago suggests the practice is quiet.

FAQs

Do coaches really need a website if they are active on LinkedIn or Instagram?

Social profiles bring attention, but a website is where people check you properly, read the details of each offer and book a call. The two work best together, with every profile linking to the booking page.

Should I show my coaching fees?

Either approach can work. Publishing prices filters out people who cannot afford you; sharing them on the call lets you explain the value first. A starting figure is a reasonable middle ground.

Can I use client testimonials?

Yes, with written permission and wording the client has approved. Stick to their experience and what changed, and avoid income or outcome promises.

Can the site sell a group programme or course?

Yes. WordPress can take payments and deliver lessons to enrolled members, or you can sell through a payment link and deliver the course elsewhere.

How many pages does a coaching website need?

Usually home, about, one page per offer, a booking page, a free resource page and a blog. A speaking page is worth adding if you speak regularly.
