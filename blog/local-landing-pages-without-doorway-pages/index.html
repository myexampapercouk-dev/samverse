Local Landing Pages Done Right: City and Area Pages That Rank Without Becoming Doorway Pages

Local landing pages done right: Google's doorway page policy, unique content for each city or area page, when not to build them and how to fix them.

Key takeaways
  • A city or area page is fine when it covers a place you genuinely serve and says things true only of that place; near-identical pages with the city name swapped are doorway pages under Google's spam policies.
  • Each page needs real local substance: the branch or team that covers it, local jobs and reviews, coverage and response times, and answers to questions customers there actually ask.
  • If you have nothing local to say, one strong service page with a clear list of areas beats a thin page per city, and existing doorway pages are best merged and redirected.

Local landing pages are safe, and useful, when each one is about a place you genuinely serve and contains things that are only true of that place: the branch or team that covers it, jobs you've done there, local reviews, coverage and response times. They become doorway pages when they're near-identical copies with the city name swapped, built to catch searches and send everyone to the same place. Google's spam policies treat doorways as spam, so a set of thin city pages can hold back the pages themselves and the site around them.

Planning which location pages to build, how to structure and link them, and which schema to add is part of my WordPress SEO services. This guide sets out the rules so you can judge your own pages.

It covers the principles for any city, area or locality page. If you have several branches, also read how to structure a multi-location business website. If you travel to customers and have no shopfront, SEO for service-area businesses covers the Business Profile side.

What Google's doorway page policy says

In its spam policies, Google describes doorways as sites or pages created to rank for specific, similar searches, which lead users to essentially the same destination. The examples Google gives include pages targeted at particular cities or regions that funnel visitors to one page, and substantially similar pages that look more like search results than a clear, browsable structure.

Google also has a policy against scaled content abuse: producing large numbers of pages mainly to manipulate rankings, however they're made, including with AI tools. Bulk city pages generated from a template are exactly what both policies describe.

The consequences range from Google simply indexing one of the near-duplicates and ignoring the rest, to a manual action shown in Search Console's Manual actions report. Either way, the pages don't do the job you built them for.

When a location page makes sense

  • You have a branch, office, clinic or showroom there. A page per real location is expected, and it pairs with that branch's Google Business Profile.
  • You genuinely serve the area and have local work to show: projects, clients, reviews.
  • Something about the service changes there: different coverage, response times, charges, local conditions or rules.
  • People search for it. Search Console queries that include the area name, or Keyword Planner data, show whether a page would answer a real search.

When not to build one

  • You could serve the place but haven't, and have nothing local to say about it
  • The only difference between pages would be the name of the city
  • The area is a neighbourhood next to your main location, already covered by your main page and Business Profile
  • You sell online across India with no local element, such as delivery from one warehouse; a page per city adds nothing for the buyer
  • You have fifty localities in mind; start with the two or three that bring the most work and do them properly
  • Nobody will keep the pages updated when branches, numbers or teams change

The alternatives often work better: one strong service page with a clear list of the areas you cover, service areas set in your Google Business Profile, and location-focused articles where you have genuine insight. For example, the site I built for Studio Agama Interiors, a Hyderabad interior design studio, includes a blog with location-focused articles for Hyderabad, Vizag and Vijayawada, the cities the studio serves.

What to put on a local landing page

  1. An opening that answers the local question: do you serve this area, how, and how quickly?
  2. Who covers it: the branch address, map and hours if customers visit, or the team and base that serve the area if you travel to them.
  3. Local proof: projects completed there with photos (with the customer's permission, and no house numbers), and genuine reviews from local customers.
  4. Coverage details: localities or pin codes covered, typical response or travel times, and any visiting charge.
  5. Local conditions: anything that changes the work there, such as building types, water quality, society rules on working hours, or local permissions.
  6. Area-specific FAQs: parking, timings, which branch to visit, whether you cover a nearby town.
  7. A local call to action: the number or WhatsApp that actually reaches the people serving that area.

How much unique content is enough?

There's no word count. The test is whether a customer in that area learns something they wouldn't learn from your main service page. If the local parts are one sentence inside a page of shared text, it's a doorway. If the local parts are most of the page, and the shared material is a short summary with a link to the main service page, you're fine.

URLs, internal links, schema and hreflang

  • URLs: pick one consistent pattern, such as /locations/pune/ or /ac-repair/pune/. Be wary of every combination of service and city: ten services in twenty cities is two hundred pages, and very few businesses have something unique to say on each.
  • Internal links: link location pages from an "Areas we serve" page and from the relevant service pages, with plain anchor text. A footer packed with city links is a classic doorway pattern.
  • Canonicals: each location page should point its canonical tag at itself, not at the main service page. If two pages are so similar you'd want to canonicalise one to the other, merge them instead; see duplicate content explained.
  • Schema: use LocalBusiness markup with the address on pages for real branches. For areas without an address, describe coverage with areaServed in your Organization or Service markup rather than inventing addresses; see schema markup explained.
  • Hreflang isn't needed: it tells Google about language or country versions of the same page. A Pune page and a Nashik page in English aren't alternate versions of each other, so they don't need it. It only matters if you also publish Hindi or Marathi versions; see hreflang for Indian websites.

Building location pages in WordPress without making doorways

Use a template for the structure, not the words. A custom post type for locations, with custom fields for the address, hours, areas covered, team, projects and reviews, keeps every page consistent and makes the gaps obvious: if a location has no projects or reviews to fill the fields, it isn't ready to publish. See custom post types and custom fields for how that works.

Avoid plugins that generate hundreds of pages from a list of city names or spun text. They're quick, and they produce exactly what Google's policies describe.

How to fix existing doorway pages

  1. List every location page and check each one in Search Console's Performance report: impressions, clicks and the queries it appears for.
  2. Keep and improve the few that get impressions and cover places you genuinely serve. Add the local substance described above.
  3. Merge the rest into the main service page or a single "Areas we serve" page listing your coverage.
  4. Redirect retired URLs with 301s to the most relevant remaining page, not all to the homepage, and remove them from your sitemap.
  5. If you have a manual action, fix every affected page first, then request reconsideration in Search Console explaining what you changed.

FAQs

What is a doorway page in SEO?

A page built mainly to rank for a particular search, often one of many near-identical pages targeting different cities or keyword variations, which sends visitors on to the same place. Google's spam policies treat doorways as spam.

Are city pages considered doorway pages?

Not automatically. A city page for a real branch, or an area you serve with genuine local content, is a normal and useful page. It becomes a doorway when it's one of a set of copies with only the place name changed.

How many location pages should I create?

As many as you have real locations, plus areas where you have genuine local work and something specific to say. For most small businesses that means a handful, not dozens. Start with your busiest two or three areas.

How much unique content does a location page need?

Enough that the local information is the main part of the page: who serves the area, local projects and reviews, coverage, timings and local questions. There's no minimum word count; a short page with real local detail beats a long templated one.

Do I need hreflang for city pages?

No. Hreflang is for language and country versions of the same page. City pages in the same language for the same country don't need it.

Can I use AI to write location pages?

As a drafting aid, carefully. The facts that make a location page useful, such as local jobs, reviews and coverage, have to come from you, and mass-producing pages with AI falls under Google's scaled content abuse policy.
