Website Design Contract Checklist: Scope, Revisions, Payments, Ownership, Support and Everything Else to Agree Before Work Starts

A website design contract checklist: scope, content, revisions, timeline, payment milestones, ownership, hosting, support, NDAs and ending the project.

Key takeaways
  • A website contract or proposal should name every page and feature, list what is excluded, and say who supplies content and how many revision rounds are included.
  • Payment milestones, change requests, ownership of the site and accounts, the support period and how either side can end the project cause most disputes, so write them down.
  • It does not need to be long, but it should be in writing and accepted by both sides; have a lawyer review anything high-value or unusual.

A website design contract should set out exactly what is being built (named pages, features, integrations and what's excluded), who supplies the content, how many rounds of revisions are included, the timeline and what pauses it, the payment milestones, who owns the finished site and its accounts, what's tested before launch, what support follows, and how either side can end the project. It doesn't have to be a long legal document. A clear proposal that both sides accept in writing covers most small projects, and anything high-value or unusual is worth a lawyer's review.

When you hire me as your WordPress developer, every project starts this way: a written scope listing the pages or tasks, the features, the revision rounds and anything excluded, with a fixed price and a timeline, before any advance is paid. Agencies outsourcing white-label work need a few extra clauses, covered below and on the WordPress developer for agencies page.

This checklist works for any developer or agency, and the points are practical rather than legal advice. A good starting point is a clear website brief, and if you're still choosing who to hire, how to compare website quotes helps first.

Proposal, contract or statement of work?

The names vary, but the jobs are the same:

  • Proposal or quotation: what will be built and what it costs.
  • Scope or statement of work: the detailed list of pages, features and deliverables.
  • Terms or agreement: payments, ownership, changes, support and cancellation.

For a small business site, a single document that covers all three, accepted by both sides by signature or in writing, is common. Larger projects often split them. What matters is that every point below is written down somewhere both sides have agreed to.

Scope: what is and isn't being built

Most website disputes come from scope. Name every page instead of giving a number, list each feature by what it does, and write down what's excluded, because exclusions are where expectations differ most. A plain-language scope can look like this:

SCOPE OF WORK (example)
Pages: Home, About, one page for each of 4 services, Contact, Privacy policy
Templates: blog post and blog archive
Features: enquiry form emailing two addresses, WhatsApp button, Google Map
Content: client supplies all text and photos; developer formats and polishes
Revisions: two rounds on the homepage design, one round on inner pages
Launch: on hosting registered in the client's name
Not included: copywriting from scratch, photography, logo design,
hosting and domain fees, premium licences, ongoing SEO

For a store, add the number of products the developer will upload, variations, payment methods such as UPI, cards and cash on delivery, shipping rules and invoice needs. For a redesign, add the redirect plan for old URLs.

Content, revisions and approvals

Content readiness is often the biggest factor in how long a project takes, so the contract should say who writes each page, who supplies photos and product data, and the date they're due.

Define a revision round: one consolidated list of feedback, from one decision-maker, on a specific stage such as the homepage design. Say how many rounds are included and how extra rounds or late changes of direction are charged. Agree how approvals are given, such as a written reply by email or WhatsApp, so there's a record of each sign-off.

Timeline and what pauses it

Tie the timeline to milestones (design approved, build ready for review, launch) rather than fixed calendar dates, and state the dependencies: content, feedback, access to hosting and accounts, and any third-party approvals such as a payment gateway. Agree what happens if the project stalls on either side, for example that the schedule moves by the length of the delay, or that a project paused for a long time is rescheduled. Payment terms

  • The total and what it includes, matched line by line to the scope.
  • Taxes, such as whether GST is added, if the developer is registered; ask your CA if you're unsure how it applies.
  • The schedule, such as an advance to start, a milestone payment and the balance before launch, tied to deliverables rather than dates.
  • Third-party costs paid separately: domain, hosting, premium themes and plugins, stock photos, paid integrations.
  • Late payment, and whether launch waits for the final payment.

Fair payment terms for a website project covers advances, milestones and refunds in detail.

Change requests

New ideas during a project are normal. The contract should say how they're handled: the request is made in writing, the developer replies with a price and any effect on the timeline, and work starts only once you approve it. It should also distinguish a change from a defect, because fixing something that doesn't work as agreed is part of the job, while adding something new isn't. Bug or change request? explains where the line usually sits.

Ownership, intellectual property and accounts

This is the clause that matters most if the relationship ends badly.

  • Who owns what, and when. Many agreements transfer ownership of the design, content and custom work to the client once the final payment is made.
  • Third-party software. WordPress and most themes and plugins come under their own licences, often the GPL, so you own your content, design and custom code rather than the software underneath.
  • What the developer keeps, such as their own reusable code, tools and the right to show the work in a portfolio, unless you agree otherwise.
  • Accounts in your name: the domain, hosting, Google accounts and premium licences, bought with your business email. The website ownership checklist lists each one.

How intellectual property passes from one party to another depends on the wording and the law that applies to your agreement, so this is the clause most worth a lawyer's eye.

For Shopify projects, agree who removes the store password and when; removing the Shopify password page and launching safely lists the checks to run first.

Testing, launch and acceptance

Write down what will be tested before launch: pages on phones and desktops, the browsers that matter, every form with emails confirmed as arriving, test orders for a store, and speed. Then define "complete": for example, the site is live on your hosting, the agreed scope is delivered and you've approved it in writing. A short acceptance period, during which you report anything that doesn't match the scope, avoids arguments later.

After launch: bug fixes, support and maintenance

  • A bug-fix period: how long it lasts, and that it covers defects in the agreed work, not new features or problems caused by later changes made by someone else.
  • Training and handover: a walkthrough, documentation, every login and a full backup; the website handover checklist lists what to receive.
  • Maintenance: optional and usually separate, with what's included and how it's billed.

Confidentiality, NDAs and white-label work

If you're sharing business plans, customer data or unreleased products, a confidentiality clause or a separate NDA is sensible. For agencies outsourcing to a developer, add the white-label terms: the developer doesn't contact your client unless you ask, adds no credits to the site, and the agency owns the client relationship. White-label WordPress development explains how those projects usually run.

Ending the project early

Agree in advance how either side can stop: the notice required, payment for work completed so far, what happens to any advance for work not yet started, and the handover of files, backups and access. A clean exit clause protects both sides, and it's far easier to agree at the start than in the middle of a disagreement.

The small print

Larger agreements usually also cover which law applies, how disputes are handled (often by talking first, then mediation or arbitration), any limit on liability, and who is responsible for the legal content of the site, such as the privacy policy. These are legal questions, so have a lawyer review them for anything high-value.

Red flags in a website proposal

  • A price with no list of pages or features.
  • No mention of whose name the domain and hosting will be in.
  • "Unlimited revisions", which usually means no clear end point.
  • Full payment up front for a new project.
  • Premium themes or plugins included free, which can mean pirated copies.
  • No mention of what happens after launch.

FAQs

Do I need a formal contract for a small website?

You need the points agreed in writing, but not necessarily a long legal document. A detailed proposal that covers scope, payments, ownership and support, accepted by both sides, is often enough for a small project.

Is an agreement over email or WhatsApp good enough?

Written agreements by email are widely used for small projects, and they're far better than a verbal understanding. Whether a particular exchange would hold up in a dispute depends on the law that applies, so for larger projects use a signed agreement and ask a lawyer.

Who should write the contract, me or the developer?

Usually the developer sends a proposal or standard terms. Read them against this checklist, ask for anything missing to be added, and don't sign until the scope matches your brief.

Should hosting and maintenance be in the same contract?

They can be, but they're usually separate: the build is a one-off project, while hosting and maintenance run month to month or year to year. Keep the hosting account in your name either way.

Is it normal for the developer to keep ownership until the final payment?

Yes, many agreements work that way. What matters is that the domain and hosting are in your name from the start, and that ownership of the site passes to you once you've paid in full.
