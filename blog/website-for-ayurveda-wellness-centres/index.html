Websites for Ayurveda and Wellness Centres: Day Treatments and Residential Retreats, Panchakarma Programmes, BAMS Credentials, Careful Claims and International Guests

Plan an Ayurveda or Panchakarma centre website: therapies, day treatments and retreats, BAMS credentials, claims that stay within the rules, and bookings.

Key takeaways
  • Separate day treatments from residential programmes, because a local patient booking a consultation and an overseas guest planning a retreat need different pages.
  • State doctors' BAMS and postgraduate degrees with university, year and registration, and describe each therapy and programme in concrete terms.
  • Avoid cure claims and disease lists, check the current advertising rules for Indian systems of medicine, and keep visa and medical advice off the booking pages.

On the same morning, a neighbour with a stiff back and a guest in Germany planning a three-week Panchakarma stay may both open your website. The Ayurveda sites that work give each of them a clear path, describe therapies and programmes in concrete terms, state the doctors' qualifications exactly, and avoid the cure claims that both the law and careful readers distrust.

The clinical side is built much like the websites I make for doctors and clinics, with doctor profiles, treatment pages, an appointment enquiry form, WhatsApp and local SEO. The residential side borrows from hotel website design: room pages, galleries, inclusions, policies and a booking or enquiry flow for stays. Deciding early how much of each your centre needs shapes everything else.

Day patients and retreat guests: two journeys in one site

Day treatment patientResidential guest
Usually livesIn the same city or nearbyAnother city, state or country
BooksA consultation, then a course of therapiesA programme of a fixed length, including stay and meals
First questionsWho is the doctor, what does it involve, timings and feesWhat is included, the daily routine, rooms, food, transfers and the total cost
DecidesWithin daysOver weeks or months, often comparing several centres
Main call to actionBook a consultationRequest a programme plan or check dates

Put both choices on the homepage and keep the paths separate after that. A guest from abroad does not need your clinic's evening OPD timings, and a local patient does not need airport transfer details.

Therapies explained in concrete terms

Most Ayurveda sites list Sanskrit therapy names with a line of poetry each. Visitors, especially first-timers and international guests, want to know what will actually happen. For each therapy you offer, answer the same practical questions:

TherapyWhat the guest experiencesQuestions the page should answer
AbhyangaA full-body massage with warm medicated oil, usually by one or two therapistsHow long it lasts, therapist gender, what to wear, whether to bathe afterwards
ShirodharaA steady stream of warm oil or other liquid poured over the forehead while lying downHow it feels, how many sessions are usual, who should not have it
SwedanaHerbal steam, often in a steam box with the head outsideHeat tolerance, blood pressure and other conditions to mention first
PanchakarmaA doctor-supervised programme of preparation, cleansing procedures and recovery over several days or weeksWhich procedures are planned and why, diet during the programme, rest, and what happens after leaving

Add a note on each page that the doctor decides which therapies suit a person after consultation, and list situations where a guest should mention their health first, such as pregnancy, heart conditions or recent surgery, as your doctors advise.

Programmes and packages

Residential programmes are where most of the revenue, and most of the questions, sit. Each programme page should cover:

  • Who it is designed for, written in terms of wellbeing goals rather than diseases
  • Length options and the minimum stay the doctors recommend for it
  • What is included: consultations, number and type of therapies, medicines used during the stay, meals, yoga and meditation sessions, room type, laundry and transfers
  • What is extra: additional therapies, medicines to take home, outings, single occupancy
  • A typical day, from early yoga to evening rest, so guests can picture the routine
  • The diet, and whether it can accommodate allergies or Jain food
  • House rules that matter, such as no alcohol or limited phone use, stated plainly before booking

Whether to publish prices is your decision, but guests from abroad compare total costs and rarely enquire without some idea. "From" prices per programme and room type, with what they include, save a long email exchange.

Doctors' credentials, stated precisely

Credentials matter as much in Ayurveda as in any other medical practice, and vague phrases such as "renowned Vaidya" do little for a sceptical reader. For each doctor, give the degree, such as BAMS, with the university and year; any postgraduate degree, such as MD or MS in Ayurveda, with its subject; registration with the state board or council of Indian medicine, and the national register where applicable; and the therapies or programmes they lead. Therapists deserve short profiles too, with their training and where they received it.

On the Dr. Sudhir Arora site, the doctor's degree, college, year and registration number appear on the homepage, and the About page states plainly what the programme is not a substitute for. An Ayurveda centre gains from the same two habits: checkable credentials and clear limits.

Mention accreditation only if you hold it. NABH, for example, runs accreditation programmes for AYUSH hospitals and for wellness centres; if you are accredited, show the certificate and its validity.

Claims: what the law and your readers expect

This is where many Ayurveda websites get into trouble. Indian law restricts advertisements claiming to treat or cure a list of diseases and conditions, and practitioners of Indian systems of medicine work under their own professional conduct rules on advertising. Because the detail and its enforcement keep shifting, read the current law and regulator guidance, or ask a lawyer, before anything goes live. A simple test for every sentence on the site helps:

  1. Does it promise a cure, a guaranteed result or a fixed timescale? If so, remove it.
  2. Does it name a serious disease as something your therapy treats? If so, rewrite it around wellbeing, or around the consultation where the doctor will advise.
  3. Would a patient read it as a reason to stop prescribed medicine? If so, add the opposite advice explicitly.

Describe what a therapy involves and how guests commonly describe the experience, such as feeling rested, and leave outcomes to the consultation. Testimonials need the same care: share them with written consent, and avoid stories that amount to cure claims. If you also sell oils and formulations, the product pages carry their own rules; websites for Ayurvedic and herbal product brands covers labels and claims. International guests

Overseas guests plan further ahead and ask more practical questions. A dedicated page, or section of each programme page, should cover:

  • The nearest airport, transfer arrangements and how long the journey takes
  • Prices in rupees with a note on how international cards are charged, or prices in another currency if your payment setup supports it
  • The seasons you recommend and why, including the monsoon months that many centres promote as a traditional time for treatment
  • Languages your doctors and therapists speak
  • What to pack, Wi-Fi, laundry and nearby medical facilities
  • Visas: India has introduced a visa category for foreign nationals travelling for treatment under AYUSH systems, but rules change, so link to the official Indian visa website and avoid giving visa advice yourself

If many guests come from one language group, a translated version of the key pages, written or checked by a fluent speaker, helps; multilingual WordPress websites explains how to set it up so search engines show the right version.

Booking: consultation first, then dates

For day patients, a consultation booking form with name, phone, preferred date and session, and a brief reason is enough. For residential programmes, a three-step flow works better:

  1. An enquiry with dates, programme of interest, number of guests and room preference
  2. A pre-arrival consultation by phone or video, where the doctor confirms the programme suits the guest
  3. Confirmation with an advance payment, your cancellation and refund terms, and a pre-arrival information sheet

Health questionnaires collect sensitive personal data, so ask for them after the enquiry, through a secure form or the consultation itself, rather than on the public enquiry form. Advances can be collected through a payment gateway or a payment link; accepting online payments in India covers the options, including international cards.

Photos and design that match the experience

Calm design fits the subject, but rich imagery can make a site slow, which hurts guests on mobile data abroad as much as at home. Use real photos of treatment rooms, the dining area, rooms and grounds, compressed properly, and a short video of the daily routine. Yoga is part of many programmes; if you also run regular classes for local students, websites for yoga studios and teachers covers timetables and batches.

FAQs

Can an Ayurveda centre's website say it cures diseases?

It should not. Indian law restricts cure claims for many diseases and conditions, and professional conduct rules restrict advertising. Describe therapies and leave outcomes to the doctor's consultation.

Should we show Panchakarma package prices?

Showing "from" prices for each programme and room type, with inclusions listed, helps guests compare and reduces long email exchanges. Exact pricing remains your decision.

How should doctors' qualifications appear?

Give the degree with university and year, any postgraduate degree and its subject, and registration details, on each doctor's profile.

Can international guests book and pay online?

Yes. An enquiry, a pre-arrival consultation and an advance paid through a gateway that accepts international cards is a common flow.

Should the website help guests with visas?

Link to the official Indian visa website and describe what documents you can provide, such as a booking confirmation, but avoid giving visa advice.
