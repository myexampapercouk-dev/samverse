Websites for Logistics and Transport Companies: FTL, Part Load, Warehousing and Last Mile, Fleet and Coverage, Shipment Tracking, Quote Requests and Driver Recruitment

Plan a logistics or transport company website: FTL, part load, warehousing and last mile pages, lanes, fleet, tracking, quote forms and driver jobs.

Key takeaways
  • Give each service its own page, FTL, part load, warehousing, last mile, because each is bought by different people who ask different questions.
  • Show coverage as the lanes and regions you actually run, describe owned and attached fleet honestly, and connect tracking in whatever way your system supports.
  • Use a quote form that captures origin, destination, load and timing, and treat driver recruitment as a second audience with its own simple, mobile-first page.

A logistics or transport company website has two jobs: convince business shippers that you can move their goods reliably on the lanes they need, and collect enough detail in a quote request for your team to price the load. A third, often neglected, is hiring drivers. The sites that work show services, coverage and fleet in specific terms rather than in general claims about being "pan-India".

A transport company sells to procurement and supply chain teams in much the same way a manufacturer does, so the planning resembles the B2B websites I build for manufacturers: a company profile, service pages that read like a specification, request-a-quote forms, downloadable profiles and industries-served pages. The build itself is a standard business site on WordPress, of the kind covered under WordPress website development, with forms and integrations added where your operations need them.

Services: a page for each way you move goods

"Transport solutions" on a homepage tells a shipper nothing. Separate the services, because each one has a different buyer:

ServiceTypical buyerWhat they need to seeExtra quote fields
Full truck load (FTL)Manufacturers, distributors, project cargoVehicle types and payloads, lanes, transit times as ranges, loading and unloading termsVehicle type, number of trucks, dates, loading point
Part load (PTL or LTL)Smaller manufacturers, traders, e-commerce sellersMinimum load, how consolidation works, pick-up and delivery options, transit rangesWeight, volume, number of packages, packaging type
WarehousingBrands and distributors needing storage near marketsLocation, area, racking, handling, inventory reporting, securitySpace required, duration, goods type, monthly throughput
Last mile deliveryD2C brands, retailers, B2B suppliers within a cityAreas covered, delivery windows, proof of delivery, cash collection if offeredDaily volumes, areas, parcel sizes
Specialised transportPharma, chemicals, ODC or temperature-controlled loadsEquipment, handling procedures and any approvals you actually holdCargo type, temperature range, hazard class

If cold storage or warehousing is the larger part of your business, websites for cold storage and warehousing deals with storage-specific pages. Household relocations are a different market with different questions; websites for packers and movers covers that.

Coverage: lanes and regions, not a map with pins everywhere

A map of India covered in dots invites scepticism. Shippers want to know whether you regularly run their lane. Be specific:

  • List your main lanes, such as Pune to Delhi NCR or Chennai to Bengaluru, with typical transit ranges and service frequency
  • Name the branches and hubs you operate yourselves, with addresses
  • Separate regions you serve directly from those you serve through partners
  • For export and import cargo, say which ports and inland container depots you work with

A page for each major lane can work well if each has genuine content: road route, typical transit, vehicle types used and the industries that ship on it. Avoid generating hundreds of near-identical city pages; they rarely help and can count against the site.

Fleet: what you own and what you arrange

Many transporters run a mix of owned and attached vehicles. There is nothing wrong with that, and saying so plainly builds trust; claiming a large owned fleet that turns out to be brokered loses a client at the first problem. On a fleet page, show:

  • Vehicle types by payload and body, such as small commercial vehicles, 19 ft and 32 ft containers, open trailers and reefers
  • Which types you own and which you arrange through partners
  • GPS tracking and any other equipment fitted, described as it is
  • Real photographs of your vehicles, depots and warehouses

Organising the fleet by capacity mirrors how buyers specify a load. The Sahni Power Solutions website does the same for generator hire, with a page for each capacity band that lists typical uses and what affects the price. A transporter can apply the idea to vehicle types: what each carries, which goods suit it, and what changes the rate.

Shipment tracking: connect it the way your system allows

Clients expect to check a consignment without calling. How this works on the website depends on the transport management system or tracking provider you use:

  1. A link to your tracking portal, prominently placed in the header, which is simple and works with almost any system.
  2. A lookup box on the website that sends the docket or LR number to the system and shows the status, where the provider offers a way to do that.
  3. Automatic updates by email, SMS or WhatsApp at key milestones, sent by the operations system rather than the website.

Ask your software provider what it supports before planning the page; I can build the website side once that is known. Whatever the method, explain what each status means, and give a phone or WhatsApp number for exceptions.

The quote request your team can price

A quote form that asks only for name and phone generates calls that start with "What do you want to send?". Ask for what the pricing team needs, in a few short steps:

  1. Service: FTL, part load, warehousing, last mile or other
  2. Origin and destination, with pin codes
  3. Goods description, weight, volume and packaging
  4. Vehicle type for FTL, if known
  5. Dates, and whether this is one-off or regular
  6. Special needs: fragile, hazardous, temperature-controlled, loading help
  7. Company name, contact person, phone, email and GSTIN for regular business

Breaking the form into steps keeps it from feeling long on a phone. Multi-step forms for lead qualification shows how, and RFQ forms for B2B websites covers routing enquiries to the right person.

Corporate clients and proof you can show

Business buyers want to know who else trusts you. Show client logos and named references only with permission, because many shippers do not allow it. Where you cannot name clients, describe the work instead: the industry, the lanes, the kind of loads and how you handled a specific challenge, such as a seasonal peak or a plant shutdown. Industry pages for manufacturing, FMCG, e-commerce and pharma help buyers see their own needs reflected.

Business details in the footer, such as registered name, CIN, GST number and branch addresses, are part of the proof. So is insurance: explain the cover available for goods in transit and how claims work, in general terms, and refer to the contract for specifics.

Paperwork, in general terms

Shippers often ask about e-way bills, GST on freight, documentation for interstate movement and liability for damage. A short guide page helps, as long as it stays general and points to the current official rules, because tax and transport regulations change. Do not promise compliance outcomes; describe what your team does and what the shipper must provide.

Careers for drivers: a second audience

Driver shortages are a recurring problem in Indian trucking, and the website can help with hiring if it is built for the people you want to reach. Drivers usually browse on phones, often in Hindi or a regional language, and are more likely to send a WhatsApp message than fill in a long form. A good careers page:

  • Is available in the languages your drivers speak
  • Lists the roles: long-haul drivers, local drivers, helpers, loaders, warehouse staff and office roles
  • States the licence type and experience needed for each
  • Describes what you offer factually: routes, rest arrangements, payment schedule, insurance or allowances, as they actually are
  • Offers a WhatsApp button and a short form with name, phone, location, licence type and experience

Keep office and management roles on the same page but separate, perhaps with a link to LinkedIn. LinkedIn and B2B website traffic also explains how a company page can bring shippers to the site.

FAQs

Should a transport company list its rates on the website?

Most do not, because rates depend on lane, load, vehicle and fuel. Explain what affects the price and use a quote form that captures those details instead.

Can the website show live shipment tracking?

Often, yes, depending on your tracking system. At minimum, link to your tracking portal from every page; where the system allows, a lookup box can show status on your own site.

Do we need a page for every city we serve?

No. Create pages for your main lanes and hubs, each with specific information, and handle other routes through the quote form.

Should we say which vehicles are owned and which are attached?

Being open about it builds trust. Show the vehicle types you can provide and which you own, and describe how you manage partner vehicles.

Can drivers apply through the website?

Yes. A short mobile form and a WhatsApp button work best, with the page available in the languages your drivers use.
