How to Transfer Your Domain to Another Registrar Without Downtime (EPP Code, Locks and DNS)

How to transfer a domain to another registrar safely: unlock it, get the EPP code, avoid the 60-day lock and keep DNS and email working, including .in domains.

Key takeaways
  • A transfer changes the company you pay to register the domain; your website and email stay online as long as the DNS records keep working throughout.
  • Copy every DNS record and prepare it first, then unlock the domain, get the EPP code and start the transfer at the new registrar.
  • Check eligibility before you start: registrars can refuse a transfer soon after registration, a previous transfer or an owner change, and close to expiry.

To transfer a domain to another registrar: check it's eligible (usually more than 60 days since it was registered or last transferred), copy all its DNS records, unlock it at your current registrar, get the authorisation (EPP) code, then start the transfer at the new registrar and approve it. For .com and similar domains, a transfer usually completes within about five to seven days, and your website and email stay online as long as the DNS records keep working throughout.

People move domains for better renewal prices, a cleaner dashboard, all their services in one place, or to get a domain out of an old developer's account. Below is the full process with the checks that prevent downtime. If you're moving hosting or email at the same time, that's the part that usually goes wrong, and it's what my WordPress migration service handles: DNS and email records, the site itself and a tested switch-over.

Transferring a domain vs changing nameservers

These are often confused, and you may not need a transfer at all:

Domain transferChanging nameservers or DNS
What changesThe company you pay to register and renew the domainWhere the domain points: website, email and other services
Typical reasonPrice, a better dashboard, consolidating accounts, recovering controlMoving hosting, using Cloudflare, changing email provider
Needs an EPP code?YesNo

If you only want to move your website to a new host, you can usually point the domain at it and leave the registration where it is. Before you start: eligibility checks

  • The 60-day lock. At the time of writing, ICANN's transfer rules let registrars refuse a transfer within 60 days of registration or of a previous transfer, and many also lock a domain for 60 days after a change to the owner's name, organisation or email. ICANN has been updating its transfer policy, so check your registrar's current rules.
  • The expiry date. Don't leave it to the last few days. A domain that expires mid-transfer can get stuck, and one already past expiry may need renewing first; see domain name expired? what to do.
  • Who owns it. Look the domain up in ICANN's lookup tool (lookup.icann.org) or any WHOIS lookup to see the registrar and its status. If it's registered in a previous developer's or employee's name, sort that out first.
  • The contact email. Codes and approvals go to the registrant or account email, so make sure it's an address you can receive, not one at a domain that no longer works.
  • Disputes and unpaid bills. A domain under a legal dispute, or with an unpaid balance at the registrar, can be refused.

Step 1: Copy your DNS records and prepare them

This is the step that prevents downtime. Your domain's nameservers show where its DNS is managed:

  • DNS at your host or Cloudflare (nameservers such as ns1.yourhost.com or name.ns.cloudflare.com): the transfer doesn't touch it. Keep the same nameservers throughout.
  • DNS at the current registrar (its default nameservers): the zone may be deleted when the domain leaves. Recreate every record at the new registrar or an independent DNS provider and switch nameservers before the transfer, or move DNS to your host or Cloudflare first.

Either way, export or screenshot every record before you start:

RecordWhat it doesWhat breaks if it's missing
A / AAAAPoints the domain to your web serverThe website
CNAMEPoints names such as www to another addresswww, subdomains and some services
MXSays which server receives your emailIncoming email
TXT (SPF, DKIM, DMARC)Authorises who may send email as your domainOutgoing email lands in spam or bounces
TXT (verification)Proves ownership to Google, Microsoft and othersSearch Console and other verified services

If DNSSEC is switched on, turn it off, or move its DS record correctly, before changing nameservers; a mismatched DNSSEC record makes the domain stop resolving altogether. SPF, DKIM and DMARC explained covers the email records in more detail.

Step 2: Unlock the domain

In your current registrar's dashboard, turn off the transfer lock, sometimes called registrar lock or theft protection. A lookup will then stop showing the status clientTransferProhibited. Some registrars also ask you to turn off WHOIS privacy before a transfer; many no longer do.

Step 3: Get the authorisation (EPP) code

Request the authorisation code, also called the EPP code, auth code or transfer key. Some registrars show it in the dashboard; others email it to the registrant address. Treat it like a password: anyone with the code and an unlocked domain can start a transfer.

Step 4: Start the transfer at the new registrar

At the new registrar, choose transfer (not a new registration), enter the domain and the code, and pay the transfer fee. For .com and most similar extensions, the fee includes a one-year extension added to your existing expiry date, so you don't lose time you've already paid for. If the domain auto-renewed at the old registrar very recently, ask whether that year carries over.

The fee is usually similar to a year's renewal. My WordPress website cost guide puts yearly .com and .in domain costs at roughly ₹500 to ₹1,200, but registrar prices change, so compare the renewal price rather than just the transfer offer.

Step 5: Approve and wait

You may get an email asking you to confirm, from the new registrar, the old one or both. Approve it. For .com and other generic domains, the old registrar has up to five days to act, after which the transfer normally goes through on its own; many registrars let you approve it early from their dashboard. Don't change DNS or contact details while the transfer is in progress.

Step 6: Check everything afterwards

  • The website loads over HTTPS, on both www and non-www.
  • Email sends and receives, including to Gmail, without landing in spam.
  • The nameservers and DNS records are what you expect.
  • Auto-renew is on, with a payment method that won't expire soon.
  • The transfer lock is back on.
  • The registrant is your business, with a contact email you'll still have in five years.

Transferring a .in domain

.in domains are run by NIXI, the National Internet Exchange of India, and sold through NIXI-accredited registrars, so the new registrar must be accredited for .in. The process is the same in outline: unlock, get the auth code, start the transfer at the new registrar and approve it. Details such as lock periods, whether a year is added and how long approval takes are set by the .in registry and can differ from .com, so confirm them with the new registrar before you pay. The same goes for .co.in and other second-level .in names. .in vs .com covers .in registration rules and ownership.

How to keep a domain transfer secure

  • Request the EPP code yourself from the registrar's own dashboard, never through a link in an unexpected email.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication at both registrars before you start.
  • Ignore transfer and renewal notices from companies you don't use; many are sales tactics or scams. See domain renewal and SEO scam emails.
  • Keep the domain in your business's own registrar account rather than a developer's or agency's, and give others access as users where the registrar allows it.
  • Turn the transfer lock back on as soon as the transfer completes.

FAQs

How long does a domain transfer take?

For .com and other generic domains, usually about five to seven days, and often faster if you approve it at the old registrar. Other extensions, including .in, follow their own registry's rules, so check with the new registrar.

Will my website or email go down during a domain transfer?

Not if DNS is prepared first. If your nameservers stay the same, or every record is recreated before the switch, the website and email keep working throughout. Downtime happens when DNS lived at the old registrar and wasn't copied.

Can I transfer a domain within 60 days of registering it?

Usually not, at the time of writing. ICANN's rules let registrars refuse transfers within 60 days of registration or a previous transfer, and many apply a similar lock after owner details change. Check your registrar's current policy, as these rules are being revised.

Do I lose the remaining years when I transfer a domain?

For .com and most generic domains, no: the remaining time carries over and the transfer adds a year. If the domain auto-renewed at the old registrar very recently, ask whether that year carries over before you start.

What is an EPP code and where do I find it?

It's the authorisation code that proves you're allowed to move the domain. You'll find it in your current registrar's domain settings, or they'll email it to the registrant address. Keep it private.

Is a domain transfer secure?

Yes, when the code comes from your registrar's own dashboard, both accounts use two-factor authentication and the lock goes back on afterwards. Be wary of unsolicited emails offering to transfer or renew your domain.
