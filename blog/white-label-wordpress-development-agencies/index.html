White-Label WordPress Development: How It Works for Agencies, From Brief to Handover

Key takeaways
  • In white-label development, your agency sells and manages the project while a partner builds the WordPress site under your brand, with no credits and no client contact.
  • Agree the basics in writing before the first project: scope and revision rounds, the pricing model, an NDA, who owns accounts and code, and how feedback flows.
  • Quality comes from process: a clear brief, staging builds, the partner's QA before your review, one consolidated feedback list per round and a handover note in your branding.

White-label WordPress development is an arrangement where your agency sells the website, manages the client and sets the price, while a development partner builds the WordPress site behind the scenes under your brand. The client only ever deals with your agency: no developer credits on the site, no direct contact unless you arrange it, and the partner invoices you, not your client.

This guide explains how it works in practice: the process from brief to handover, who does what, the usual pricing models, NDAs, communication and quality control. What white-label development is, and what it isn't

Agencies use several arrangements that overlap. The differences matter for contracts, margins and client relationships:

How a white-label project works, step by step

  1. Brief and NDA. You send the designs (Figma, XD or PDF), the content or a note on who provides it, functionality notes and the deadline. The partner signs your NDA before client details are shared.
  2. Questions, then a quote. A good partner asks questions before quoting, then confirms the scope, timeline, revision rounds and price in writing, so you can quote your client with a margin.
  3. Build on staging. The site is built on a staging copy kept out of search engines, at an address you can share with your client under your brand.
  4. Progress updates. Regular written updates with staging links, what's next and any questions or blockers, so your account manager always knows where things stand.
  5. Partner QA, then yours. The partner tests before your team reviews, and feedback goes back as one consolidated list per round.
  6. Client review and revisions, run by your agency, with agreed changes passed to the partner.
  7. Launch and handover. The site goes live on your client's hosting, with a handover note in your branding and logins in your or your client's name.
  8. Ongoing care, if you sell it: updates, backups and small changes, still under your brand.

Incomplete briefs cause most of the back-and-forth; the agency handoff checklist lists what to send.

Your margin is what you add on top of the partner's price for project management, design, client communication and the risk of extra revision rounds. Avoid choosing a partner on hourly rate alone: a cheaper build that needs more of your team's time to review and fix often costs more overall. NDAs, confidentiality and ownership

Put these in writing before the first project. An NDA covers the first point; the rest belong in your agreement with the partner:

  • Confidentiality: your client's name, designs, data and business information stay private.
  • No credits or branding: no "developed by" links, footer mentions or developer branding on the site, on staging or in handover documents.
  • Non-solicitation: the partner doesn't approach your clients directly, during the project or after it.
  • Ownership: the finished work passes to your agency or client on payment, and premium licences are bought in your or your client's name, so updates never depend on the partner.
  • Access: hosting and admin accounts stay in your or your client's name, and the partner's logins can be removed at any time.
  • Personal data: if the work touches customer data such as orders, agree what access the partner needs and which rules apply, such as India's DPDP Act, UK GDPR or your local equivalent.

Contract wording varies by country, so have a lawyer check anything you're unsure about.

Communication across teams and time zones

  • One point of contact on each side, and one decision-maker for feedback, so instructions don't conflict.
  • Your tools: a shared Slack channel, your project board or email, with one ticket per issue including the page URL, device and a screenshot.
  • A written update rhythm, such as a note at the end of each working session with staging links, so work moves even when working hours barely overlap.
  • Agreed response times for problems on live sites, not just for build work.

Quality control before your client sees anything

Two layers of QA work best. The partner checks the build against the design on desktop, tablet and real phones, tests every form and integration end to end, and checks speed and SEO basics. Then your team reviews it as your client would, spending its time on judgement calls rather than broken links. A shared checklist keeps both layers consistent; see the website QA checklist for agencies.

Specialised builds need extra checks: payment gateways, shipping and test orders for stores, covered in white-label WooCommerce development, and editable layouts and global styles for page builder sites, covered in white-label Elementor development.

What a good handover includes

  • The site live on your client's hosting, or ready for your team to launch.
  • Admin accounts, licences and hosting in your or your client's name.
  • A handover note with no developer branding: what was built, which plugins and templates control what, and how to edit common content.
  • A full backup taken at launch, and a short walkthrough video if you want one for your client.
  • A clear agreement on who handles support after launch, and how quickly.

Choosing a white-label partner

  • Live work: open their sites on your phone and ask what they personally built.
  • Design accuracy: can they match your Figma files closely, including mobile layouts and hover states?
  • Questions before quotes: sensible questions about your brief are a good sign; an instant quote with none is not.
  • Clean, editable builds: global styles and templates your clients can edit without breaking the layout.
  • Licensed software only: genuine premium plugins and themes, never nulled copies.
  • A small trial project first, such as a landing page or a couple of templates, before trusting them with a major client.

FAQs

What is white-label WordPress development?

It's when a developer or studio builds WordPress websites for an agency, which sells them to its clients under its own brand. The client deals only with the agency, and the developer works behind the scenes under an NDA, with no credits on the site.

How much does white-label WordPress development cost?

It depends on the scope: the number of unique templates, features such as WooCommerce or integrations, and how final the designs are. Most partners quote a fixed price per project after reviewing the brief, with hourly or retainer arrangements for ongoing changes.

Is white-label web development ethical?

Yes. It's a common, long-standing arrangement, and your agency stays responsible for the result and the client relationship, as with any subcontracted work. Don't misrepresent what you deliver, and keep contracts clear about ownership and confidentiality.

Will my client know I use a white-label developer?

Not unless you tell them. A proper partner adds no credits, contacts your client only if you ask, and joins client calls as part of your team. Some agencies are open about using specialist partners, and that works too.

Who owns the website and the code?

That should be agreed in writing. Usually ownership passes to the agency or its client once the work is paid for, with hosting, licences and admin accounts in the agency's or client's name from the start.

How do I find a reliable white-label WordPress partner?

Look at live sites they built, judge their communication before you pay, confirm they'll sign your NDA and work in your tools, and start with a small trial project before handing over a major client.
