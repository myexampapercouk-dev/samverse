Domain, Hosting, DNS, SSL and Email Explained Simply: How They Connect and Who Should Own Each Account

Domain, hosting, DNS, SSL and business email explained in plain English: how they connect, what renews when, and who should own each account.

Key takeaways
  • The domain is your address, DNS points it to your hosting and your email, hosting stores and serves the website, and SSL encrypts the connection.
  • Each part renews separately, so put every renewal date in one place, turn on auto-renew and keep payment details current.
  • Register the domain, hosting and email in your business name with your own login, and give your developer access rather than ownership.

A domain is your website's address, such as yourbusiness.in. Hosting is the server that stores your website's files and database and sends pages to visitors. DNS is the directory that connects the domain to the hosting and to your email, and an SSL certificate encrypts the connection so browsers show https and a padlock. You need all of them, each renews on its own schedule, and every account should be in your business's name.

When I build a site through WordPress website development, connecting the domain, setting up SSL and checking that email records still work are part of the launch, and every account stays in your name so you own everything. This guide explains what each piece does, in plain English, so you can make sense of the bills and the jargon.

If you've had a website for a while and aren't sure who controls what, the website ownership checklist is a good companion to this page.

What happens when someone types your address

  1. DNS lookup. The browser asks DNS where yourbusiness.in lives and gets back the IP address of your hosting server.
  2. Secure connection. The browser connects to that server, which presents its SSL certificate. The browser checks the certificate is valid, hasn't expired and matches the domain. If any check fails, the visitor sees a warning instead of your site.
  3. The page is built. WordPress runs on the server, reads your content from the database and builds the page, or a cache hands over a ready-made copy.
  4. The page arrives, encrypted, and the browser displays it.

Email follows a separate path through the same DNS. When someone emails you, their mail server looks up your domain's MX records to find out which provider receives your mail. That is why a careless DNS change during a website move can stop email without touching the website at all.

Domain names

You register a domain through a registrar for one or more years at a time. The registrar records you as the registrant, the legal holder of the domain, and gives you an account to manage it.

  • Choosing one: short, easy to say and spell, and close to your business name. How to choose a domain name for your business has ten practical tips.
  • Extension: .com and .in are the usual choices for Indian businesses; .in vs .com explains when each makes sense.
  • Cost: the WordPress website cost guide on this site puts a .com or .in at roughly ₹500 to ₹1,200 a year. Check the renewal price as well as the first-year offer.
  • Auto-renew and contact details: turn on auto-renew, keep the card or wallet on file current, and make sure the registrant email is one you read.
  • Transfer lock: keep the domain locked against transfers unless you're deliberately moving it to another registrar.

If a domain expires, the website and email stop working. Registrars usually allow a grace period to renew, then a more expensive recovery period, and after that the domain can be released to anyone. The exact periods vary by registrar and extension, so don't rely on them.

DNS: the records that matter

DNS is managed wherever your domain's nameservers point: the registrar, the hosting company or a service such as Cloudflare. Only one place is in charge at a time, which is why "I changed the record but nothing happened" is often a case of editing DNS in the wrong account.

RecordWhat it does
NSSays which DNS provider is in charge of the domain
A and AAAAPoint the domain to the hosting server's IPv4 and IPv6 addresses
CNAMEMakes one name an alias of another, often www
MXSays which provider receives your email
TXTHolds verification codes and email security records: SPF, DKIM and DMARC

A typical small business setup looks something like this, with the website on one provider and email on another:

example.com.        A      203.0.113.10
www.example.com.    CNAME  example.com.
example.com.        MX     10 mx1.mail-provider.example.
example.com.        TXT    "v=spf1 include:spf.mail-provider.example ~all"

Each record has a TTL, the time other servers may cache it. Changes can take from minutes to a day or so to reach everyone, which is why migrations lower the TTL in advance. The email records deserve special care; SPF, DKIM and DMARC explained covers what each one does.

Hosting

Hosting is a server, or a share of one, that stores your WordPress files and database and runs the software that builds each page. Its quality decides how fast pages arrive, how the site copes with busy days and how easily it can be restored when something goes wrong.

The main types are shared hosting, managed WordPress hosting, cloud hosting and VPS, in roughly rising order of power and responsibility. For a small business site in India, a server in or near India, current PHP, proper caching, off-site backups and WordPress-aware support matter more than the brand name. How to choose WordPress hosting in India walks through the checks.

The domain and the hosting don't have to come from the same company. Buying them separately means a hosting problem or a move never puts the domain at risk, at the cost of one more account to manage.

SSL certificates and HTTPS

An SSL certificate (strictly, TLS, but everyone still says SSL) does two jobs: it encrypts what passes between the visitor and your site, such as form entries and payment details, and it proves the server really belongs to your domain. Without one, browsers mark the site "Not secure", and contact forms and payment gateways become a problem.

  • Free certificates are usually enough. Most hosts issue domain-validated certificates, often from Let's Encrypt, at no cost. They encrypt just as strongly as paid ones. Paid certificates that verify the organisation are mainly needed where a bank, client or tender asks for them.
  • They renew automatically, every few months for free certificates. Renewal fails when the domain points somewhere else, or when a proxy or firewall blocks the check, so an expired-certificate warning usually means a DNS or configuration problem.
  • Installing it isn't the last step. Every http address should redirect to https, the WordPress Address and Site Address settings should use https, and images or scripts still loading over http cause "mixed content" warnings.

If visitors already see warnings, SSL certificate errors explained goes through each message and its fix.

Business email on your domain

An address such as info@yourbusiness.in looks more professional than a free email address and stays with the business when staff change. You can use mailboxes included with some hosting plans, or a dedicated service such as Google Workspace, Microsoft 365 or Zoho Mail. The website's own emails, such as form notifications, are a separate matter and often need SMTP set up to arrive reliably. Business email options for small businesses compares the choices.

Who should own each account

The safest rule is simple: the business owns every account, and the developer gets access. If a former developer or agency holds the domain or hosting in their own account, you can lose your website and email over a dispute or an unanswered phone.

AccountRegistered toWho else needs access
Domain registrarYour business, with a business email you checkYour developer, only for DNS changes or a transfer
DNS service, if separateYour businessYour developer, as an added member

WordPressAn administrator account for youYour developer, with their own login
Business emailYour businessWhoever manages users for you

Keep the logins in a password manager and remove access when someone stops working on the site.

Mistakes that take websites offline

  • An expired domain, usually because auto-renew was off or the card on file had expired.
  • A domain or hosting account in someone else's name, who then can't be reached.
  • Changing nameservers without copying the MX and TXT records, which silently breaks email.
  • Cancelling old hosting before checking that the site, forms and email work on the new one.
  • Ignoring renewal and expiry emails, or the opposite: paying fake "domain renewal" invoices from companies you've never used.

Keeping every date in one place prevents most of these; the domain and hosting renewal checklist is a template for it.

A setup checklist for a new website

  1. Register the domain in your business name, with auto-renew on.
  2. Buy hosting in your business name, with a data centre near your visitors and off-site backups.
  3. Point the domain to the hosting, by nameservers or A records.
  4. Set up business email and its MX, SPF, DKIM and DMARC records.
  5. Issue the SSL certificate, redirect http to https and check for mixed content.
  6. Set up SMTP so website form emails arrive.
  7. Record every account, login and renewal date in one secure place.

FAQs

What is the difference between a domain and hosting?

The domain is the address people type; hosting is the server where the website actually lives. You need both, and DNS connects the two.

Can I buy my domain and hosting from different companies?

Yes. You point the domain to the host with nameservers or DNS records. Keeping them separate means a hosting problem or a move never puts your domain at risk.

Do I need to buy an SSL certificate separately?

Usually not. Most hosts include a free certificate that renews automatically, and it encrypts as strongly as a paid one. Buy a paid certificate only if a specific partner, bank or tender requires organisation validation.

What happens if my domain expires?

Your website and email stop working. Most registrars allow a grace period to renew and then a costlier recovery period before the domain is released, but the periods vary, so renew as soon as you notice.

How much does a domain cost in India?

The cost guide on this site puts a .com or .in at roughly ₹500 to ₹1,200 a year. Renewal prices can differ from first-year offers, so compare those.
