Google Discover for Small Business Websites: What to Know

What Google Discover is, how it differs from search, what content tends to appear, how to read the Discover report and why it's a bonus rather than a plan.

Key takeaways
  • Discover is a personalised feed shown without a search, so its visitors are browsing rather than ready to hire.
  • Timely, helpful content with large original images, the max-image-preview:large setting and honest titles has the best chance.
  • Google doesn't guarantee Discover traffic, so check the Search Console report occasionally but treat it as a bonus.

Some website owners open Search Console one day and notice a report they didn't have before: Discover. It's a source of traffic that works very differently from normal Google search, and it can send a sudden burst of visitors to a single page. For a small business it's worth understanding, but not worth chasing. Here's what it is and how to approach it sensibly.

What Google Discover is

Discover is a feed of articles and videos that Google shows people without them searching for anything. It appears in the Google app and on Google's mobile homepage, and on many Android phones it's a swipe away from the home screen. The feed is personalised: Google picks content based on what it thinks each person is interested in, using signals such as their activity on Google products (where they've allowed it) and topics they've chosen to follow.

There's nothing to sign up for. If your pages are indexed by Google and meet its content policies, they're eligible to appear. Eligible isn't the same as shown, though, and Google doesn't guarantee Discover traffic to any site.

How Discover differs from search

AspectGoogle SearchGoogle Discover
What triggers itSomeone types or speaks a queryGoogle predicts what a person might like
Type of contentAnswers to a specific need, often evergreenOften fresh, timely or visually appealing
Traffic patternUsually steady, building over timeOften spiky: a burst, then little or nothing
KeywordsCentral to how pages are matchedMuch less relevant, as there's no query to match
Visitor mindsetOften actively looking to buy or hireBrowsing out of interest

That last row matters most for a business. Someone searching "AC repair near me" wants help today. Someone scrolling Discover might enjoy your article and never think about hiring anyone.

What tends to appear

Google doesn't publish a formula, but its guidance and what you see in the feed point the same way. Content that appears in Discover is usually:

  • Timely: new launches, seasonal guides, changes in rules that affect people, local events
  • Visual: led by a strong, original photo rather than a stock image or a logo
  • Genuinely helpful or interesting: it tells a story well or offers insight readers can't easily find elsewhere
  • From a site with clear expertise on the topic, built up over many pages rather than one lucky post

For small businesses, realistic candidates include a well-photographed home renovation story from an interior designer, a festive gifting guide from a sweet shop, a pre-monsoon checklist from a waterproofing company, or a travel agency's guide to a destination people are suddenly interested in. Service pages, contact pages and price lists are unlikely to appear, and that's fine: those pages are built for search.

Images and the max-image-preview setting

Discover is a visual feed, and large images stand out far more than small thumbnails. Google's guidance for Discover recommends:

  • Using a high-quality image at least 1,200 pixels wide as the main image for the page
  • Allowing large image previews with the max-image-preview:large robots setting
  • Not using your site logo as the main image

WordPress adds the large image preview setting by default on public sites, but SEO plugins and themes can change robots settings, so check your plugin's options or the page source. Then make sure each article has a relevant featured image and social sharing image that represents the content. Our image SEO guide covers choosing and preparing these images. They need to load quickly too, so compress them without making them blurry.

Titles that inform, not mislead

Because Discover shows your title and image without any search context, it's tempting to write teasing headlines. Google's guidance warns against this: titles should capture what the page is about, and preview content shouldn't exaggerate, withhold key information to force a click, or rely on shock. Clickbait might win one click, but it works against the kind of content Google says it wants in the feed, and it disappoints the reader.

A simple test: would the reader feel the article delivered what the headline promised? If not, rewrite it. The same principle applies to search results; see how to write meta titles and descriptions.

Checking the Discover report in Search Console

In Google Search Console, look under Performance. If your site has had enough Discover impressions, you'll see a Discover report alongside the usual search results report. If it isn't there, your site simply hasn't appeared in the feed enough to qualify, which is very common for small business sites.

The report shows:

  • Impressions: how often your content appeared in people's feeds
  • Clicks and CTR: how many people tapped through
  • Pages: which pages appeared, so you can see what kind of content was picked

There's no query data, because nobody typed a search. Analytics tools don't always label Discover visits clearly, so treat Search Console as the more reliable record. For the rest of the tool, see Search Console reports explained.

A bonus, not a strategy

Google itself describes Discover traffic as less predictable than search traffic, and there's no way to make your content appear. A page that brings a burst of visitors one week may never show again. Building your marketing around it is risky, especially for a business that depends on steady enquiries.

A sensible approach for a small business:

  1. Focus first on what brings enquiries reliably: strong service pages, local SEO and your Google Business Profile
  2. When you publish articles, make them timely or genuinely useful, with an original lead image and an honest title
  3. Check the technical basics once: large image previews allowed, featured images set, pages fast on mobile
  4. If Discover traffic arrives, treat it as a bonus. Look at which page was picked and whether those visitors went on to read a service page or get in touch

Be wary of anyone selling "guaranteed Discover traffic". Nobody outside Google controls the feed.
