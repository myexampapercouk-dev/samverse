How to Write a Homepage That Converts Visitors Into Enquiries: The Sections in Order, the First-Screen Test and Mobile

How to write a homepage that converts: the first-screen test, sections in order from hero to final call to action, mobile layout, and common mistakes.

Key takeaways
  • The first screen must say what you do, who it is for and what to do next, with one primary call to action, and it must pass that test on a phone.
  • A reliable order is hero, quick proof, services, why you, process, work, testimonials, FAQs and a final call to action, each linking to a deeper page.
  • Repeat one primary action through the page, use only proof you can back up, and judge changes by enquiries rather than opinions.

A homepage that converts answers four questions in the first screen: what you do, who it's for, why a visitor should trust you, and what to do next. Below that, a reliable order is a hero section, quick proof, your services, why choose you, how it works, your work, testimonials, FAQs and a final call to action, with the same primary action repeated down the page and every section linking to a deeper page. It has to work on a phone first, because that's where many visitors will see it.

The homepage is usually where content restructuring starts when I take on a website redesign: the sections are reordered around what visitors need, and every page gets a clear call to action. If you're starting from nothing, the same structure applies to a new site through WordPress website development.

The first-screen test

Open your homepage on a phone and look only at what shows before you scroll. Then show it to someone who doesn't know your business for five seconds, hide it, and ask:

  1. What does this business do?
  2. Who is it for, and where?
  3. What would you tap if you were interested?

If they can't answer all three, the hero needs work, however good the rest of the page is. The usual culprits are a slogan instead of a description, a slider whose first slide says nothing, or a large image that pushes the headline below the fold.

Not every business needs every section, and the order can flex. A restaurant may lead with the menu and bookings; a manufacturer may put certifications and infrastructure higher. The principle stays the same: answer the visitor's questions in the order they ask them.

Section by section

1. Hero

The headline should say what you do, for whom and, if you're local, where. A supporting line adds the main benefit or difference. Then one primary button, such as "Get a quote" or "Book a consultation", and optionally a secondary action such as WhatsApp or "See our work". Use a real photo of your work, team or premises rather than generic stock. Two fictional examples:

  • Weak: "Innovative solutions for a better tomorrow."
  • Clear: "Industrial control panels designed and built in Pune, for OEMs and plant upgrades."

2. Quick proof

Straight under the hero, a short strip of evidence: client logos you have permission to show, your Google rating, registrations or certifications, or a few facts you can back up. Keep it to one line or one row. Never invent figures or use logos of companies you haven't worked with.

3. Services

A short card for each main service, with one or two lines and a link to its own page. This helps visitors self-select, and it passes your homepage's authority to the pages you want to rank; internal linking explained covers why. The service pages carry the detail; how to write service pages that convert shows how.

4. Why choose you

Three to five specific reasons, such as who does the work, how you communicate, what's included, your service area or your specialism. "Quality, trust and commitment" says nothing; "One project manager from measurement to installation" says something.

5. How it works

Three or four short steps, from first contact to result. A visible process reassures people who have never hired a business like yours, and it sets expectations about what happens after they get in touch.

6. Work and case studies

Show a selection of real projects, each with a photo, a line on what was done and a link to more detail. How to write case studies explains how to turn projects into proof, and why results should only be ones you can verify.

7. Testimonials

Two or three specific, named testimonials, with permission, placed near a call to action. A quote that mentions the problem and the outcome persuades far more than "Great service!". Collecting and displaying testimonials covers asking for them and using them honestly.

8. FAQs

Four to six questions people ask before contacting you: price or what affects it, timings, service area, process, what they need to prepare. How to write a good FAQ section has examples.

9. Final call to action

End with the same primary action as the hero, plus the alternatives people prefer: a short form, WhatsApp and a phone number. Say what happens next, such as who will reply and how.

A real example: this site's homepage

The homepage of samverse.space follows this structure, adapted for a freelance developer. It's custom-coded rather than built on WordPress, but the sections would be the same in any builder:

  • Hero: a headline saying what I do ("I build WordPress websites that work"), a line on who it's for, two buttons ("View My Work" and "WhatsApp Me") and a short row of facts.
  • About: a brief paragraph on how the work started and who it serves, with a link to the full About page.
  • Skills and services: a card per service, each linking to its own service page, followed by a mid-page call to action with a link to the cost calculator.
  • Industries: cards for the main types of client, each linking to an industry page.
  • Selected work: project cards linking to case studies.
  • Process: four steps, Discuss, Design, Develop, and Launch and Support.
  • Articles: recent guides from the blog.
  • FAQs, then contact: call, email, WhatsApp and a short form that also opens WhatsApp with the details filled in.

Designing the homepage for mobile

  • Keep the hero short so the headline and button fit on the first screen of a typical phone.
  • Make buttons large and easy to reach with a thumb, with tap-to-call and WhatsApp links.
  • Consider a sticky call or WhatsApp button that stays visible without covering content.
  • Avoid autoplay video and heavy sliders in the hero; the main image is often the largest element on the page, so compress it and size it properly.
  • Stack sections cleanly: cards in one column, short paragraphs, nothing that needs sideways scrolling.
  • Test on a mid-range Android phone on mobile data, not only on a fast office connection.

Calls to action: one primary, repeated

Choose one primary action for the whole homepage and repeat it after major sections: in the hero, after services, after proof and at the end. Secondary options, such as WhatsApp or "See our work", can sit beside it, but avoid five different buttons competing for attention. Button labels should say what happens: "Get a quote on WhatsApp" is clearer than "Submit" or "Click here".

How to tell if your homepage works

Opinions about a homepage are cheap; enquiries are the real test. In GA4, check how many sessions that start on the homepage lead to a key event, such as a form submission, call or WhatsApp click, and which links people click from it. A free heatmap tool, such as Microsoft Clarity, shows how far people scroll and where they tap. Change one thing at a time, such as the headline or the order of sections, and compare a few weeks before and after. Conversion rate optimisation basics explains the method.

FAQs

What should be on a business homepage?

A clear headline saying what you do, for whom and where; one primary call to action; quick proof; your services with links; how it works; examples of your work; testimonials; FAQs; and a final call to action with contact options.

How long should a homepage be?

Long enough to answer a first-time visitor's main questions and route them to the right page. Most business homepages need several sections, but each should be short and link to a page with the detail.

Should I use a slider on my homepage?

Usually not. Few visitors wait for later slides, sliders often slow the page, and they tempt you into vague slogans. One clear hero with one message works better.

What makes a good homepage headline?

One that says what you do, for whom and, if local, where, in words your customers use. If a stranger can't tell what you sell from the headline alone, rewrite it.

Should my homepage target keywords for SEO?

The homepage usually ranks for your business name and your main service with your location. Specific services are better targeted by their own pages, which the homepage should link to.
