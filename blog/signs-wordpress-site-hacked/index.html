How to Check if Your WordPress Site Is Hacked: Warning Signs, Quick Checks and What to Do First

Check if your WordPress site is hacked: warning signs, 10 quick checks you can run today, extra checks for online stores, and the first steps to take.

Key takeaways
  • A hack usually shows up in one of four places: what visitors see, what Google shows, what the WordPress admin shows and what the server shows.
  • To check if a WordPress site is hacked, run a site: search, open the Search Console Security Issues report, review admin users and scan files, then visit as a stranger on mobile data.
  • If you find a problem, back up the infected copy, change every password, clean core, plugins and database, hunt for backdoors and close the entry point before asking Google for a review.

You can tell your WordPress site has been hacked when you see signs such as visitors being redirected to spam or scam sites, unfamiliar pages or Japanese and pharma keywords appearing in Google results, admin users you did not create, or a "This site may be hacked" or "Deceptive site ahead" warning. To know for sure, run a few quick checks: search Google for your domain, open the Security Issues report in Search Console, review your WordPress users, and scan the files with a malware scanner.

Short answer: a hack usually shows up in one of four places: what visitors see (redirects, pop-ups, warnings), what Google shows (spam pages, odd titles), what the admin shows (new users, unknown plugins, changed settings) and what the server shows (modified files, high resource use, spam emails). Check all four, because many hacks hide from logged-in admins and only show to visitors from Google.

Warning signs that your WordPress site has been hacked

These are the symptoms owners most often notice first. One sign on its own can have an innocent cause, but two or more together almost always means you should investigate properly.

  • Redirects to other sites: visitors, especially on mobile or coming from Google, get sent to gambling, dating, fake prize or scam pages. You may not see it yourself because many redirects skip logged-in users. See fixing the WordPress redirect hack.
  • Spam pages in Google: searching your domain shows pages you never made, often in Japanese, or selling medicines, replica goods or loans. This is the Japanese keyword SEO spam hack and its cousins.
  • Browser or Google warnings: a red "Deceptive site ahead" screen in Chrome or "This site may be hacked" under your listing in search results.
  • Unknown admin users: new administrator accounts with random names or odd email addresses.
  • Plugins or files you did not install: plugins with generic names, or PHP files sitting in the uploads folder.
  • Hosting suspension or warning email: your host suspends the account or emails you about malware or abuse.
  • Spam emails sent from your domain: bounce messages you never sent, or your host limiting outgoing mail.
  • Sudden slowness or high CPU usage: hidden scripts mining, sending spam or attacking other sites use server resources.
  • Changed content or defacement: new links in the footer, injected text, or your homepage replaced.
  • Locked out of the admin: your password no longer works and the reset email never arrives because the admin email was changed.

How to know if your WordPress site has been hacked: 10 quick checks

If you suspect something is wrong, work through these checks in order. Most take a few minutes and need no technical tools.

  1. Run a site: search. Type site:yourdomain.com into Google. Scroll through the results and look for titles, languages or URLs you do not recognise. Also try site:yourdomain.com viagra, casino or a Japanese character search to surface hidden spam.
  2. Open Search Console Security Issues. In Google Search Console, open Security and Manual Actions, then Security Issues. Google lists detected malware, hacked content and deceptive pages here.
  3. Check Search Console pages and sitemaps. A sudden jump in indexed pages, or sitemaps you did not submit, points to injected spam.
  4. Visit the site as a stranger. Use a private browser window on your phone with mobile data, and click through from a Google result rather than typing the address. Many redirect hacks only trigger this way.
  5. Review your users. In Users, filter by Administrator. Delete or demote anyone you cannot identify, and check the email address on your own account.
  6. Review plugins and themes. Look for plugins you did not install, and for anything nulled or downloaded from unofficial sites. Read why nulled themes and plugins are risky.
  7. Look at recently modified files. In your hosting file manager or over SFTP, sort folders by modified date. PHP files changed on a day nobody worked on the site, or any PHP file in wp-content/uploads, need a closer look.
  8. Check key files. Open .htaccess, wp-config.php and index.php. Long lines of encoded text, eval, base64_decode or unfamiliar redirect rules are red flags.
  9. Run a malware scanner. Use a reputable security plugin scan and an external scanner. Neither catches everything, so treat a clean result as reassurance rather than proof. Our malware scanner comparison explains the differences.
  10. Check the database. Search the wp_options and wp_posts tables for script tags, iframes or unfamiliar domains, especially in siteurl, home and widget settings.

How to check if a WordPress site is hacked without logging in

Sometimes you cannot log in, or you want to check a site before you take it on. You can still learn a lot from outside:

  • Run the site: search and look at titles and snippets.
  • Use Google Safe Browsing's site status page to see if the domain is flagged.
  • Open the site from a Google result on mobile data in a private window.
  • View the page source and search for unfamiliar script sources or iframes pointing to other domains.
  • Run an external website scanner, which checks the public pages for known malware and blacklist status.

External checks only see what is served publicly. A backdoor that is waiting quietly will not show up, so a full check still needs file and database access.

What to do first if your WordPress site has been hacked

Once you are fairly sure the site is compromised, the order of steps matters. Rushing to delete things can destroy the evidence you need to find how attackers got in.

  1. Do not panic-delete. Take a full backup of the files and database as they are now, labelled as infected. It helps with the clean-up and with finding the entry point.
  2. Change every password. WordPress admins, hosting panel, SFTP, database and the email account linked to the admin. Update the security keys in wp-config.php so every session is logged out.
  3. Put the site in maintenance mode if visitors are at risk, for example when it redirects to scams or the checkout is affected.
  4. Clean properly. Replace WordPress core with a fresh copy, reinstall plugins and themes from official sources, remove unknown files and clean injected database content. Our step-by-step malware removal guide covers this.
  5. Hunt for backdoors. Attackers usually leave a way back in. See finding and removing WordPress backdoors.
  6. Find and close the entry point. Usually an outdated plugin, a weak or reused password, a nulled theme or another infected site on the same hosting account.
  7. Request a review. Once clean, request a review in Search Console so Google removes the warning, then follow our guide to recovering rankings after a hack.

Why restoring an old backup is not always enough

Restoring yesterday's backup feels like the quickest fix, and sometimes it works. The problem is that many hacks sit quietly for weeks before anything visible happens, so your backups may already contain the backdoor. Restoring also brings back the same outdated plugin that let the attacker in. If you restore, still update everything, change passwords and scan the restored copy before going live.

How to stop it happening again

Most WordPress hacks come from a short list of causes, explained in why WordPress sites get hacked. The habits that prevent most of them are simple:

  • Keep WordPress, plugins and themes updated, and remove anything you do not use.
  • Use strong unique passwords and two-factor authentication for every admin.
  • Give each person their own account with the lowest role they need.
  • Avoid nulled themes and plugins completely.
  • Keep automatic off-site backups, and test that they restore.
  • Use a firewall and monitoring so you hear about problems before your customers do.

How to check if a WordPress store or payment page is hacked

Online stores need a few extra checks, because attackers who get into a WooCommerce site often target money rather than search rankings. These hacks can stay hidden for weeks because the shop keeps working normally while customer data or payments are quietly diverted.

  • Checkout scripts: view the source of the checkout page and look for scripts loading from domains you do not recognise. Card and payment skimmers sit here. See WooCommerce checkout skimmer malware.
  • Payment settings: open each payment gateway's settings and confirm the account details, API keys and any UPI ID shown to customers are still yours.
  • Contact details: check that the phone number, WhatsApp link and bank details on the contact page, order emails and invoice templates have not been changed.
  • Orders and customers: look for unusual admin-created orders, coupons you did not create, or new shop manager accounts.
  • Emails: place a test order and read the customer email carefully for added links or changed text.

If anything here looks wrong, treat it as urgent: put the checkout into maintenance mode, tell your payment provider, and follow the clean-up steps above before taking orders again.

A monthly hack check you can do in 15 minutes

Most owners only check when something already looks broken. A short monthly routine catches problems earlier, while they are cheaper to fix:

  1. Run a site: search for your domain and skim the titles.
  2. Open Search Console and check Security Issues and the Pages report for a sudden jump in indexed pages.
  3. Review the Users list and the Plugins list for anything you do not recognise.
  4. Confirm your latest off-site backup exists and is recent. Our WordPress backup and restore guide explains what a usable backup looks like.
  5. Visit the site from a Google result on your phone using mobile data.

This fits naturally into a regular maintenance routine. If you would rather not do it yourself, a WordPress maintenance plan can include it.

FAQs

How do I tell if my WordPress site has been hacked?

Look for redirects to unrelated sites, spam pages when you search site:yourdomain.com, unknown admin users, Google or browser warnings, and unexpected file changes. Then confirm with Search Console's Security Issues report and a malware scan.

How can I check if my WordPress site is hacked for free?

A site: search, the Search Console Security Issues report, Google Safe Browsing site status, a free security plugin scan and a review of your users and recently modified files are all free and cover most common hacks.

Why can't I see the hack when I visit my own site?

Many hacks hide from logged-in users and from people who type the address directly. They only trigger for visitors arriving from Google, often on mobile. Test in a private window on mobile data, clicking through from a search result.

Can a malware scanner tell me my site is definitely clean?

No. Scanners catch known patterns, but new or well-hidden code can slip through. A clean scan is a good sign, not proof, so combine it with file, user and database checks.

Will my Google rankings recover after a hack?

Usually, once the spam pages are removed, the site is cleaned and Google's warning is lifted. How quickly depends on how long the hack ran and how many spam pages were indexed.

Should I fix a hacked WordPress site myself or hire someone?

If you are comfortable with SFTP, databases and file comparison, you can follow a careful process. If the site handles payments or enquiries, or the hack keeps coming back, professional help is usually quicker and safer.
