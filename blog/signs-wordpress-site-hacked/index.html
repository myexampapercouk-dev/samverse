How to Know If Your WordPress Site Has Been Hacked: Quick Checks, Warning Signs and What to Do First

How to tell if your WordPress site has been hacked: a 10-minute check with Google tools, the clear warning signs, and what to do first if something is wrong.

Key takeaways
  • Check Google Safe Browsing, Search Console's Security issues report and a site: search for spam pages first.
  • Unknown admins, PHP files in uploads and redirects that only hit mobile visitors are strong signs of a hack.
  • If hacked: back up, tell your host, change every password from a clean device, then clean before asking Google to review.

Hacked WordPress sites often look perfectly normal to the owner. Many infections are built to stay hidden: they redirect only mobile visitors, show spam only to Google, or skip anyone who is logged in. So "it looks fine when I open it" doesn't mean the site is clean.

Here's a ten-minute check, the clear signs of a hack, the false alarms, and what to do first.

The quick answer

Your WordPress site has probably been hacked if any of these are true:

  • Google Search Console shows anything in the Security issues report
  • A site:yourdomain.com search on Google shows pages you never created
  • There are administrator accounts in WordPress that nobody on your team made
  • Visitors, especially on mobile or arriving from Google, are sent to other websites
  • There are PHP files inside wp-content/uploads
  • Your host has emailed you about malware, spam or a suspension

Here's how to check each of these properly.

Check if your WordPress site is hacked in 10 minutes

1. Google Safe Browsing site status (1 minute)

Google's Transparency Report has a free Safe Browsing site status tool. Enter your domain and it tells you whether Google currently considers the site unsafe, for example for malware or phishing. This is the system behind red "Deceptive site ahead" browser warnings. A clean result only covers what Google has detected so far.

2. Search Console: Security issues and Manual actions (2 minutes)

If your site is verified in Google Search Console, open Security & Manual Actions and check both reports:

  • Security issues lists hacked content, malware, deceptive pages and similar problems, often with sample URLs
  • Manual actions shows whether Google has taken action against spam on your site

While you're there, open Settings > Users and permissions. An owner you don't recognise is a serious red flag, because attackers sometimes verify themselves so they can submit their own spam sitemaps. If you haven't set up Search Console yet, see setting up Analytics and Search Console.

3. A site: search for spam pages (2 minutes)

Search Google for site:yourdomain.com and scroll through the results. Look for titles in a language you don't use, cheap medicines, casino or loan keywords, replica goods, or URLs with random strings. Then try targeted searches such as site:yourdomain.com casino or site:yourdomain.com viagra. Spam pages are often cloaked, so they appear in Google even though you can't see them on your site. If you find them, see fixing the Japanese keyword and SEO spam hack.

4. Unknown admin users (1 minute)

In the WordPress dashboard, go to Users and click the Administrator filter. Every admin should be someone you can name. Some malware hides its accounts from this list, so compare the number shown next to "Administrator" with the accounts you can actually see. A mismatch is suspicious. Also check Settings > General: if "Anyone can register" is ticked and the default role is Administrator, someone has changed it. See auditing WordPress user accounts.

5. Recently modified or unfamiliar files (2 minutes)

Open your hosting control panel's File Manager (or connect by SFTP) and look at:

  • wp-content/uploads: this folder should hold images and documents. PHP files here are almost always malicious.
  • The main folder: unfamiliar files with random names, or names that imitate WordPress files
  • Last modified dates: sort by date. Core files, wp-config.php, index.php or .htaccess changing when nobody updated anything is a warning sign.

If you or your developer have SSH access, WP-CLI can compare files with the official versions:

wp core verify-checksums
wp plugin verify-checksums --all

The plugin check covers WordPress.org plugins only.

6. Redirects that only happen on mobile (1 minute)

Take your phone, switch off Wi-Fi so you're on mobile data, open a private or incognito tab, search Google for your business name and tap your own result. Redirect malware often targets exactly this visitor: mobile, logged out and arriving from a search engine. Some infections redirect a visitor only once, so use a fresh private tab for each test. Details are in how to fix the WordPress redirect hack.

7. Hosting alerts and your inbox (1 minute)

Search your email for messages from your host about malware, quarantined files, high resource usage, outgoing spam or account suspension. Many hosts run their own malware scanner, so check its results in your control panel too. Bounced emails or complaints that your messages are landing in spam can mean your server is sending spam; see WordPress site sending spam emails.

Passing all seven checks is reassuring but not a guarantee, as some backdoors sit quietly until used. For a deeper scan, see WordPress malware scanners compared.

Clear signs your WordPress site has been hacked

Signs your visitors and Google see

  • Redirects to gambling, dating, fake prize, tech-support or adult sites
  • Browser warnings such as "Deceptive site ahead"; see fixing browser warnings
  • "This site may be hacked" under your listing in search results; see removing that label
  • Spam titles or descriptions on your real pages in search results
  • Pop-ups, ads or push-notification prompts you never added
  • Visitors' antivirus software blocking your site

Signs inside WordPress

  • Administrator accounts nobody recognises
  • Plugins you didn't install, often with harmless-sounding names
  • Your security plugin switched off, or its alerts suddenly stopping
  • Posts or pages you didn't write, sometimes hidden as drafts or private
  • The site address or admin email changed under Settings > General
  • Your password no longer works and reset emails go somewhere else

Signs on the server

  • PHP files in the uploads folder, or files with random names
  • Long blocks of scrambled code at the top or bottom of theme or core files
  • Unknown cron jobs in your hosting control panel
  • CPU or memory usage spiking with no rise in real visitors
  • Your host suspending the account; see hosting suspended for malware

Signs in your data

  • A sudden drop in Google traffic; see what to check when traffic drops
  • A sharp rise in indexed pages in Search Console's Pages report
  • Search Console queries for keywords you've never used, often in other languages
  • Sitemaps in Search Console that you didn't submit
  • An email from Google saying a new owner was added to your property

Things that look like a hack but usually aren't

Not every problem is malware. These usually have ordinary causes:

  • A critical error or white screen right after an update: usually a plugin or theme conflict; see fixing the critical error
  • "Too many redirects": usually a settings or SSL loop, not a spam redirect; see fixing ERR_TOO_MANY_REDIRECTS
  • "Not secure" in the address bar: an SSL or mixed content issue; see SSL certificate errors
  • Only one person sees redirects or pop-ups: a browser extension or adware on their own device is possible, so ask whether it happens on other devices too

If you're unsure, run the seven checks above. Real hacks usually leave more than one trace.

What to do first if your site is hacked

  1. Don't delete the site or restore an old backup straight away. Your content can usually be saved, and older backups may already contain the infection.
  2. Back up the current files and database, even though they're infected. It preserves evidence and your latest real content.
  3. Tell your host. They may already know what's wrong, can share logs and may need to lift a suspension later.
  4. Change every password from a device you trust: hosting, SFTP, database, all WordPress admins and the email account linked to the site. If your own computer might be infected, scan it first.
  5. Note what you've seen (unknown users, file names, redirect destinations, dates) before removing anything. It helps find the entry point.
  6. Protect visitors. If people are being redirected or a checkout may be compromised, put up a maintenance page or pause payments. For stores, see checkout skimmer malware.
  7. Don't ask Google for a review yet. Clean first; a failed review only slows things down.

Then work through the full clean-up: how to remove malware from WordPress step by step. Backdoors are the main reason cleaned sites get reinfected, so read finding and removing backdoors too. Once the site is clean, recovering rankings after a hack covers the Google side.

How to stop it happening again

  • Keep WordPress, themes and plugins updated, delete what you don't use, and never install nulled themes or plugins
  • Use strong, unique passwords and two-factor authentication; see securing your WordPress login
  • Add a firewall, automatic off-site backups and an activity log
  • Check Search Console and your security alerts every month

FAQs

How can I check if my WordPress site is hacked for free?

Use Google's Safe Browsing site status tool, the Security issues report in Search Console and a site:yourdomain.com search on Google. Then check your admin users, look for PHP files in the uploads folder and test the site on your phone using mobile data. Free security plugins and your host's scanner can add a file scan.

Why does my site look fine to me but customers say it's hacked?

Many infections deliberately hide from site owners. They skip logged-in users, visitors who come back, or IP addresses that have used the dashboard, and target mobile visitors arriving from Google instead. Your caching setup can also show you a different copy of the page from the one visitors get. Test in a private window on mobile data.

Can a security plugin tell me if my site is hacked?

It can catch a lot, especially known malware and modified core files, but no scanner catches everything. Plugins run inside WordPress, so malware that has tampered with WordPress can sometimes hide from them. Combine a plugin scan with a server-side scan from your host and the manual checks above.

Will Google tell me if my site is hacked?

Often, but not always and not instantly. If your site is verified in Search Console, Google can email you when it detects hacked content or malware, and the issue shows in the Security issues report. Infections that Google hasn't spotted yet, or that only affect some visitors, can go unreported, so don't rely on Google as your only alarm.

Should I restore a backup or clean the site?

Restoring is quicker if you have a backup from before the infection and you know when it happened. Many hacks sit unnoticed for weeks, though, so recent backups may be infected too. Either way, you still need to change passwords and fix the hole the attacker used, or the site will be hacked again.

Can a hacked WordPress site be fixed without losing content?

Usually, yes. Posts, pages and media live in the database and uploads folder and can be kept after the malicious code is removed. A proper clean-up replaces WordPress core, plugins and themes with fresh copies while keeping your content.
