Website for SaaS Startups: The Marketing Site Beside Your App, Pricing and Feature Pages, the Trial Hand-off, Docs, Changelog and Fair Comparison Pages

How a SaaS startup website should work: a marketing site separate from the app, pricing and feature pages, a clean trial hand-off, docs and a changelog.

Key takeaways
  • Keep the marketing site and the product apart, usually on the main domain and an app subdomain, so marketing can change pages without touching the product.
  • Pricing, feature and use-case pages do most of the selling, and the sign-up button should hand visitors to the app with their plan and campaign source intact.
  • Docs, a changelog and honest comparison pages answer questions that come after the first visit, and they often bring in better search traffic than the homepage.

A SaaS startup needs two separate things: the product, where customers log in and work, and a marketing site that explains the product and sends people into a trial or demo. The marketing site is where WordPress fits well, because your team can edit it without a deploy. The app itself is built by your engineering team, and that is not what I build.

What I do build is the part in front of the login: homepage, feature pages, pricing, sign-up and demo flows, a blog, help articles and campaign pages, as set out on my startup website development page. For paid campaigns that need their own focused pages and conversion tracking, the landing page design service covers that side.

Draw the line between site and app first

Most confusion in SaaS website projects comes from not deciding early what lives where. A common split:

Lives on the marketing site (WordPress)Lives in the app (your codebase)
Homepage, features, use cases, pricingSign-up form itself, if it creates an account directly
Blog, guides and help articlesLogin, password reset, dashboards
Changelog and roadmap notesBilling, plan changes, invoices
Demo booking and contact sales formsOnboarding screens and in-app tours
Legal pages: privacy, terms, refund policyAccount settings and data export

The usual domain setup is the marketing site on yourdomain.com and the product on app.yourdomain.com, with Log in and Start trial buttons linking across. Keeping them apart means a broken plugin can never take the product down, and a product release never breaks the pricing page. Each can be hosted wherever suits it.

One grey area is the sign-up form. If your app creates the account, put the form inside the app and link to it. If you only collect interest before onboarding by hand, a WordPress form connected to your CRM is enough.

The pricing page carries more weight than you expect

Visitors who reach pricing are usually close to a decision, and they come with specific questions. A pricing page that answers them:

  • States plans in the currency your buyers use, with a note on whether GST or other taxes are included for Indian customers
  • Shows monthly and annual prices honestly, without hiding the monthly figure behind the annual discount
  • Names the limits that matter: seats, projects, records, storage, API calls, whatever your product meters
  • Says what happens at the end of a trial: does it downgrade, lock, or charge a card already on file?
  • Answers billing questions in a short FAQ block: refunds, invoices, switching plans, paying by bank transfer
  • Offers a "talk to sales" route for larger teams, without forcing everyone through a call

If you are unsure whether to show prices at all, the guide on showing prices on a website weighs both sides. For B2B tools with long sales cycles, a starting price with "from" wording is often a fair middle ground.

Keep the plan details editable. When the plan grid is a block your team can change in the WordPress editor, a price change on Monday does not need a developer on Tuesday.

Feature pages and use-case pages are different jobs

A feature page explains one capability: what it does, a screenshot or short clip, the settings that matter, and which plans include it. A use-case page starts from the buyer: "for agencies tracking client hours" or "for clinics sending appointment reminders", and shows the two or three features that solve that problem together.

Both rank for different searches. People who already know the category search for features ("bulk invoice export"); people who do not know your category yet search for their problem. A small product can launch with three or four feature pages and two use-case pages, then add more as sales calls show which questions keep coming up.

Screenshots that stay current

Product screenshots go out of date with every release. Crop them tightly around the part being explained, so a change elsewhere in the interface does not make the image wrong, and keep a list of which page uses which screenshot so updates are quick.

The hand-off from "Start trial" to the app

This is the step that leaks the most. Someone clicks a button on the site, lands on the app's sign-up screen, and the context is lost. A cleaner hand-off:

  1. Each pricing button links to the app's sign-up URL with the chosen plan in the link, so the app can pre-select it.
  2. Campaign parameters from ads and emails are carried across, so you can tell which channel produced the trial.
  3. The sign-up screen repeats the promise from the button: same plan name, same trial length, same "no card needed" line if that is true.
  4. Analytics records the click on the site and the completed sign-up in the app as two separate events, so you can see where people drop off.
  5. Demo requests go to a separate thank-you page that says who will reply and how, rather than a generic "thanks".

I set up the site side of this: buttons, link parameters, events and forms connected to tools such as HubSpot or Zoho. The app side needs a short note to your developers about which parameters to read. Connecting website forms to a CRM covers the forms part in more detail.

Docs and a help centre

Documentation sells. Buyers read setup guides before they sign up, to see whether the product does what they need and how much work it will take. Options for where docs live:

  • Inside the WordPress site, as a help section with categories and search. Simple to edit, and articles can rank in Google alongside the rest of the site.
  • A dedicated docs tool on a subdomain such as docs.yourdomain.com. Better for API references and versioned docs that engineers maintain.
  • A support platform's help centre, if you already use one for tickets, so articles and support replies share one place.

Whichever you pick, link to it from the main navigation, and make sure it is crawlable if you want the articles found in search.

A changelog that shows the product is alive

A dated list of what shipped tells prospects the product is maintained, gives existing users a reason to come back, and gives sales something to point at. Keep entries short: the date, what changed, who it helps, and a link to the help article if one exists. A WordPress post type with a simple template works well, and it can feed an email update or an in-app notice if your developers want to pull the latest entries.

Avoid publishing a detailed roadmap with dates you may not hit. "Recently shipped" is safer than "coming in March".

Comparison pages, written fairly

Buyers search for "X vs Y" and "alternatives to X". A comparison page can help them, but only if it is honest. Some ground rules I would keep to:

  • Compare on facts a reader can check, such as published features, pricing models and integrations, and date the page
  • Say where the other product is the better choice, and for whom
  • Avoid claims about another company's performance, security or customers that you cannot support
  • Review the page every few months, because other products change too

Comparative advertising can raise legal questions, so have someone with legal knowledge read these pages before they go live. A fair page also tends to be trusted more than one that lists twenty wins in a row.

Measuring what the site actually does

For a SaaS site, page views matter less than a short list of events: pricing page viewed, trial button clicked, demo form submitted, docs searched, and sign-up completed in the app. GA4 events explained walks through setting these up. Connect Search Console too, and look at the queries reaching your docs and blog; they show what people expected your product to do.

Product-led content

The articles that work best for software are close to the product: how to do a task your tool helps with, templates that people can use with or without signing up, and explainers on the problem your category solves. Group them around a few core topics, as described in topic clusters and pillar pages, and link each article to the feature or use-case page it relates to.

A launch checklist for the marketing site

  • Homepage with one primary call to action, either trial or demo, not both competing
  • Three to five feature pages and at least one use-case page
  • Pricing with a billing FAQ, or a clear reason prices are on request
  • Log in link that goes straight to the app
  • Privacy policy and terms, with wording from you or your lawyer
  • Analytics events and Search Console connected before launch
  • A help section, even if it starts with five articles

FAQs

Can WordPress run our SaaS product?

For the marketing site, yes. The product itself is normally built in your own codebase by your engineers. Keeping them separate is the safer arrangement for both.

Should the sign-up form be on the website or in the app?

If the app creates an account immediately, keep the form in the app and link to it with the plan pre-selected. A website form suits waitlists and sales-led demos.

Do SaaS FAQ pages help with SEO?

They can, when each answer is specific and the questions are ones buyers really ask. FAQ pages and SEO covers how to structure them without padding.

Where should product documentation live?

A help section on the WordPress site suits short how-to articles. API references maintained by engineers are often better in a dedicated docs tool on a subdomain.

Is it fine to publish comparison pages against other tools?

Yes, if they are factual, dated and fair, and you take legal advice on the wording. Pages that admit where another tool fits better are usually more credible.
