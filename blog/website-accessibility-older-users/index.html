Making Your Website Easy for Older Visitors: Text Size, Contrast, Tap Targets, Plain Language and Phone or WhatsApp Options

Make your website easy for older visitors: larger text, strong contrast, big tap targets, plain words, short forms, calm pages, and phone or WhatsApp.

Key takeaways

  • Use plain, familiar words, a short menu and short forms, and always offer a tap-to-call number and WhatsApp next to the form.

To make a website easy for older visitors, use larger body text with strong contrast, big buttons with space between them, plain familiar words, a short menu, and short forms with clear labels, and always offer a tap-to-call number and WhatsApp next to the form. Remove auto-playing videos, rotating sliders and pop-ups, and make sure the site respects the larger text size many older people set on their phones. Then watch an older relative use it, because that one test shows more than any checklist.

These changes matter most where older people are the main visitors or decision-makers: clinics and hospitals, temples and trusts, pharmacies, insurance and elder care. If your current site fails them, a website redesign lets you fix text, layout and forms at the template level instead of page by page. For clinics specifically, see websites for doctors and clinics.

This guide builds on website accessibility basics, which covers the standards and a quick audit. Here the focus is on what older visitors find hard, and what to do about it.

Why older visitors struggle with websites

Ageing brings gradual changes that many websites ignore:

  • Eyesight: small print becomes harder to read, low contrast becomes harder to see, and glare on a phone screen makes both worse.
  • Hands: tremors and stiffness make small links, tiny close buttons and precise swipes difficult.
  • Hearing: phone calls and videos without captions can be hard to follow.
  • Unfamiliar patterns: icons without labels, hidden menus and swipe-only galleries assume habits not everyone has.
  • Caution: many older people are, rightly, wary of scams, so an unclear page or a pushy pop-up makes them leave.

None of this means older visitors aren't capable online. It means a site that makes them work harder than necessary will lose them, and often lose the family members who help them decide.

Text that's easy to read

  • Size: around 18px for body text on phones and computers is a comfortable default; 16px is the minimum. Headings should be clearly larger.
  • Spacing: line height of about one and a half times the text size, and short paragraphs.
  • Weight and style: avoid thin and light font weights, long passages in italics, and whole sentences in capital letters.
  • Line length: on a computer, keep lines from stretching right across a wide screen.
  • Respect the phone's text size. Many older people increase the text size in their phone settings. Build with flexible text sizes so the site follows that setting, never disable pinch-to-zoom, and check that larger text doesn't overlap buttons or cut off menu items.
  • Regional languages: Hindi, Marathi, Telugu and other scripts usually need a slightly larger size than English text to read comfortably.

Contrast and colour

Use dark text on a light background for anything people need to read: timings, fees, addresses and instructions. WCAG's minimum contrast ratio for normal text is 4.5:1; for an older audience, aim comfortably above it rather than just passing. Avoid placing text directly on photographs, avoid pale grey for "less important" text, and never rely on colour alone, such as red for "closed", without a word to go with it.

Buttons, links and tap targets

  • Make buttons look like buttons: a solid shape with a clear text label, such as "Book appointment" or "Call the clinic".
  • Give them room. A large target with space around it is easier for shaky hands; WCAG 2.2 sets a minimum of 24 by 24 CSS pixels, and 44 to 48 pixels is far more comfortable.
  • Underline links in paragraphs so they don't depend on colour.
  • Avoid actions that only appear on hover, swipe-only galleries and tiny "x" buttons for closing things.
  • Label icons with words. A phone icon alone is less clear than a phone icon with "Call us".

Plain language and simple navigation

Use the words visitors would use themselves: "Timings", "Doctors", "How to reach", "Donate", "Book appointment", not "Solutions", "Engage" or "Connect". Keep the main menu short, keep it in the same place on every page, and use a visible "Menu" label next to the icon on phones. Breadcrumbs and a clear Home link help people who land on an inner page from Google or a WhatsApp forward find their way.

In the content itself, put the most-asked information near the top of each page, use one idea per paragraph, explain any medical, legal or technical term in plain words, and turn steps such as "how to book a puja" into a numbered list.

Phone and WhatsApp alongside forms

Many older visitors would rather talk than type, and many family members enquire on a parent's behalf. Give them every route:

  • Tap-to-call buttons that open the dialler, with the number also written out in full so people can dial it from a landline.
  • WhatsApp with a pre-filled first message, which also helps visitors with hearing loss who find calls difficult; see adding WhatsApp to your website.
  • The hours someone answers, so people don't call at lunch and give up.
  • A form for those who prefer it, with an option such as "I am booking for a family member".

Forms older visitors can finish

  1. Ask only for what you need to respond: name, phone number and a short message is often enough.
  2. Put labels above fields, and make fields large.
  3. Use the right keyboard, such as the number pad for phone numbers.
  4. Accept phone numbers with or without spaces and country codes, instead of rejecting them.
  5. Show errors next to the field in plain words, and keep everything already typed.
  6. Avoid puzzle CAPTCHAs and short time limits.
  7. Finish on a thank-you page that says what happens next and when.

For appointment booking, a short request form with a call back often suits older patients better than a full calendar; online appointment booking compares the options.

Calm pages with fewer surprises

  • No auto-playing video or sound; play video only when tapped.
  • No sliders that change while someone is reading. One clear image and message is easier to follow.
  • No pop-ups on arrival, and nothing that covers the page on a phone.
  • Keep chat bubbles and sticky bars small, so they don't cover buttons or text.
  • Respect the "reduce motion" setting on phones and computers for any animation.

For visitors with hearing loss, a phone call can be the hardest way to reach you, so always offer WhatsApp, email or a form as well; see websites for hearing aid centres.

Trust and reassurance

Show the details a cautious visitor checks before calling: a full address with a map, real photos of the place and people, names and qualifications where relevant, fees or a clear note on what affects them, and plain policies. Avoid countdown timers, "only two slots left" banners and anything that feels like pressure. The website trust signals checklist covers the rest.

Clinics, temples and NGOs: what to put first

  • Clinics and hospitals: doctor names and specialties, timings, how to book, how to reach, parking and lift or wheelchair access. The clinic website checklist lists the essentials.
  • Temples and trusts: darshan and aarti timings, festival dates, how to reach, and simple donation steps, ideally in the languages devotees read. Large readable text, clear menus and fast loading on basic phones are part of how I approach websites for temples, trusts and NGOs.
  • Elder care and home nursing: written as much for the adult children researching on a parent's behalf as for the parents themselves.

Test with real people

Ask an older relative, customer or patient to do three real tasks on their own phone, with their usual text size: find your timings, find how to reach you, and send an enquiry. Watch without helping, and note every pause, wrong tap and question. Fix those first, then repeat with someone else.

FAQs

What font size should a website use for older visitors?

Around 18px for body text is a comfortable default, with 16px as the minimum, and headings clearly larger. Just as important, the site should grow with the text size people set on their phones without breaking the layout.

Should I add buttons to make the text bigger?

They rarely get used, and phones and browsers already have text-size and zoom settings. A comfortable default size and a layout that respects those settings help more than a resize button.

Is designing for older visitors the same as accessibility?

It overlaps heavily. Accessibility standards cover contrast, tap targets, labels and motion, which all help older visitors. Designing for older people adds plain language, phone and WhatsApp options, and extra attention to trust.

How can I test my website with older users?

Ask two or three older people to complete real tasks on their own phones while you watch without helping. Note where they hesitate or tap the wrong thing, fix those points, and test again.
