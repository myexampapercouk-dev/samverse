Digital PR for Small Businesses: Earning Press Mentions and Links the Right Way

Digital PR for small businesses: earn mentions and links from local news, trade publications and podcasts with real stories, your own data and expert comment.

Key takeaways
  • Digital PR earns coverage by giving journalists genuine stories, your own accurate data and useful expert comment.
  • Pitch local news, trade publications and podcasts with short, personal emails, and never demand a link.
  • Avoid paid link packages, undisclosed paid articles, made-up surveys and pay-to-enter award lists.

Digital PR means getting your business mentioned, and ideally linked, by news websites, trade publications, blogs and podcasts because you have something worth covering. For a small business that might be a local news story, a quote in an industry magazine or a guest spot on a podcast. Done well, it builds credibility, brings referral visitors and earns the kind of links that help SEO. This guide focuses on working with journalists, editors and hosts; for the wider picture, see ethical link building for small businesses.

What digital PR is, and what it isn't

Digital PR is about being useful to people who publish: giving them a story, a data point or an expert comment their audience will value. Coverage is earned, so nothing is guaranteed.

It isn't paying for "news features" that are really adverts, buying "guaranteed backlinks from news sites", or sending the same press release to hundreds of inboxes. Google's spam policies treat links bought to manipulate rankings as link spam, and paid placements are meant to be marked as sponsored or nofollow. A paid feature can still be fine for exposure, as long as it's labelled and you don't expect it to lift your rankings.

Find the stories you genuinely have

Most small businesses have more newsworthy material than they think. Look for:

  • Local firsts and changes: a service no one else in the area offers, a new branch that creates jobs, a move into exports
  • Community work: free health camps, workshops for students, sponsoring a local team, help during floods or heatwaves
  • People and craft: a third-generation family business, an unusual skill, a team that grew from a home setup
  • Timely angles: monsoon home repairs, exam-season stress, festival demand, new rules affecting your customers
  • Customer stories, shared only with their permission

A simple test: would someone who doesn't know your business find this interesting or useful? If it's only a sales pitch, it isn't a story.

Use data you actually have

Journalists like numbers, and your business may already hold some: the busiest months for repairs, the most-booked services by area, the questions customers ask most, or how orders change around festivals. Turned into a short, honest report, data like this can earn coverage that a sales message never would.

  • Use only aggregated, anonymised figures, and check that using data this way fits your privacy policy and India's DPDP Act
  • State the time period, the sample and how you collected it
  • Don't stretch findings: "most of our customers in Pune" is not "most Indians"
  • Publish the full findings on your own website, so coverage has something to link to

Offer expert comment

Reporters often need a practitioner to explain something quickly: a dentist on children's teeth during exam season, an AC technician on preparing for summer, a CA on an approaching filing deadline. Being that reliable source is one of the easiest routes into coverage.

  • Choose two or three topics you can speak about with real authority
  • Reply quickly, give a short quote they can use as it is, and stay within your expertise
  • Check your profession's rules first; some professions, such as advocates and doctors, have restrictions on publicity
  • Watch for journalists asking for sources on social media and source-request platforms, and see how active they are in your sector

Named, credentialed comment also supports the experience and expertise Google looks for; see E-E-A-T explained.

Pitch local news, trade publications and podcasts

Start small and relevant rather than aiming for national headlines. Good targets include city news websites and newspaper supplements, regional language outlets, trade magazines and association newsletters, niche blogs, and podcasts or YouTube channels your customers follow.

A good pitch is short and personal:

  1. Read or listen to what they've published recently, and find the right person
  2. Write a subject line that states the story, not your company name
  3. Explain in a few sentences what happened and why their audience will care
  4. Say what you can provide: photos, data, a spokesperson or a customer willing to talk
  5. Follow up once, politely, and then let it go

Don't demand a link. If they cover you, it's reasonable to ask whether they can link to the page with your data, but many publications rarely link out, and a mention still has value. For podcasts, suggest a few specific topics and share the episode once it's live.

Get your website ready for coverage

Journalists and producers check you out before featuring you. Make that easy with a press or media page that includes:

  • A short company description and founder bio
  • High-resolution photos, your logo and a fact sheet (year founded, locations, services)
  • A media contact who actually responds
  • Past coverage, linked to the original articles

Only use "as featured in" logos for genuine editorial coverage. Your data pages, guides and tools are what give publishers a reason to link, so make sure they're accurate, fast and easy to read on a phone.

What to avoid

  • Link packages, "guaranteed" news placements and private blog networks
  • Paid articles presented as independent news without disclosure
  • Press release distribution sold as an SEO link-building service
  • Made-up surveys, inflated numbers or claims you can't support
  • Attaching your brand to tragedies or sensitive news for attention
  • Paying to appear in "awards" or "top 10" lists that anyone can buy into

If an agency promises a fixed number of news links a month, read SEO red flags first.

Measure it realistically

Results are uneven: months of quiet, then one piece of coverage that keeps sending visitors for years. Track new mentions with a free alert tool such as Google Alerts, referral traffic and enquiries in GA4, and brand searches and new links in Search Console, and ask new customers how they heard of you.
