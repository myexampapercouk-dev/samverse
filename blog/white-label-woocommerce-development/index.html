White-Label WooCommerce Development for Agencies: Who Does What

How white-label WooCommerce builds work for agencies: what your partner sets up, what your agency keeps, how to test checkout and how to support the store.

Sameer Gupta 10 October 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways
  • A white-label partner handles store setup, payments and shipping for the client's market, product imports and custom features, while your agency keeps the client, the design and the brand.
  • The client opens payment gateway accounts in its own name and confirms tax settings with its accountant; the partner connects and configures what they decide.
  • Test every checkout path in test mode before the client review, then package ongoing store support as a commerce tier in your care plans.

Selling an online store to a client is a bigger commitment than selling a brochure site. Someone has to set up payments, shipping, taxes, products and order emails, and make sure checkout works on day one. Many agencies hand that technical work to a white-label WooCommerce developer while keeping the client, the brand and the design in-house. For the model itself, see white-label WordPress development; this guide covers what changes when the project is a store.

What the development partner handles

The partner takes on the technical work that turns an approved design into a working shop:

  • Store setup: WooCommerce installed and configured, with currency, store address, guest and account checkout, stock options and order statuses set the way the client trades
  • Templates: shop, category, product, cart, checkout, account and order confirmation pages built to your design on desktop and mobile
  • Payments and shipping configured for the client's market (covered below)
  • Product imports from a spreadsheet, a supplier file or an old store
  • Custom features beyond what standard extensions do well
  • Order emails: branded for the client and sent through a reliable email service so they reach inboxes
  • Technical checks: speed, mobile layout, security basics and checkout testing before your team reviews anything

Payments and shipping for the client's market

Agencies with clients in the UK, US, Australia and India soon find that each market expects something different at checkout. A good partner asks where the client sells, who its customers are and how it ships before suggesting any plugins.

DecisionWhat varies by marketWho decides
Payment methodsCards are standard everywhere and many stores add digital wallets; Indian stores usually need UPI and often cash on deliveryThe client, with advice from the partner on what integrates cleanly
Payment gateway accountAvailability, verification steps, payout times and supported currencies differ by countryThe client opens the account in its own business name; the partner connects it
ShippingZones by postcode, pin code, state or country; flat, weight-based or free-over-a-threshold rates; couriers, local delivery and collectionThe client sets the rules; the partner builds them
TaxVAT, GST or sales tax, prices shown with or without tax, and invoice requirementsThe client's accountant; the partner configures what they specify

That last row matters. A developer can set up tax rates and invoices, but shouldn't decide what they are. Ask the client to confirm their tax treatment with an accountant before the build is signed off.

Product imports and custom features

Product data depends on the client, so it's where store projects often slip. Agree early:

  • Source: a spreadsheet, a supplier file, an export from another platform, or manual entry
  • Structure: categories, attributes such as size and colour, variations, and the fields each product needs
  • Images: who supplies them, at what size, and how files are named so they match the right products
  • A sample import first: a small batch on staging, checked by your team before the full catalogue goes in
  • Data clean-up: fixing duplicate SKUs, missing prices and inconsistent names is real work, so decide whether it's in scope

Custom features might include product configurators, wholesale pricing, booking slots, subscriptions or a link to the client's stock system. For each, the partner should say whether an established extension covers it or custom code is needed, since that affects cost, testing and future updates.

What your agency keeps

White-label works because the client's experience doesn't change. Your agency keeps:

  • The client relationship: every call, email and approval goes through you, and the partner never contacts your client unless you arrange it
  • Strategy and scope: what the store sells, to whom, and what goes into phase one
  • Design and brand: layouts, product page structure and the tone of order emails
  • Content and marketing: product copy, policies, launch campaigns and SEO, if you sell them
  • Pricing and contracts: what you charge and how you package support

Use the agency handoff checklist for the full package to send, and add the store items above: payment methods, shipping rules, tax instructions and the product data file.

Testing checkout before handover

A store can look finished and still fail at the one step that matters. Before the client sees it, the partner should test checkout end to end in the gateway's test or sandbox mode, and your team should repeat the key paths on your own devices:

  1. Place orders with every payment method, including a declined card and a cancelled payment
  2. Check out as a guest and as a logged-in customer
  3. Use addresses in each shipping zone and confirm the right rates and delivery options appear
  4. Check tax and totals against what the client's accountant specified
  5. Apply a valid coupon, and try one that should be rejected
  6. Confirm customer and store emails arrive, look right and land in the inbox rather than spam
  7. Check that stock reduces after an order and that out-of-stock products behave as agreed
  8. Run the whole flow on a phone as well as a desktop
  9. Confirm purchase tracking fires once per order, if analytics or ad pixels are set up

After launch, a real order for a low-value product, refunded afterwards, confirms that live payment keys and payouts work. The WooCommerce store launch checklist covers the go-live steps in detail.

Ongoing store support

Stores need more care than brochure sites. WooCommerce, its extensions and payment plugins update often, and a bad update can quietly stop orders. A sensible support arrangement covers:

  • Updates tested on staging first, with checkout retested after each round
  • Frequent backups that include the order database, not just the files
  • Uptime monitoring, with alerts going to both your team and the partner
  • Licence renewals for paid extensions, so they keep receiving updates
  • Small changes such as new products, banners and sale settings, within an agreed allowance
  • An agreed emergency path for payment failures and outages

You can package this as a commerce tier in your white-label care plans, with requests still coming to your agency and the partner working behind your brand.

Starting a white-label store project

For a first store with a new partner, choose a client with a manageable catalogue and standard payment and shipping needs. Ask the partner to confirm the plugin list, payment and shipping setup and exclusions before you quote, so your price reflects the real work. Keep gateway, hosting and licence accounts in the client's name, and give the partner its own logins.
