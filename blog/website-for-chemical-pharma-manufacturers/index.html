Websites for Chemical and Pharma Manufacturers: Catalogues by CAS Number and Grade, TDS and SDS Downloads, Certifications You Hold, Export Enquiries and RFQ Forms

Plan a B2B chemical or pharma manufacturer website: CAS and grade catalogues, TDS and SDS downloads, real certifications, export and RFQ forms.

Key takeaways
  • Build the catalogue around how buyers search: product name, synonyms, CAS number and grade, with specifications in a table on each product page.
  • Decide which documents are free downloads and which are sent on request, and list only certifications and registrations you hold, with scope and validity.
  • Use an RFQ form that asks for grade, quantity, packing, destination and documents required, so your sales team can quote without a round of emails.

Buyers of chemicals, APIs, intermediates and excipients use a supplier's website to run a first round of due diligence before anyone picks up the phone. They check whether you make the exact grade they need, whether the documents exist, whether your quality systems will pass their audit, and whether you can ship to them. A good site lets them answer all four in a few minutes and then send a quote request your team can act on.

The core of the build is the same as the websites I build for manufacturers and B2B suppliers: a company profile, specification-led product listings, a quote button on each product, brochure and datasheet downloads, certifications and industries-served pages. Chemical and pharma sites add a layer of technical detail and regulatory caution that general industrial sites do not need, and the way buyers search, often by CAS number, shapes the whole structure. Getting that structure right is also the foundation of technical SEO for WordPress, because each product and grade needs a page Google can find.

The sections below follow the order in which a procurement or regulatory team usually evaluates a new supplier.

Step one for the buyer: do you make it, in the grade we need?

A buyer arrives with a product name, a synonym or a CAS registry number, and often a pharmacopoeia grade. The product page has to confirm the match immediately. Plan each page with these fields:

FieldExample of what to showNotes
Product name and synonymsThe name you sell under, plus common alternative names and abbreviationsBuyers and their systems use different names for the same molecule
CAS numberShown near the top, as text rather than in an imageSo it can be searched on your site and found by Google
Molecular formula and weightAs plain textHelps a technical buyer confirm the match
Grades offeredTechnical, industrial, food, or pharmacopoeia grades such as IP, BP, USP or EP, only where you manufacture to themEach grade can carry its own specification table
Key specificationsAssay, appearance, moisture, impurity limits, particle size where relevantTypical values, with a note that the COA for each batch governs
PackingBags, fibre drums, HDPE drums, IBCs, with sizesBuyers often need to know this before they can quote internally
ApplicationsIndustries and uses, described factuallyAvoid therapeutic or performance claims you cannot support
DocumentsTDS, SDS, COA, and any others you provideMark which are downloads and which are on request

Products that differ only by grade or particle size usually sit best on one page with a table per grade. Products with different uses or buyers deserve separate pages. Planning an industrial product catalogue covers that decision in more detail.

Search by CAS number, name or synonym

A catalogue of a few hundred products needs a search box that matches CAS numbers exactly, ignores hyphen and spacing differences, and also finds synonyms. A filter by product category, such as solvents, intermediates or APIs, and by industry served helps buyers who are browsing rather than searching. Keep the product data in a spreadsheet you maintain, so it can be imported and updated in bulk.

Step two: does the paperwork exist?

Technical and safety documents are often the real reason a buyer visits. Decide document by document how each is handled:

  • Technical data sheet (TDS): usually a free download. It answers a buyer's first technical question, and it can be found through search.
  • Safety data sheet (SDS or MSDS): many manufacturers offer it freely, because it is needed for safe handling and transport. Keep the revision date visible and replace old versions rather than adding new files beside them.
  • Certificate of analysis (COA): batch-specific, so a sample COA can be shown, with real ones supplied with each shipment or on request.
  • Regulatory and quality documents: such as declarations, stability data or drug master file details, normally shared only after a confidentiality agreement, through a request form.

Give PDFs descriptive file names and titles that include the product name and CAS number, so they are useful when someone finds them out of context. SEO for PDFs and brochures explains how to make document downloads work for search without losing the lead.

Step three: will your quality systems pass our audit?

For pharma buyers in particular, certifications and site approvals are a filter. The rule is simple: list only what you hold, and state it precisely.

If you hold itHow to show itWhat to avoid
ISO certification, such as ISO 9001 or ISO 14001The standard, the certifying body, the scope and the validity date, with the certificate availableShowing a logo without a scope, or a certificate that has lapsed
GMP compliance under Indian rulesWhich site and which products the licence or certificate covers, as issued by the licensing authorityApplying one site's approval to every product you sell
WHO-GMP certificationThe site and product scope as stated on the certificate, and its dateCalling the company "WHO approved", which suggests something different
Overseas inspections or registrationsOnly what is on public record or in documents you can share, worded as the regulator words itImplying approval for a market or product the inspection did not cover

Certificates expire and scopes change, so put a review date beside each one and assign someone to check them. A quality page that describes your QC laboratory, the instruments you use, your documentation practices and how you handle audits gives an auditor a reason to visit rather than a reason to rule you out.

Step four: can you supply us, and ship to us?

Export buyers want different details from domestic ones. A section for international enquiries should cover:

  • The countries and regions you already export to, stated generally if client names are confidential
  • Incoterms you usually work with and the ports you ship from
  • Packing and labelling suitable for export, and how dangerous goods are handled where relevant
  • Sample policy: whether samples are free, what quantity, and who pays freight
  • Documentation you supply with exports
  • Time zones and the languages your team works in

If a large share of enquiries comes from abroad, the site may need content in more than one language; WordPress can handle that with each language on its own pages. Websites for export businesses covers international buyers more broadly.

Step five: the request for quotation

A general contact form produces vague enquiries that take three emails to qualify. An RFQ form on every product page, pre-filled with the product name, should ask for:

  1. Company name, country and the buyer's role
  2. Product and grade, with CAS number
  3. Quantity, and whether this is a trial order or a regular requirement
  4. Packing preference
  5. Delivery destination and Incoterm if known
  6. Documents required with the quote, such as COA, SDS or regulatory documents
  7. Any specification file they want to attach

The Vansh Group website, built for an electronics manufacturer, uses a similar idea in a simpler form: its enquiry form has a service dropdown so every request arrives already tagged with the type of work, and its company profile is a download a buyer can share internally. For a chemical business, the same thinking means routing pharma enquiries, industrial enquiries and export enquiries to the right person automatically. Regulatory content: useful, careful and dated

Pages that explain regulatory topics, such as the documents a buyer typically needs to register a new source, or how a change in a pharmacopoeia monograph affects a grade, can attract exactly the right visitors. They also carry risk if they go out of date or overstate your position. Keep them factual, date them, link to the official source, and have your regulatory team review them. Drug and chemical rules in India and abroad change, and enforcement differs by product and market, so describe them in general terms and suggest buyers confirm the current position with the relevant authority.

Avoid health or therapeutic claims about products on a B2B site, especially APIs. Describe what a product is and what it is used for in manufacturing, not what it does for patients.

Trust signals that a B2B buyer recognises

  • Plant addresses, the products made at each site and photographs of the actual facility
  • Registered company name, CIN and GST number in the footer
  • Named contacts for sales, export and quality, with business email addresses on your domain
  • Industries served, with client names only where you have permission to use them
  • Safety and environmental practices described concretely, such as effluent treatment and handling procedures

FAQs

Should every chemical have its own page?

Every distinct product should, with grades and sizes in a table on that page. Separate pages for each product and CAS number make them findable by search and easier for buyers to share.

Should we put SDS and TDS behind a form?

Many manufacturers keep TDS and SDS freely downloadable because they help buyers decide and can be found by search. Keep the form for documents that need a confidentiality agreement or a qualified request.

Can we show WHO-GMP or other certification logos?

Only if you hold the certification, and only for the site and products it covers. Show the scope and validity, and follow the certifying body's rules on use of its name or mark.

Can the website show stock or live prices?

It can, but most chemical and pharma manufacturers quote on request because prices depend on grade, quantity, packing and destination. A catalogue with an RFQ form suits most of them better than a cart.

Can we update product specifications ourselves?

Yes. Products and specification tables can be edited from the WordPress dashboard, and a large catalogue can be updated in bulk from a spreadsheet.

If buyers currently email you for basic specifications and documents before they will quote, a structured catalogue can answer those questions on the page and send you better RFQs. The manufacturer websites page sets out what these B2B projects include.
