How to Recover Google Rankings After Your Website Was Hacked: Manual Actions, Spam URLs and a Realistic Timeline

Recover Google rankings after a hack: security issues vs manual actions, review requests, clearing spam URLs, fixing SEO damage and a realistic timeline.

Key takeaways
  • Clean the site fully and close the entry point before asking Google for a review, or the hack and warning return.
  • Spam URLs should return 404 or 410 so they drop out of the index; redirecting them to your homepage slows recovery.
  • Track recovery in Search Console by branded searches, indexed page counts and clicks on your real pages, not by a fixed date.

Rankings usually come back after a hack once four things are true: the site is genuinely clean, Google's warnings and any manual action have been lifted, the spam URLs have dropped out of the index, and your real pages, titles and settings are back to how they were. How long that takes depends on how long the hack was live, how many spam URLs were indexed and whether a manual action was involved, so nobody can honestly promise a date. What you can control is doing each step properly, in the right order.

If the site hasn't been cleaned yet, do that first: recovery can't start while spam or redirects are still live. Security issues and manual actions are different

Search Console has two reports under Security & Manual Actions, and a hacked site can appear in either or both.

Security issuesManual actions
What it meansGoogle's systems detected hacked content, malware or phishingA Google reviewer found a breach of its spam policies
Typical labels after a hackHacked: content injection, code injection or URL injectionCloaking and/or sneaky redirects, sneaky mobile redirects, unnatural links from your site, pure spam
How to fixClean the site, then Request reviewClean the site, then submit a reconsideration request
EffectWarnings shown to searchers and in browsersPages or the whole site ranked lower or removed until fixed

A reconsideration request should explain that the site was hacked, what the attacker added, what you removed, how they got in and what you've changed to stop it happening again. Be specific and honest. If a request is rejected, Google usually gives examples of what is still wrong; fix them and submit again. Reviews can take days or weeks.

Step 1: Confirm the site is truly clean

Requesting reviews before the cleanup is finished is one of the easiest ways to slow recovery down. Use the URL Inspection live test on a few spam URLs and your main pages, test from Google on a phone using mobile data, and check that no new spam pages appear over several days. The full process is in how to remove malware from WordPress step by step.

Step 2: Get warnings and manual actions lifted

Request a review for each security issue and submit a reconsideration request for each manual action. First remove any Search Console owner the attacker added, along with their verification method, or they can undo your work. A reconsideration request for a hack-related manual action might read like this:

Our site was hacked through an outdated form plugin. The attacker added cloaked spam pages under /shop-sale/ and code that redirected mobile visitors from Google. We have removed the code and a backdoor in the uploads folder, reinstalled WordPress and all plugins from official sources, and the spam URLs now return 410. We removed an unknown Search Console owner, changed all passwords, turned on two-factor login and now update weekly.

Step 3: Clear the spam URLs out of the index

  • Make every spam URL return 404 or 410, never a redirect to your homepage
  • Don't block them in robots.txt, or Google can't see they've gone
  • Remove spam sitemaps from Search Console and from the server
  • Use the Removals tool for spam showing on brand searches

Large spam hacks take the longest to clear; the Japanese keyword hack guide covers 410 rules and the Removals tool in detail.

Step 4: Undo SEO sabotage

Attackers, and sometimes rushed cleanups, change settings that quietly keep rankings down after the malware is gone. Check:

  • Settings > Reading: "Discourage search engines from indexing this site" must be unticked
  • robots.txt: no new Disallow lines for important folders and no Sitemap line pointing elsewhere; see robots.txt explained
  • Noindex tags on key pages, in the SEO plugin's settings for each page and for whole post types
  • Canonical tags pointing to another domain
  • Titles and meta descriptions rewritten with spam, often stored in the SEO plugin's data rather than in the page content
  • Redirects in .htaccess or a redirect plugin that you didn't create
  • Outbound links injected into posts, footers or widgets

The fastest check is URL Inspection on each key page: View crawled page shows the HTML Google actually received, including the title, robots meta tag and canonical.

Step 5: Restore lost content and URLs

Compare the pages Google used to send traffic to with what exists now. Search Console's Performance report, set to a date range before the hack, lists your top pages; an older sitemap or backup lists the rest. Restore anything deleted at its original URL, fix internal links that break, and if a page has to move, redirect the old URL to the new one.

When restoring moved or deleted pages, use the right redirect type. This guide on 301 vs 302 redirects explains which one passes signals permanently.

Step 6: Ask Google to recrawl

Resubmit your genuine sitemap and use Request indexing in URL Inspection for your most important pages. There is a daily limit on manual requests, so spend them on pages that matter. Good internal links from your homepage and menus help Google find the rest.

What about disavowing links?

Usually you don't need to. Spam hacks attract spam links, but they mostly point at the spam pages, and once those return 404 or 410 the links lead nowhere. Google says its systems ignore most spammy links anyway. Disavowing is worth considering only if you have a manual action for unnatural links to your site, or clear evidence of a link scheme you can't get removed. Backlink audits and the disavow tool explains when it really matters.

A realistic timeline

Every site is different, so treat these as stages rather than a schedule:

StageWhat decides how long it takes
CleanupSize of the infection, number of sites in the account, access available
Security reviewGoogle's guidance has said malware and phishing reviews often take days, and spam-hack reviews can take weeks
Reconsideration requestGoogle's review queue; days or weeks
Spam URLs dropping outHow many were indexed and how often Google recrawls them; large hacks take longest
Rankings settlingUsually follows the stages above, sometimes with ups and downs on the way

Things that slow recovery: a hack that ran for months, a reinfection after a review (Google may make you wait longer before the next request), lost content, and other changes made at the same time, such as a redesign or new URLs.

How to tell it's working

  • In the Performance report, clicks and impressions for your key pages and brand searches climb back towards the weeks before the hack
  • In the Pages report, spam URLs move to Not found (404) while your real pages stay indexed
  • site: searches show fewer spam results each week

If rankings stay down long after everything is clean, look for other causes in the same period, such as a Google update or a competitor improving; what to check when traffic drops walks through them.

A recovery checklist to keep beside Search Console

Recovery has many small steps and it is easy to skip one. Work through this list and note the date you finish each item, so you can match later changes in Search Console to the work you did:

  1. Malware removed, entry point found and closed, all passwords and keys changed.
  2. Security issues report shows no issues, and any manual action review request has been answered.
  3. Spam URLs return 404 or 410, not a redirect to the homepage.
  4. Your sitemap lists only real pages and has been resubmitted.
  5. Robots.txt, the site language, canonical tags and the "discourage search engines" setting are correct.
  6. Titles and meta descriptions on key service pages are your own again, not the hacker's.
  7. Google Business Profile still points to the correct website address.
  8. Key pages requested for indexing with the URL Inspection tool.

For a business in India that depends on local searches, check your branded search first: search your business name with your city and see whether your real homepage and contact page show with the right title. That usually recovers before broader service keywords do, and it is the clearest early sign that Google trusts the site again.

Keep a simple log of these dates. If rankings stay low weeks after everything is ticked, the log helps you see whether the problem is still the hack or something unrelated, such as an algorithm update or a competitor.

FAQs

How long does it take to recover rankings after a hack?

It varies. Small infections cleaned quickly can recover soon after Google recrawls, while large spam hacks or manual actions take longer. Nobody can honestly promise a timeline, but a thorough first cleanup is the biggest factor you control.

Does Google penalise a site for being hacked?

Google generally treats hacked sites as victims rather than spammers, but it still warns searchers and may apply a manual action if spam or cloaking breaks its policies. Once the site is clean and reviewed, those are lifted.

Do I need to disavow spam links after a hack?

Rarely. Most spam links point at pages that no longer exist, and Google ignores most spammy links. Consider it only for a manual action about unnatural links to your site.

Should I move to a new domain after a hack?

No. A new domain starts without your existing reputation, and a clean, reviewed site recovers on its own domain. Moving doesn't fix the vulnerability either.

Why do spam pages still show for my business name?

Either Google hasn't recrawled them yet, or they still return content to Googlebot because the cloaking code is active. Check with the URL Inspection live test, then use the Removals tool to hide them while Google catches up.

Should I redirect hacked spam URLs to my homepage?

No. Let spam URLs return a 404 or 410 so Google drops them. Redirecting thousands of junk URLs to your homepage can look like soft 404s and slows the clean-up.
