How to Choose Website Colours and Fonts for Your Brand: Palettes, Contrast, Font Pairing and Global Styles

How to choose website colours and fonts: build a palette from your logo, check contrast, pair readable fonts, support Hindi and set them as global styles.

Key takeaways
  • Build a small palette by role, with a primary, an accent for buttons, and neutrals for text and backgrounds, starting from your logo rather than colour psychology.
  • Check every text and button colour against WCAG contrast ratios; a brand colour that fails with white text needs a darker shade or dark text instead.
  • Use one or two font families with only the weights you need, body text of at least 16 pixels, and set colours and fonts once as global styles.

To choose website colours, start from your logo and build a small palette by role: one primary brand colour, one accent for buttons and highlights, and neutrals for text, backgrounds and borders. Check that every text and button colour has enough contrast to read easily. For fonts, use one or two families, a body size of at least 16 pixels and only the weights you need, make sure they support any Indian scripts you publish in, then set both as global styles so the whole site stays consistent.

Colours and fonts are worth settling before a website redesign starts, because every page inherits them, and changing them halfway through means rechecking every layout. If a designer has already set up colour and text styles in Figma, those map directly onto WordPress global styles, one of the first steps in my Figma to WordPress builds. Below is a practical way to choose them yourself, or to judge what a designer proposes.

Start from your brand, not colour psychology

You'll find plenty of charts claiming blue means trust and orange means energy. Colour associations are loose and cultural; in India, saffron, red and green carry religious, festive and political meanings that vary by region and audience. What matters more is that your website matches the brand customers already know, from your logo, signboard, packaging or vehicles, and suits your sector.

  • Clinics and hospitals usually suit calm, clean palettes with plenty of white space, so information feels clear rather than clinical.
  • Restaurants and food brands can use warmer, richer colours, but the food photos should be the most colourful thing on the page.
  • Manufacturers and B2B firms benefit from restrained colours that let specifications and product photos lead.
  • Law firms and consultants tend towards sober, high-contrast palettes that read as professional.

If you don't have a logo yet, sort that first; logo, favicon and brand basics covers the files a website needs.

Build a small palette by role

A website needs fewer colours than most people think. Give each one a job:

RoleUsed forTips
PrimaryYour brand colour: heading accents, links, key shapesUsually taken from the logo; you may need a darker shade for text
AccentButtons and calls to actionMust stand out from the primary and pass contrast with its button text
TextBody copy and headingsNear-black or a very dark brand tint, not mid-grey
BackgroundsPage and section backgroundsWhite or off-white, plus one light tint for alternating sections
Borders and muted textDividers, form fields, captionsLight greys that still keep form fields visible
Status coloursError, success and warning messagesNever rely on colour alone; add an icon or words

A common rule of thumb is to keep neutrals dominant, the primary colour secondary and the accent rare, so the accent draws the eye to the button that matters. If every element is in your brand colour, nothing stands out.

Turning one brand colour into a usable palette

  1. Take the exact hex code from your logo file, not from a screenshot.
  2. Create a darker shade for text and hover states, and a very light tint for backgrounds.
  3. Pick an accent that contrasts with the primary, often from the opposite side of the colour wheel or a brighter neighbour.
  4. Test each combination for contrast before anything is designed around it.

Check contrast before you fall in love with a palette

Low contrast is the most common colour mistake on small business sites: light grey text on white, white text on yellow or orange buttons, or text over busy photos. WCAG, the international accessibility guidelines, set minimum contrast ratios at level AA:

  • 4.5 to 1 for normal body text.
  • 3 to 1 for large text, roughly 24 pixels and above, or about 19 pixels if bold.
  • 3 to 1 for button outlines, form field borders, icons and other parts people need to see to use the page.

Use a free contrast checker such as WebAIM's, or the contrast reading in your browser's developer tools. A typical case: a bright orange that looks great in the logo might measure around 3 to 1 against white, so white button text fails for normal-sized text. Either darken the orange for buttons or use dark text on it. Then test on your own phone outdoors, at reduced screen brightness, because that's how many customers will see it. Website accessibility basics covers the other checks, and designing for older visitors explains why contrast and size matter even more for them.

Choosing fonts that read well

One family or two

One well-designed family with a few weights can carry a whole site. If you want more character, pair a distinctive heading font with a plain, readable body font. More than two families looks messy and slows the page.

ApproachFeelsExamples on Google Fonts at the time of writing
One sans-serif for everythingClean, modern, safeInter, DM Sans, Lato, Open Sans
Serif headings, sans-serif bodyEstablished, editorial, premiumPlayfair Display or Merriweather with Source Sans 3
Geometric headings, plain bodyFriendly, confidentPoppins or Montserrat with Inter or Lato
System fontsNeutral, fastest to loadThe phone or computer's built-in fonts, with no download

Readable sizes and spacing

  • Body text of at least 16 pixels on phones; many sites read better at 17 or 18.
  • Line height around 1.5 to 1.7 for paragraphs, tighter for large headings.
  • Lines of roughly 60 to 75 characters on desktop, so the eye doesn't get lost.
  • A clear size step between headings, subheadings and body text.
  • Two or three weights, such as regular, semi-bold and bold. Every extra weight is another file to download.

Hindi, Marathi and other Indian scripts

If you publish in Hindi or Marathi, the font must include Devanagari, and its Latin and Devanagari letters should look balanced together. Families such as Mukta, Hind and Noto Sans Devanagari are designed for this; test real sentences, including conjuncts and matras, not just the alphabet. Hindi and Devanagari fonts on your website covers sizes, line height and loading.

Licences

Fonts on Google Fonts are free to use under open licences. A font bought for print may need a separate web licence, so check before your designer builds the site around it.

Keep fonts from slowing your site

Every font family and weight is a download. Load only the weights you use, prefer the WOFF2 format, host the files on your own server where possible, and make sure text shows in a fallback font while the web font loads, so nothing stays invisible. How to load web fonts without slowing your site covers font-display, preloading and avoiding layout shift in detail.

Set them once as global styles

However you build, colours and fonts belong in one central place, so a change updates every page. In Elementor, that's Site Settings > Global Colors and Global Fonts. In a block theme, it's the Styles panel in the Site Editor, backed by the theme's theme.json file:

{
  "version": 3,
  "settings": {
    "color": {
      "palette": [
        { "slug": "primary", "color": "#1F4E79", "name": "Primary" },
        { "slug": "accent", "color": "#E07A1F", "name": "Accent" },
        { "slug": "text", "color": "#1A1A1A", "name": "Text" },
        { "slug": "background", "color": "#FFFFFF", "name": "Background" }
      ]
    },
    "typography": {
      "fontFamilies": [
        { "slug": "body", "name": "Body", "fontFamily": "Inter, system-ui, sans-serif" }
      ]
    }
  }
}

In this example, the dark blue passes comfortably with white text, but the orange accent measures about 3 to 1 against white, so it suits large text and highlights, or buttons with dark text. Name colours by role, not by appearance, so "accent" still makes sense if it changes from orange to green. Designers preparing a Figma file can follow the same idea; see preparing a Figma file for a WordPress build.

Test before you commit

  • View a real page, not a palette swatch, on a budget Android phone and an iPhone.
  • Check it in sunlight and at low brightness.
  • Switch your screen to greyscale; headings, buttons and links should still be obvious.
  • Make sure links are distinguishable without colour, for example by underlining them in body text.
  • Ask someone outside the business whether the main button is the first thing they notice.

FAQs

How many colours should a website have?

Usually a primary brand colour, one accent for buttons, and a set of neutrals for text, backgrounds and borders, plus status colours for errors and success messages. More than that tends to look busy.

What font size should website body text be?

At least 16 pixels, with a line height of around 1.5. Many sites are more comfortable to read at 17 or 18 pixels, especially for older visitors and on small phones.

Should my website use the same colours as my logo?

Yes, as a starting point, but logo colours often need adjusting for screens. A light or bright logo colour may need a darker shade for text and buttons to pass contrast checks.

Are Google Fonts free for business websites?

Yes, Google Fonts are released under open licences that allow commercial use. Self-hosting them on your own server is usually faster than loading them from Google.

Can I change my website colours later?

If they're set as global styles, changing them is quick. If every heading and button was styled by hand, it means editing each one, which is a good reason to set up global styles from the start.
