Websites for Commercial Kitchen Equipment Suppliers: Spec-Led Catalogues, Turnkey Projects, Quotes and AMC

What a commercial kitchen equipment supplier website needs in India: specs by equipment type, turnkey project pages, precise brand wording, quotes and AMC.

Key takeaways
  • Organise the catalogue by equipment type and give every product its dimensions, fuel or power, capacity and material, because that is what chefs and consultants check first.
  • Sell turnkey kitchens as a process with project pages, and word brand authorisations, approvals and food safety advice exactly as far as they are true.
  • Split enquiries into a single-product quote, a full-kitchen project brief and a service or AMC request, so each lands with the right person and the right details.

A commercial kitchen equipment supplier's website has four jobs: show the range by equipment type with real specifications, prove you can deliver a complete kitchen, state exactly which brands you make, distribute or service, and capture quote and service requests with enough detail to act on. Restaurant owners, hotel purchase teams and kitchen consultants compare several suppliers before they call anyone, and the site that answers "will it fit, will it run on our gas or power, and who fixes it" wins the shortlist.

Your buyers are a mix: restaurant, cafe and cloud kitchen owners who want a working kitchen within a budget and deadline; hotel and hospital teams who need datasheets and dependable after-sales; consultants and architects who specify equipment; and canteens, hostels and industrial kitchens cooking in bulk. A catalogue site built the way I approach websites for manufacturers serves all of them, as long as each finds their route quickly.

Start with the four questions every buyer asks

  1. Do you make it, trade it, or both? Say plainly whether you fabricate stainless steel items in your own workshop, distribute other brands, or do both.
  2. Will it fit and run in my kitchen? Dimensions, fuel type and power load decide this, so they belong on every product page.
  3. Can you deliver the whole kitchen? Many buyers want one supplier for layout, equipment, fabrication and installation.
  4. What happens when it breaks? Service cities, AMC and spares are often what tips the decision.

Answer these on the homepage in short blocks, each linking to the deeper page. A restaurant owner is also handling interiors, licences and their own restaurant website, so a supplier site that can be scanned in a minute stands out.

Catalogue structure: by equipment type, with specifications

Organise categories the way kitchens are planned, not the way your stock register is laid out. Each category then gets its own page and each product its own URL. What every product page needs

  • Real photos of the item, ideally in your workshop or at an installed site
  • A specification table with dimensions, weight, material, capacity and fuel or power
  • Whether it is standard or can be made to size
  • A downloadable datasheet that consultants can attach to approvals
  • Two buttons: "Request a quote" and "Ask on WhatsApp"

For items you resell, copy specifications from the manufacturer's own datasheet rather than retyping from memory, and note that specifications may change with the model. A wrong burner rating or power load on your site becomes your problem at installation.

State brand authorisation precisely

Buyers check this, and so do the brands. Use wording that matches your actual agreement:

Your real positionWording that fits
You manufacture under your own name"Manufactured in our workshop in (city)"
You have a written dealer or distributor agreement"Authorised dealer for (brand) in (region)", only for the region and products it covers
You buy from a distributor and resell"We supply (brand) equipment", without the word authorised
You repair a brand but have no agreement"We service (type of equipment), including models from (brands)", not "authorised service centre"

Use a brand's logo only if your agreement allows it, and keep the list current when agreements lapse.

Turnkey kitchen projects as a process

Many buyers do not want a list of machines; they want a kitchen that works on opening day. If you take on complete projects, give it a dedicated page that walks through your steps:

  1. Site visit, or review of the architect's drawing
  2. Understanding the menu, meals or covers per day and service style
  3. Layout proposal showing the flow from receiving and storage to prep, cooking, washing and service
  4. Equipment schedule and coordination points for gas, electrical, plumbing and exhaust
  5. Fabrication, delivery, installation and commissioning
  6. Staff demonstration and handover documents

Hedge the layout and FSSAI advice

Kitchen layout touches food safety, fire safety and gas installation, and the requirements vary by city, type of establishment and the current rules. FSSAI and local authorities set the standards; you do not grant licences. Write that you design layouts with hygiene and workflow in mind, separate raw and cooked areas where the space allows, and work alongside the client's consultant or licensing advisor. Avoid phrases like "FSSAI approved kitchen" or "guaranteed licence", and suggest clients confirm current requirements with the authority or a consultant.

Project pages, shared with permission

Completed kitchens are your strongest proof. Give each a page grouped by type: hotels, restaurants and QSRs, cloud kitchens, canteens, hospitals. Describe the scope, the main equipment supplied and fabricated, and what made it tricky, such as a basement kitchen or a tight opening date. Name the client and show their logo only with written permission; otherwise describe it generically, such as "a multi-cuisine restaurant in Pune". Photograph kitchens cleanly before service starts, without staff in frame unless they agree. The approach in writing case studies for a business website works well here.

Three enquiry routes instead of one form

RouteWho uses itFields worth asking
Product quoteOwner replacing one itemProduct, quantity, fuel available, delivery city
Kitchen project briefNew outlet, expansion, consultantEstablishment type, covers or meals per day, cuisine, new or replacement, site city, opening date, optional drawing upload
Service or AMC requestExisting customerEquipment type, model, serial number if known, problem, photo, site address

Keep most fields optional so busy owners still submit, and route each form to the right person by email and WhatsApp. The guide to RFQ forms for B2B websites covers follow-up and file uploads. A WhatsApp button suits owners who prefer to send photos of the space.

Installation, AMC and spares

After-sales support often wins repeat business and referrals, so it deserves a proper section of its own.

  • Installation: what you handle and what the client arranges, such as gas pipeline work by a licensed agency, electrical points and drainage
  • AMC: what a contract covers, for example preventive visits, burner and condenser cleaning, and how breakdown calls are handled, without promising response times you cannot keep
  • Warranty: terms as given by you or the brand, including what is not covered
  • Spares: burners, knobs, gaskets, thermostats and similar parts listed by product, with an enquiry button
  • Service areas: the cities your technicians actually cover

Local search, trust and speed

Buyers search for terms like "commercial kitchen equipment manufacturer in Pune", "SS kitchen fabrication in Bengaluru" or "hotel kitchen equipment supplier". Build pages for your main categories, and for cities only where you have real projects or service coverage there; the local SEO guide for Indian businesses explains how. Keep your Google Business Profile filled with workshop, showroom and installation photos.

Trust comes from specifics: factory and team photos, GST and company details, certifications you actually hold and a clear address. Catalogues get heavy with photos and PDFs, so compress images and keep datasheets light for buyers checking on a phone at a site.

Launch checklist

  • Categories by equipment type, each product with a spec table and datasheet
  • Fuel or power stated on every relevant product
  • Brand wording checked against each agreement
  • Turnkey process page and at least three project pages, with permissions recorded
  • Three enquiry forms, each tested end to end
  • AMC, warranty, spares and service-area pages
  • Compliance wording reviewed so nothing promises approvals

FAQs

Should I publish prices for commercial kitchen equipment?

For standard items, a "starting from" price can filter enquiries, but steel prices and specifications change, so many suppliers prefer "price on request". If you show prices, date them and say whether GST and delivery are included. Both sides are weighed up in should you show prices on your website.

Can I list brands I do not have an agreement with?

You can say you supply or service equipment from those brands if that is true, but avoid "authorised" and avoid their logos unless an agreement allows it.

Can the website say our kitchens meet FSSAI requirements?

Be careful. Requirements depend on the establishment and the current rules, and the licence is the client's. Say you design with hygiene and workflow in mind and coordinate with the client's consultant.

Do I need a page for every product?

For products people search or ask about, yes. Very similar sizes of the same item can share a page with a size table.

How should I handle custom fabrication on the site?

Show standard sizes with specs, add a clear "made to size" note, and offer a drawing upload in the project form.
