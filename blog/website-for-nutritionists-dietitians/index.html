Websites for Nutritionists and Dietitians: Consultation Formats, Programmes, Credentials Stated Precisely, Booking and Payments, and Content Clients Trust

What a nutritionist or dietitian website needs: online and clinic consultations, programmes, precise credentials, booking, payments and careful claims.

Key takeaways
  • Describe each consultation format, online, in clinic or by phone, with its length, what happens in it and what the client receives afterwards.
  • State your qualifications exactly as awarded, name the body behind any credential, and keep every result claim free of guarantees.
  • Use recipes and articles to show how you work, and publish before and after photos only with written consent and a note that results vary.

A nutritionist or dietitian website should do three things well: explain how a consultation works, show exactly what you are qualified to do, and let a client book and pay without a phone call. Everything else, from recipes to programme pages, supports those three, and none of it should promise a result you cannot control.

Much of the build overlaps with the clinic websites I design for doctors: a profile with qualifications, a page for each service, a form for appointment requests, buttons to call or message you, and local SEO for the city where your clinic sits. The difference is that many of your clients will never visit the clinic. Some find you through Instagram, some through a referral from a gynaecologist or physician, and a growing share book an online consultation from another city, so the site has to work as your front desk for both.

When you run ads for one programme, such as a PCOS plan or a diabetes plan, send that traffic to a landing page built for that programme alone instead of the homepage.

Start with the consultation, because that is what people buy first

Most visitors are not ready to sign up for a three-month plan. They want to know what the first conversation involves. A page titled "How consultations work" answers that, and it should cover each format you offer separately:

FormatSuitsWhat the page should say
In-clinic consultationLocal clients, older clients, anyone who wants body measurements taken in personAddress, parking, clinic days, length of the session, whether a family member can join, and what to bring, such as recent blood reports
Online video consultationClients in other cities or abroad, working professionals, new mothersWhich video tool you use, how the link arrives, time zones you can accommodate, and how reports are shared before the call
Phone or chat follow-upExisting clients between full sessionsHow often check-ins happen, response times you can realistically keep, and what counts as a follow-up rather than a new consultation
Corporate or group sessionsEmployers, gyms, housing societiesSession formats, group sizes and a separate enquiry form

For each format, describe what the client receives afterwards. A written diet plan, a food diary template, a shopping list, a follow-up schedule: whatever you actually provide, name it. That single detail answers the question many people are too polite to ask, which is what they are paying for.

Programmes: one page for each, written around the client's situation

A list of "weight management, diabetes, PCOS, thyroid, sports nutrition" tells visitors you cover a lot without telling them how. Give each programme its own page, and build it from the client's side:

  • Who it is for: for example, women recently diagnosed with PCOS who want to understand food choices alongside their doctor's treatment
  • What you work on together: meal timing, portion habits, cooking at home with Indian staples, eating out, festival weeks
  • How it runs: number of consultations, check-in frequency, plan revisions and how long the programme lasts
  • What you need from them: recent reports, current medicines, a few days of food records
  • Where your role ends: that you work alongside their doctor, and do not adjust medicines

Clients with medical conditions often arrive through a doctor's referral, so say plainly that you are happy to coordinate with their treating doctor if they wish. Doctors who refer patients will read that line too.

Qualifications: write them exactly as awarded

Nutrition is a field where titles are used loosely, and clients who have been burned before look carefully. Precision is the best trust signal you have. On your profile page:

  1. List each degree or diploma with its full name, the institution and the year, for example a BSc in Home Science with a nutrition specialisation, or an MSc in Food Science and Nutrition.
  2. If you hold a credential such as Registered Dietitian, name the body that awarded it. At the time of writing, the RD credential in India is awarded by the Indian Dietetic Association after its own examination; check the current position and show your registration number if you have one.
  3. Keep certificates from short online courses separate from degrees, under a heading such as "Further training".
  4. Describe hospital or clinical experience factually: the department and the kind of patients, without naming patients.

The rules on who may call themselves a dietitian or nutritionist in India are less settled than in some countries, and a national framework for allied and healthcare professions is still being put in place. Use only the titles you are entitled to, avoid "Dr." unless you hold a doctorate and make clear what it is in, and check the current guidance from your professional body before launch.

The Dr. Sudhir Arora website is a useful reference here, though it is a wellness education site rather than a nutrition one. It gives the degree, the college, the year and the registration number, and it states just as clearly what the programme is not: not psychiatric care and not a substitute for medical treatment. Saying where your work stops reassures people more than a long list of conditions.

Booking, payment and the intake form

For a solo practitioner, the booking flow is often the difference between a busy week and a quiet one. A workable setup has four parts:

  • A calendar with real slots, clinic and online shown separately, so nobody books an in-person session on a day you are not there. Online appointment booking for small businesses compares the options.
  • Payment at the time of booking for online consultations, by UPI, card or net banking through a gateway such as Razorpay. Accepting online payments on WordPress explains the setup and fees.
  • A short intake form sent after booking, not before: age, goals, current medicines, food preferences such as Jain, vegetarian or eggetarian, and any allergies.
  • A clear cancellation and rescheduling policy on the booking page itself.

The intake form collects health information, which is sensitive personal data. Ask only what you need for the first session, explain why you ask, keep blood reports out of open web forms, and have them shared through a channel you control. The DPDP Act and your website sets out the basics of consent and storage.

Should you publish fees?

Many clients will not enquire without some idea of cost. A complete price list is optional, but showing what a first consultation costs, or what a programme includes and how payment is split, filters out people who were never going to book. If prices differ for online and clinic sessions, say so.

Recipes and articles that show how you think

Content is where a nutrition practice can stand out, because it shows your approach before the first call. Useful topics tend to come straight from your consultations:

  • Swaps clients actually make, such as a lighter version of a regional breakfast, with the recipe and the reason
  • How to eat at a wedding or during Navratri without abandoning the plan
  • Reading the label on a packaged snack, with a real example you photographed
  • Tiffin ideas for office days and school lunchboxes
  • What the numbers on a common blood report mean in plain language, and why the doctor still interprets them

Format recipes properly, with ingredients, quantities, steps and servings, so they are easy to follow on a phone in the kitchen. Avoid calorie counts or nutrient values you have not worked out properly, and do not present any food as a cure. A recipe that ends with "book a consultation to adapt this to your needs" converts better than one that ends with a hard sell.

Results, testimonials and before and after photos

This is where nutrition websites most often go wrong. A few rules keep you on safe ground:

AvoidWrite instead
Lose 10 kg in 30 days, guaranteedA description of how the programme works and what clients commit to
Reverse diabetes, cure PCOS, fix thyroidFood habits that can support the treatment your doctor prescribes
A wall of before and after photosA few stories shared with written consent, each with a note that results vary
Detox, flush, burn fat fastPlain descriptions of meals, timing and habits

Before and after photos need the client's signed permission, stating the pages and channels where each image may be used, and clients should be able to withdraw it later. Crop out faces if the client prefers, never use stock images presented as clients, and do not edit photos to exaggerate a change. Health and weight-loss claims are an area where advertising standards and consumer protection rules apply, so check current guidance if you plan to advertise. Collecting and displaying testimonials covers consent forms and presentation.

Local search, referrals and the rest of the site

For in-clinic work, a properly categorised Google Business Profile, with correct opening times and photos of your actual consultation room does much of the local work. On the website, a dedicated page for each clinic location, with its own address, days and map, helps people searching for a dietitian in their area find the right one. If you consult at a hospital or a gym on certain days, list those days and places too.

For online work, your city matters less and your specialisation matters more. Pages about specific situations, such as nutrition during pregnancy or for young athletes, bring in people searching for exactly that. If you work closely with fitness professionals, websites for gyms and fitness studios shows how they present their programmes, which can help you plan joint offers.

A launch checklist for your practice website

  • Profile with degrees, institutions, years and any registration number
  • A "How consultations work" page covering every format you offer
  • One page for each programme, written around who it is for
  • Booking calendar, payment at booking for online sessions, and a cancellation policy
  • An intake form that asks only what you need, sent after booking
  • Recipes and articles drawn from real consultations
  • Testimonials and photos backed by written consent, with "results vary" stated
  • WhatsApp and call buttons on every page, and a privacy policy that mentions health data

FAQs

Can I offer online diet consultations through my website?

Yes. A booking calendar with online slots, payment at the time of booking and an automatic email with the video link covers most of it. Make the time zone clear if you see clients abroad.

Can I say I help with diabetes or PCOS?

You can describe how you support clients with those conditions through food and habits, alongside their doctor's care. Avoid any wording that suggests you cure or reverse a condition.

Should my website show my RD number?

If you hold a registration, show the number and the body that issued it, because it lets clients check. If you do not, describe your qualifications precisely instead and leave the title out.

Can clients upload blood reports on the booking form?

I advise against collecting reports through an open form. Ask for them after booking, through email or the secure channel you already use with clients, and store them carefully.

Are before and after photos allowed?

Only with the client's written consent, without editing that exaggerates the change, and with a clear note that individual results vary. Check current advertising guidance if you use them in ads.
