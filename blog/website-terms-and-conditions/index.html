Website Terms and Conditions in India: What to Include for Service Sites and Online Stores

Website terms and conditions explained for Indian businesses: what to include, how they differ from privacy and refund policies, and where to place them.

Key takeaways
  • Terms and conditions set the rules for using your site and buying from you; they sit alongside, not instead of, a privacy policy and a refund or returns policy.
  • Online stores on WooCommerce or Shopify need clearer terms than brochure sites, covering pricing, payment, delivery, cancellations and returns.
  • Templates are a starting point only; have a lawyer review the final wording for your business, because this guide is not legal advice.

Website terms and conditions are the rules for using your site and, if you sell online, for buying from you. They tell visitors what they can and cannot do, what you are and are not responsible for, how orders and payments work, and how disputes will be handled. For a simple service website they can be short. For an online store on WooCommerce or Shopify they need to be clear and specific, because customers and payment gateways will rely on them.

Important: this article explains the usual structure and practical points from a website-building view. It is not legal advice. Laws and rules change, and every business is different, so ask a lawyer to review your final terms.

Terms and conditions vs privacy policy vs refund policy

These three pages are often confused. Each does a different job:

PageWhat it coversWho needs it
Terms and conditionsRules for using the site, orders, payments, liability, disputesMost business sites; essential for stores
Privacy policyWhat personal data you collect, why, how it is stored and sharedAny site with forms, analytics or cookies
Refund or returns policyWhen customers can cancel, return or get money backOnline stores and paid bookings
Shipping policyDelivery areas, timelines, charges, COD rulesStores shipping physical products

For the privacy side, see privacy policy and cookie basics for India. For refunds, the guide to a WooCommerce returns and refunds policy goes deeper.

What website terms and conditions usually include

Most terms follow a similar outline. Not every section applies to every business, and your lawyer may add or remove parts.

  1. Who you are: business name, registered address and contact details.
  2. Acceptance: by using the site or placing an order, the visitor agrees to the terms.
  3. Use of the site: what is not allowed, such as misuse, scraping, or posting unlawful content.
  4. Intellectual property: who owns the content, images and logos on the site.
  5. Products, services and pricing: how prices are shown, whether tax is included, and how errors are handled.
  6. Orders and payment: when an order is confirmed, accepted payment methods, and what happens if payment fails.
  7. Delivery or service timelines: usually by reference to a separate shipping policy.
  8. Cancellations and refunds: usually by reference to a separate refund policy.
  9. Limitation of liability: the limits of what you are responsible for.
  10. Third-party links and services: payment gateways, courier tracking, embedded tools.
  11. Governing law and disputes: which law applies and where disputes are handled.
  12. Changes to the terms: how and when you may update them, with a last-updated date.

Terms for online stores on WooCommerce and Shopify

Stores need more detail than brochure sites because money changes hands. Customers want to know exactly what they are agreeing to, and payment gateways commonly ask to see terms, privacy, refund and contact pages before activating a merchant account. A store that launches without them can face delays.

Points that often need attention in Indian stores:

  • Prices and GST: state whether prices include GST and how invoices are issued. The guide to GST invoices on Shopify covers the store side.
  • Cash on delivery: if you offer COD, explain any limits, extra charges or verification steps. See cash on delivery on Shopify in India.
  • Order cancellation: when a customer can cancel, and when you may cancel an order, for example if stock runs out or a price is listed in error.
  • Stock and product images: a note that colours on screen may vary slightly is common for fashion and furniture.
  • Delivery: serviceable pin codes, typical timelines and what happens with failed deliveries.

Shopify offers policy templates in its settings, and WooCommerce stores can add policy pages like any other page. Either way, edit the template so it reflects how your business actually works, then have it reviewed.

Terms for service businesses and bookings

Consultants, clinics, coaching institutes and agencies often take enquiries, bookings or advance payments online. Their terms usually focus on:

  • How bookings are confirmed and what deposit, if any, is needed.
  • Rescheduling and cancellation windows.
  • What happens if the business has to cancel.
  • Disclaimers, for example that website content is general information and not professional advice.

Doctors, lawyers and financial advisers may be subject to professional rules on what they can say and promise online. Check those with your professional body or lawyer before publishing.

Where to place your terms on the website

  • Footer link on every page, next to privacy policy and contact.
  • Checkout: a link near the place order button, and if your lawyer recommends it, a checkbox to confirm acceptance.
  • Booking and sign-up forms: a short line linking to the terms.
  • Order confirmation emails: a link to the policies that applied at the time.

On WordPress and WooCommerce, policy pages are ordinary pages, so they can be linked in menus, footers and checkout settings. On Shopify, policies added in the settings appear automatically in the checkout footer, and you can also link them from the store footer menu. Test the links on mobile after every theme change, because footers are often rebuilt.

Keep the page readable: headings, short paragraphs and plain language. Terms that customers can actually understand reduce disputes and support tickets.

Common mistakes with website terms

  • Copying another company's terms, including their name, address or policies that do not fit your business.
  • Promising refund or delivery times you cannot keep.
  • Terms that contradict what the checkout or product pages say.
  • No last-updated date, so nobody knows which version applied.
  • Policies hidden or missing, which can hold up payment gateway approval.

Terms for digital products, courses and subscriptions

Businesses selling online courses, downloadable templates, memberships or subscriptions face questions that physical stores do not. Customers want to know how long they keep access, whether they can share a login, what happens to their access if they ask for a refund, and how recurring payments can be stopped. Terms for these products often cover:

  • Access period: lifetime, a fixed number of months, or while the subscription is active.
  • Licence: whether the buyer can use templates or material for clients, or only for personal use.
  • Account sharing: whether one purchase covers one person or a team.
  • Recurring billing: how renewals work, how to cancel, and when cancellation takes effect.
  • Refunds for digital goods: many sellers limit refunds once content is downloaded or accessed, but the rule should be stated clearly and fairly.

Recurring payments through Indian gateways may involve extra rules on mandates and customer notifications, so check current requirements with your gateway and your lawyer.

Making terms readable for customers

Legal accuracy matters, but so does readability. Long blocks of capital letters and dense clauses put customers off and lead to more support questions. A few practical steps help without changing the legal meaning:

  • Add a short plain-language summary at the top, with a note that the full terms below apply.
  • Use numbered headings so customers and support staff can point to a specific clause.
  • Link to the refund, shipping and privacy pages instead of repeating them.
  • Offer the terms in Hindi or a regional language if most of your customers prefer it, keeping one version as the reference.

Ask your lawyer to approve any summary so it does not contradict the full terms.

Keeping terms up to date

Review your terms whenever something changes: a new payment method, a new courier, international shipping, subscriptions, or a change in law. Note the update date at the top. Keep a copy of older versions in case a dispute concerns an earlier order. If someone else maintains your website, make policy updates part of the regular maintenance checklist.

FAQs

Do I need terms and conditions on my website in India?

For an online store or any site taking payments, clear terms are strongly recommended and payment gateways commonly ask for them. For simple service sites they are still good practice. A lawyer can tell you what your business specifically needs.

What should website terms and conditions include?

Usually: business details, acceptance of terms, site use rules, intellectual property, pricing and payment, delivery and cancellations, limitation of liability, governing law and how changes are made.

Can I use a free terms and conditions template?

As a starting point, yes. Edit it to match your real processes and have a lawyer review it. A template copied without changes often promises things you do not do.

Does a Shopify store need its own terms and conditions?

Yes. Shopify provides templates, but you should adapt them to your products, shipping, COD and refund rules, and get them reviewed before launch.

Is this article legal advice?

No. It explains common structure and practical website points. Please consult a qualified lawyer for terms that fit your business.
