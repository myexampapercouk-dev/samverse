Website Terms and Conditions: What They Cover and Why Templates Need Care

What website terms and conditions usually cover for business sites and online stores, how they differ from policies, and why copied templates need care.

Key takeaways
  • Terms of use set the rules for your website, while terms of sale cover orders, payments, delivery and cancellations for stores and bookings.
  • Copied templates often cite the wrong laws, contradict your other policies or include one-sided clauses that may not hold up.
  • Prepare the facts of how your business works, have a lawyer draft or review the wording, and make terms easy to find and accept at checkout.

Most business websites have a "Terms and Conditions" link in the footer, and many of those pages were copied from somewhere else years ago. That's risky. Good terms explain how your website and services work, set expectations with customers and support your other policies. This guide explains what terms pages usually cover and why templates need care. It's general information, not legal advice; for terms you'll rely on, work with a lawyer.

Terms, policies and disclaimers: what's the difference?

Websites often have several legal pages, and each does a different job:

PageWhat it usually covers
Terms of useThe rules for using your website: acceptable use, content ownership, disclaimers and liability
Terms and conditions of saleThe agreement when someone buys or books: orders, pricing, payment, delivery and cancellations
Privacy policyWhat personal data you collect, why, and how you protect it
Returns and refunds policyHow returns, exchanges and refunds work
Shipping or delivery policyDelivery areas, timelines and charges

Smaller sites often combine terms of use and terms of sale on one page. Privacy has its own rules and deserves its own page; see privacy policy and cookie consent basics.

Does your website need terms?

  • Brochure or service website: a short terms of use page is usually enough, covering content ownership, accuracy of information and links to other sites
  • Online store: terms of sale are essential, alongside returns and shipping policies
  • Bookings, courses and subscriptions: you need clear terms on deposits, rescheduling, cancellations, renewals and refunds
  • User accounts, reviews or listings: you need rules for what users can post and what happens if they break them

Payment gateways commonly check for terms, privacy, refund and contact pages when reviewing a website for activation, so having them ready before launch can save delays.

What a terms of use page usually covers

  • Who you are: your legal business name, address and contact details
  • Acceptance: that using the site means agreeing to the terms
  • Intellectual property: that your text, photos, logos and designs belong to you (or are licensed) and can't be copied without permission
  • Acceptable use: no spam, scraping, hacking attempts or misuse of forms
  • Accuracy and disclaimers: that information is general and may change, which matters for doctors, consultants and financial services
  • Third-party links and tools: that you're not responsible for other websites
  • Limitation of liability: limits on what you're responsible for, drafted carefully and within the law
  • Governing law and jurisdiction: often Indian law and the courts of your city
  • Changes: how you update the terms, with a "last updated" date

Extra terms for online stores and bookings

If you sell or take bookings, customers need to know how the deal works before they pay:

  • How orders are confirmed, and what happens if an item is out of stock or wrongly priced
  • Prices, taxes such as GST, and delivery charges
  • Accepted payment methods: UPI, cards, net banking, wallets or cash on delivery
  • Delivery areas and timelines
  • Cancellations, whether by the customer or by you
  • Returns, refunds and warranties, with a link to your full policy
  • For bookings: deposits, rescheduling, late arrivals and no-shows
  • For subscriptions: renewal dates, how to cancel and whether partial refunds apply
  • Contact and grievance details

India's Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020 require online sellers to display certain information clearly, including return, refund, exchange, warranty, delivery and grievance redressal details. Check the current rules or ask a lawyer about what applies to you. For the returns side, see returns and refunds for WooCommerce stores.

Why copied templates cause problems

Free generators and copied terms are tempting, but they often create more risk than they remove:

  • Wrong country: references to foreign laws, courts or regulators that don't apply to an Indian business
  • Wrong business: clauses about software licences on a bakery website, or nothing about deliveries on an online store
  • Contradictions: terms that promise a 30-day return window while your returns page says 7 days
  • Clauses that may not hold up: India's consumer protection law allows certain one-sided terms to be challenged as unfair, so "no refunds under any circumstances" may not protect you the way you expect
  • Copyright: copying another company's terms word for word can raise copyright issues of its own
  • Out of date: terms written years ago that don't reflect how you sell today

A template can be a useful checklist of topics. The actual wording should reflect your business and be reviewed by a lawyer.

Making terms easy to find and accept

  • Link your terms and policies in the footer of every page
  • Show a clear "I agree to the terms and conditions" checkbox at checkout or on booking forms; WooCommerce has a built-in setting for this once you assign a terms page
  • Summarise key points near the buy button, such as delivery time and return window
  • Write in plain language with headings, so customers can actually read it
  • Add a "last updated" date and keep old versions for your records
  • Link the terms in order confirmation emails where relevant

Terms that customers actively accept are generally easier to rely on than a link buried in the footer, but whether a particular clause is enforceable is a question for your lawyer. The WooCommerce store launch checklist shows where these pages fit before you go live.

Working with a lawyer and your developer

You'll save time and fees by preparing the facts before a lawyer drafts or reviews your terms:

  1. What you sell, to whom, and where you deliver
  2. Your payment, cancellation, delivery and returns processes as they actually work
  3. Any accounts, reviews, uploads or user content on the site
  4. The third-party services you rely on, such as payment gateways, booking systems and couriers

Your developer then builds the pages, adds checkout or booking consent and makes sure everything is linked consistently. Review your terms whenever you add a new service, payment method or sales channel.
