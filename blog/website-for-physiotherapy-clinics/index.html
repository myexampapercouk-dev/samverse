Websites for Physiotherapy Clinics: Condition Pages, Assessment Booking, Home Visits, Sports Physio, Packages and Honest Recovery Wording

Plan a physiotherapy clinic website: pages for each condition, assessment booking, home visits, sports physio, fair packages and safe exercise videos.

Key takeaways
  • Build the menu around the pain or injury people search for, and give each condition a page that explains the assessment, the treatment and when to see a doctor instead.
  • Describe the first assessment, home visits and session packages in practical detail, and let the therapist recommend the number of sessions after assessing the patient.
  • State therapist qualifications precisely, check current council guidance on titles, and talk about recovery as goals and progress rather than promises.

Most people who search for a physiotherapist are in pain, recovering from surgery, or worried that an injury will stop them working or playing. A physiotherapy clinic's website earns the booking by meeting them at their problem: a page for their condition, a clear account of the first assessment, practical details on home visits and packages, and therapists whose qualifications are stated exactly.

The underlying build is the same as for the clinic websites I make for doctors and clinics: treatment pages, practitioner profiles, a booking enquiry form, tap-to-call and WhatsApp buttons, opening hours, maps and local SEO. If you run ads for a specific problem, such as knee pain or post-surgery rehabilitation, a separate landing page for that campaign usually converts better than sending clicks to the homepage.

Build the menu around pain, not techniques

Patients rarely search for "manual therapy" or "IFT". They type "lower back pain treatment near me", "frozen shoulder physiotherapy" or "physio after knee replacement". The menu should therefore start from the body and the problem, grouped so that people can find themselves quickly:

  • Neck and back: lower back pain, sciatica, cervical pain, posture problems from desk work
  • Shoulder, elbow and hand: frozen shoulder, rotator cuff problems, tennis elbow, wrist pain
  • Hip, knee and ankle: knee osteoarthritis, ligament injuries, ankle sprains, plantar heel pain
  • After surgery: knee and hip replacement, ligament reconstruction, fracture rehabilitation
  • Neurological: stroke rehabilitation, Parkinson's disease, balance problems
  • Women's health, children and older adults: antenatal and postnatal care, developmental delays, falls prevention

Include only conditions your team genuinely treats. When I built the Dr. Sudhir Arora site, the menu started from the visitor's problem, with a page for each concern the programmes address, and that same principle suits a physiotherapy clinic: a patient should recognise their own problem in the menu within a few seconds.

What goes on a condition page

  1. The condition in two or three plain sentences, as a patient would describe it
  2. How physiotherapy may help, and how it fits with a doctor's treatment
  3. What the assessment looks at for this problem
  4. The kinds of treatment you may use, from exercise and manual therapy to electrotherapy, without implying every patient gets all of them
  5. What the patient will be asked to do at home
  6. Signs that need a doctor first, such as numbness spreading down the legs, loss of bladder control, or pain after a fall in an older person, written and approved by your senior therapist
  7. A booking button and links to related conditions

The first assessment, step by step

People in pain worry that the first session will hurt or be a waste of money. A page explaining what happens takes that worry away:

  • How long the first visit takes, and that it includes assessment as well as some treatment if appropriate
  • Questions the therapist will ask about the pain, work, sleep and activities
  • Movement and strength tests, explained simply, with a note that the therapist stops if anything is too painful
  • What to wear, such as shorts for knee problems or a loose top for shoulders
  • What to bring: X-ray or MRI reports, the surgeon's discharge notes and any prescription
  • What the patient leaves with: an explanation of the problem, goals, a plan and the first home exercises

Clinic sessions or home visits?

Home physiotherapy is a large part of the work for many Indian clinics, especially after surgery and for older patients. Patients choose between the two on practical grounds, so compare them honestly:

QuestionAt the clinicAt home
Who it suitsPatients who can travel, sports injuries, most back and neck problemsPatients soon after surgery, after a stroke, or who find travel hard
EquipmentFull range of exercise and electrotherapy equipmentPortable equipment the therapist carries, plus exercises using the home
SchedulingFixed clinic slotsVisits planned around the therapist's route, with an arrival window
What the website should stateTimings, parking, lift or ramp accessLocalities covered, any travel charge, and what the family should prepare

On the home visit page, list localities by name rather than "all over the city", say whether patients can request a male or female therapist, and explain what helps, such as a firm bed or a clear space on the floor. For patients who also need nursing or caregivers, home nursing and elder care websites covers that neighbouring service.

Sports physiotherapy deserves its own section

Runners, cricketers, footballers, dancers and gym members search differently and expect different things. Their pages should cover injury assessment, return-to-sport testing, strength and conditioning, taping, and advice on training loads. Say which sports your therapists know well, whether you work with academies, clubs or schools, and whether you cover tournaments or marathons, only if you do. Photos of real athletes training in your clinic, with permission, carry more weight here than anywhere else on the site. Academies looking for a physio partner check these pages too, and websites for sports academies shows what that side of the relationship looks for.

Packages without pressure

Session packages help patients budget, and clinics use them to encourage people to finish a course of treatment. They also cause disputes when terms are vague. For each package, state:

  • The number of sessions and how long each one lasts
  • Whether the first assessment is included
  • How long the package remains usable, and what happens to unused sessions if a patient recovers early or has to stop
  • Whether clinic and home sessions can be mixed
  • Whether sessions can be shared with a family member

Avoid selling large packages before anyone has been assessed. "Your physiotherapist will suggest how many sessions you are likely to need after the first assessment, and we review progress as we go" is honest and still encourages a booking. Whether to publish prices is your decision; even "from" prices for an assessment and a single session help people decide.

Therapist profiles and the question of titles

Give each therapist a profile with facts patients can check: their degree, such as BPT or MPT, with the university and year; the specialisation of their master's degree, such as musculoskeletal, neurological, sports or cardiopulmonary physiotherapy; registration with a state council where one applies to them; and any courses in particular techniques, naming the organisation that ran them.

Physiotherapy is one of the professions covered by the National Commission for Allied and Healthcare Professions Act, and the councils and registers under it have been taking shape at different speeds across states. Whether physiotherapists may use the prefix "Dr." has also been disputed, with official guidance changing. Check the current position with your state council or professional association before deciding how names appear on the site, and keep the wording consistent across the website, signboard and Google Business Profile.

Exercise videos that are safe to follow

Exercise videos are among the most useful content a clinic can publish, and they show your therapists' manner better than any profile. A few rules keep them safe and useful:

  • Film your own therapists in your own clinic, one exercise per video, with the common mistake shown alongside the correct movement
  • Open each video with who it is for, and who should not try it without advice
  • Add captions, and consider Hindi or regional versions if many of your patients prefer them
  • Host on YouTube and embed with a lightweight player so pages stay fast
  • End with an invitation to book an assessment if pain persists or worsens

Talking about recovery without promising it

Patients want to know how long recovery will take, and it is tempting to answer with "pain-free in five sessions". Recovery depends on the condition, how long it has been present, age, general health and how consistently the patient does their exercises, so a promise is likely to be broken. Wording like this is accurate and still encouraging:

Many people with this kind of shoulder problem notice their movement improving over the first few weeks of treatment, though everyone recovers at their own pace. After your assessment, your physiotherapist will set goals with you and review them at each stage.

Patient stories work well when they describe what changed for the person, such as climbing stairs again or returning to cricket, are shared with written consent, and do not suggest that the result is typical.

A page for doctors who refer patients

Orthopaedic surgeons, neurologists and general physicians send many patients to physiotherapists. A short page for them explains how to refer, which conditions and post-operative protocols you handle, and how you report progress back. Mention hospital tie-ups or visiting arrangements only where they are real and current.

FAQs

Do I need a doctor's referral to see a physiotherapist?

Many clinics accept patients directly, while some insurers ask for a doctor's prescription. Your website should state your own policy and tell patients to check their insurer's terms.

Should a physiotherapy website show session prices?

It helps to show at least a starting price for the assessment and a single session, with package terms explained. Exact pricing remains a business decision.

Can patients book home physiotherapy online?

Yes. A form can collect the address, locality, condition, preferred days and any therapist gender preference, and confirm the visit on WhatsApp.

Is it safe to publish exercise videos?

Yes, if each video says who it is for, who should seek advice first, and when to stop, and if it encourages an assessment for persistent pain.

Can a physiotherapist use "Dr." on the website?

Guidance on this has changed and is disputed, so check the current position with your state council or professional association before deciding, and use the same form of name everywhere.
