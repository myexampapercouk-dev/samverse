Websites for Immigration and Visa Consultants: Visa Categories, Assessment Booking, Document Checklists, Clear Fees and Fraud Warnings

Plan an immigration or visa consultant website: services by visa type and country, assessment booking, checklists, clear fees and no guarantees.

Key takeaways
  • Organise services by visa category and destination, and say plainly which decisions rest with the immigration authorities rather than with you.
  • Show a licence or registration only if your firm or adviser genuinely holds it, with the number and a link to the public register where one exists.
  • Split professional fees from government and third-party charges, publish a refund policy, and add a scam warning page that tells clients how you will and will not contact them.

An immigration or visa consultant's website has to persuade a cautious reader that you are real, competent and honest about what you cannot control. The sites that win consultations set out each visa route clearly, describe the assessment as a defined service, separate every fee, and never promise an approval that only a foreign government can give.

The build itself follows the pattern of the websites I make for lawyers, CAs and consultants: service pages, adviser profiles with checkable credentials, a consultation request form, one-tap call and WhatsApp, and an articles section for updates. What sets this sector apart is how much the wording matters: one "100% visa success" banner can undo every other trust signal on the site.

If you advertise one route, such as visitor visas for parents, point those ads at a campaign landing page about that route alone, ending in an assessment request.

Sort services by visa category first, then by country

Many consultancies put a row of flags on the homepage and stop there. Clients think in terms of purpose: visit family, take a job, join a spouse, settle or study. Lead with the purpose, then list the countries you handle for it:

CategoryTypical clientWhat you can doWhat stays with the authorities
Visitor and tourist visasParents visiting children abroad, families on holidayExplain the requirements, prepare and review the file, book appointmentsThe decision, and any request for more documents
Work permits and employer-sponsored visasProfessionals with a job offer, or employers hiring from IndiaCheck the offer and documents against the route, prepare the applicationApproval of the employer, the role and the visa
Skilled migration and permanent residenceGraduates and experienced professionalsAssess the profile against published criteria, plan steps such as tests and credential checksInvitations, scores and final selection
Spouse, partner and family visasMarried couples and dependantsExplain the evidence that is usually asked for and organise itWhether the relationship evidence is accepted
Refusals and reapplicationsApplicants who have been refused beforeRead the refusal letter and advise whether and how to reapplyThe outcome of the fresh application

Each category then gets a page per country you handle, covering eligibility in general terms, the stages, common documents and a link to the official government page. Study visas sit better on their own section or site, because students and parents ask different questions; websites for overseas education consultants covers that work.

Registrations and licences: show what you hold, and nothing else

Clients are often told to check whether an adviser is licensed, so give them something to check. At the time of writing, several popular destinations, including Canada, Australia, New Zealand and the UK, regulate who may give paid advice on their immigration systems and keep a public register of licensed or registered advisers. If someone in your firm holds such a licence, show their name, the licence or registration number, the body that issued it and a link to that register, on the adviser's profile and in the footer of the relevant country pages.

If nobody in the firm holds one, do not borrow the language. Describe what you actually do, such as document preparation, and check whether each destination allows that work without a licence, because the rules differ and have tightened in places.

Two India-specific points are easy to miss:

  • If you place people in jobs abroad, India's emigration law requires recruiting agents to be registered with the Protector General of Emigrants, and the Ministry of External Affairs lists registered agents on its eMigrate portal. Check the current rules with the ministry, describe your work as recruitment only if you hold that registration, and show the certificate number. Websites for recruitment agencies covers the employer and candidate side of that business.
  • Do not use national emblems, embassy logos or flags arranged to suggest an official connection. A consultancy is a private business, and the site should read like one.

The assessment is your product, so describe it properly

With most consultancies the first step is an assessment, and the website's main job is to book it. Answer these questions on the assessment page:

  • Is it free or paid, and if paid, is the fee adjusted against later work?
  • How long does it take, and is it in person, by phone or by video?
  • Who conducts it: a licensed adviser, a case officer or a counsellor?
  • What does the client receive afterwards: a verbal opinion, a written summary of options, or a quote for the work?
  • What should they have ready: passport validity, education and work history, test scores if any, and any previous refusals?

A pre-assessment form that asks enough

Ask for name, phone, email, the country and purpose they are interested in, their highest qualification, years of work experience, whether they have taken a language test, any previous refusal for any country, and their preferred consultation mode. Split it into two or three short steps so it does not feel like an application in itself; multi-step forms shows how. Leave out passport numbers and document uploads at this stage. Identity documents, bank statements and medical records are sensitive personal data under the DPDP Act, and they belong in a secure channel once you are engaged; the DPDP Act and your website explains the basics of consent and storage.

Document checklists people can actually follow

A good checklist is among the most useful things a consultancy can publish, and clients return to it while they gather papers. For each route you handle, make one page or downloadable sheet that:

  1. Groups documents by who issues them: the applicant, the employer or sponsor, Indian authorities, and the destination country
  2. Notes documents that take time to arrange in India, such as a police clearance certificate through Passport Seva, or attestation and apostille where the destination asks for them
  3. Explains format points that cause rejections, such as certified translations, photograph specifications and how recent bank statements must be
  4. Links to the official checklist and shows the date you last compared yours against it
  5. Ends with a line saying the authority may ask for more, and that your team will review the final file

When the official list changes, update yours promptly, keep the same address and change the date.

Fees in three columns

The commonest complaint about consultants is a fee that grew. Setting out every charge, and who receives it, prevents most of those disputes:

Your professional feeGovernment and third-party chargesOptional or situational costs
Assessment, file preparation, review, form filling, appointment booking and follow-up, with what each stage includesVisa application fees, biometrics, medical examinations by approved doctors, language tests and credential assessments, paid to those bodiesTranslations, attestation, courier, travel to appointment centres, and any premium or priority services the authority offers

Below the table, say when each instalment is due and what happens to fees if a client withdraws or an application is refused. Government fees are generally not refunded by the authority; say whether any part of your own fee is. Invoices should carry your registered name and GST number, and payments should go only to the firm's account.

Outcome language: what to write instead

Immigration advertising attracts complaints, and destination regulators take a dim view of guarantee claims by advisers they license. Most risky phrases have an honest replacement that still sells:

AvoidWrite instead
Guaranteed visa, or visa or refundWe prepare complete, accurate applications; the decision rests with the immigration authority
Visa in a fixed number of daysProcessing times are set by the authority and change; here is where to see the current figure
Approval rate shown as a percentageA description of the routes you handle most and how you assess suitability before taking a case
Contacts at the embassyNothing; no consultant can influence a decision, and implying otherwise is a warning sign
PR for everyone, no IELTS neededWho a route may suit, which tests it usually requires, and what the assessment will check

The same care applies to client stories. Share an approval only with the client's written permission, avoid suggesting it is typical, and never post visa stickers or passports, even blurred.

A scam warning page that protects clients and your name

Fake job offers and demands for "processing fees" are common enough that a warning page is a genuine service, and it protects you too, because fraudsters copy real firms' names and logos. Cover:

  • Common patterns: overseas job offers that require a fee before an interview, offers made only on WhatsApp or social media, pressure to pay the same day, and advice to submit fake experience letters or inflated bank balances, which can lead to refusals and bans
  • How you contact clients: your official domain, email addresses and phone numbers, listed in full, with a note that you never ask for payment to a personal account or UPI ID
  • How to check any adviser: the public registers for the destinations you serve, and the eMigrate lists for recruiting agents
  • What to do if targeted: keep the messages, do not pay, and report to the cybercrime helpline and portal

Pages for refusals and reapplications

Many people look for a consultant only after a refusal, often on a file they prepared themselves. Give them a page of their own. Explain that the refusal letter states the reasons, that resubmitting the same file rarely helps, and that some refusals can be challenged while others call for a stronger fresh application. Ask them to bring the letter to the assessment, and never suggest you can reverse a decision.

Country pages that stay accurate

Immigration rules, fees and processing times change several times a year. Put a "last reviewed" date and the reviewer's name on every country and visa page, link to the official source, and update the existing page instead of publishing a new one that leaves the old version live. A short news section on rule changes, in your own words and linked to the official announcement, shows that someone is watching. Clients who still need a language test can be referred to a coaching partner, and if you run classes yourself, a site for IELTS and spoken English classes needs its own structure.

FAQs

Can an immigration consultant's website say "guaranteed visa"?

It should not. Only the destination's immigration authority decides, and guarantee claims mislead clients and can breach the conduct rules of advisers' regulators. Describe what you do and how carefully you do it.

Should we show our licence number on the website?

Yes, if an adviser in your firm genuinely holds one. Show the adviser's name, the number, the issuing body and a link to its public register, and keep it current.

Should the website show our fees?

At least show how fees are structured, with your refund policy. Exact figures are a business choice; the structure should never be hidden.

Can clients upload passports and bank statements through the website?

Not through an open enquiry form. Collect contact details and a short profile first, then use a secure channel for documents once the client has engaged you.

Do we need a separate page for each country?

For each country and visa category you genuinely handle, yes. Each page answers different questions and searches, and should link to that country's official source.
